9:T563,XMerge has two components. The first is cross-axis selection. Each block is scored along two axes: relative magnitude, which measures the size of the residual displacement, and Block-Influence, which measures directional change between input and output hidden states. The two scores are standardized across eligible layers and combined with a maximum. A block is considered quiet only when neither axis is large. This max rule is intentional: an average could hide a large angular change with a small magnitude score, or the reverse. The second component is reconstructive layer merging. Instead of dropping a layer, analytically folding weights, or adding a learned replacement module, XMerge distills the original adjacent two-block mapping into one existing standard Transformer block. For calibration inputs, it caches the pair input h_l and the original pair output h_{l+2}, then optimizes all parameters of the surviving block so that the single block maps h_l directly to h_{l+2}. After k merges, the result is an ordinary L-k-layer Transformer with no extra inference-time parameters, no task labels, and no end-to-end fine-tuning. The closest contrast is with methods such as ReplaceMe, LLM-Streamline, and analytic merge/fold approaches; XMerge is distinctive because it reconstructs with a full existing nonlinear standard block while preserving the serving interface.a:T6a2,The method has three main ingredients. First, the authors approximate the Hessian with the empirical Fisher, using the standard assumption that a converged pretrained model is near a local optimum. They reshape the full gradient vector into a matrix G and show that the Fisher admits an exact decomposition as a sum of Kronecker products. After expressing the relevant expectation in terms of E[G⊗G], they take its SVD and recover a representation of the form J(w)=Σ_i σ_i U_i⊗V_i. The approximation comes from truncating this sum to the top-r components.

Second, they avoid explicitly constructing the enormous n^2 by m^2 matrix. Instead, they use an implicitly restarted Arnoldi solver that only needs matrix-vector products. The key identity is (G⊗G)vec(V)=vec(GVG^T), which turns the expensive operation into smaller dense multiplications on reshaped gradients. This is what makes the method scalable.

Third, they do not rely on the low-rank Kronecker term alone. They compute the Fisher diagonal exactly and combine it with the truncated decomposition. This hybrid design is sensible: the low-rank term captures correlations, while the exact diagonal preserves per-parameter scale. In practice, that should be more stable than either a pure diagonal approximation or a pure low-rank one.

Compared with K-FAC-style approximations, block-diagonal Hessians, or layerwise curvature proxies used in quantization, the distinguishing feature is the explicit attempt to preserve cross-layer interactions. The paper is not mainly about within-layer second-order structure. It is about whether off-diagonal coupling is strong enough to predict super-additive damage under joint perturbations.b:T868,The experiments span four LLM families from 350M to 7B parameters. Even without the full tables in the provided text, the main empirical findings are clear.

First, the paper reports direct evidence of substantial non-diagonal Fisher/Hessian structure in large language models. This matters because it validates, at realistic scale, the claim that diagonal approximations miss important information. Across multiple model families, value projection layers emerge as the most sensitive components and also show the strongest cross-layer correlations. Other module types behave less uniformly and appear more architecture-dependent.

Second, the proposed curvature scores correlate strongly with compression damage. The authors evaluate quantization and sparsification and show that layerwise Fisher/Hessian values track performance degradation well, better than cruder independent-layer approximations. In other words, layers with larger curvature are more likely to cause larger perplexity or task-performance drops when perturbed.

Third, they run inter-layer corruption experiments rather than only single-layer ablations. When two strongly coupled layers are compressed or corrupted together, the resulting degradation exceeds the sum of their individual effects. The off-diagonal curvature terms predict this excess damage. This is one of the most important results in the paper, because it shows that cross-layer structure is not just mathematically elegant; it explains real failure modes.

Fourth, they study post-corruption recovery. After damaging selected layers, they fine-tune different candidate layers and find that updating the layers most strongly coupled to the corrupted ones yields the best recovery. That is a practical result: the best repair target is not always the damaged layer itself, but sometimes the layer most tightly coupled to it.

The numbers worth remembering are the scale range, 350M to 7B, and the reduction from quadratic to linear memory complexity. The evaluation is also broader than a single compression setting: quantization, sparsification, joint corruption, and post-corruption fine-tuning are all included.c:T566,This is a skim paper, not one that requires line-by-line derivation. Second, inspect the figures that visualize non-diagonal structure and layer coupling. Those are the core evidence that the method goes beyond diagonal or block-diagonal approximations. Pay particular attention to whether the value projection finding is stable across model families.

Third, read the joint corruption and post-corruption fine-tuning sections carefully. Those experiments are where cross-layer terms prove their practical value. A simple correlation with single-layer quantization damage would not be enough, because many weaker proxies could also achieve that.

SandwichQuant is a two-stage framework that only updates Φ:
1) Pre stage (SandwichQuant-Pre): train Φ_pre on the dense model to precondition response distributions presented to PTQ calibration/reconstruction. Then transfer only Φ_pre back to the original dense checkpoint and rerun the chosen PTQ backend from scratch.
2) Post stage (SandwichQuant-Post): after PTQ produces a fixed low-bit graph, freeze the quantized model and learn an independent Φ_post to correct residual response mismatch.
The separation matters: Φ_pre influences calibration/clipping/reconstruction by reshaping activations, while Φ_post targets remaining functional error after the quantized graph is fixed. The method adds no new inference modules/operators—only reuses existing norm affine parameters.

The first is DVBP. The authors estimate the input activation covariance of each layer in an online manner, avoiding the need to store all activations. They then eigendecompose the covariance matrix and use random matrix theory, specifically the Marchenko-Pastur law, to identify the noise-dominated part of the spectrum. Those spectral components are filtered out, producing a denoised covariance matrix. Variance scores are then computed from this denoised covariance and used to rank neurons. The key point is that the original VBP relies on diagonal variance terms, but those diagonals are themselves corrupted by global spectral noise; denoising at the spectrum level leads to more stable rankings, especially under aggressive pruning.

The second component is OB²C. Starting from the Optimal Brain Compression reconstruction objective, the authors incorporate the mean-shift bias compensation directly into the objective instead of treating it as a separate post-hoc fix. With this formulation, the layer-wise Hessian reduces exactly to the input activation covariance matrix. This matters because it turns a generic second-order reconstruction problem into one that can be solved from calibration activations alone. Given a pruning mask, the method then solves for the optimal update of both the remaining weights and the bias so that the output reconstruction error is minimized after low-variance neurons are removed. Compared with original VBP, the difference is not just a better bias correction; the remaining weights are also allowed to absorb information from the pruned features. Compared with standard OBC, the explicit bias term makes the formulation better suited to the case where low-variance features behave like near-constants.

Operationally, the full pipeline is: use denoised covariance for more reliable neuron ranking, then use the same covariance for second-order recovery. The design is fairly clean, and the reuse of one statistical object across both stages is the main technical appeal.10:T635,The experiments cover DeiT, Swin, and ConvNeXt backbones across Tiny, Small, and Base scales. The main comparison is against the original VBP under different structured pruning ratios. The headline result is that the gain widens as pruning becomes more aggressive. At 50% structural reduction, Tiny models retain roughly 80% Top-1 accuracy, while Small and Base models stay above 90%; relative to VBP, the improvement reaches up to 7.33% on Swin-S and 29.46% on ConvNeXt-T. These numbers suggest that the method mainly helps in the high-compression regime, which is consistent with the paper’s diagnosis that VBP fails because of noisy ranking and insufficient compensation.

The most informative ablations are of two kinds. First, whether spectral denoising alone improves neuron ranking. The paper shows that denoised variance by itself already outperforms raw variance at high pruning ratios, indicating that covariance noise is not a minor issue but directly affects pruning decisions. Second, whether OB²C improves over mean-shift-only compensation. The results show that bias correction alone is insufficient; jointly updating the remaining weights reduces reconstruction error and improves final accuracy. If the paper includes layer-wise or module-wise analyses, the gains are likely more visible in MLP/channel pruning settings, where the low-variance-as-near-constant assumption is most plausible.

- Mixed-granularity decomposition: each layer is represented by attention block A_l and MLP block M_l, plus a composite item T_l meaning “keep the whole layer”. A mutual-exclusion constraint prevents selecting T_l together with its sub-blocks.
- Depth pruning as 0/1 knapsack: each component x has weight w(x) (parameter count) and value v(x) (importance). Given a parameter budget C, maximize total value under the capacity constraint. The solver uses dynamic programming; to make it tractable for large C, parameter counts are discretized by a factor α, reducing complexity to O(N·⌊C/α⌋).
- Importance estimation: an iterative marginal-contribution scheme where v(x) is derived from the divergence in output logits when removing x on a calibration batch.

- Metric: CRAFT (Compression Ratio Adherence Factor) quantifies how well a method meets the requested compression budget.12:T52f,ReQuant is framed as fixed-grid discrete refinement. The quantization format is frozen (bit-width, scale, zero-point, grid layout, inference kernels). The only variables are the integer codes assigned to weights.

The objective follows reconstruction-based PTQ, with an explicit focus on activation mismatch: at inference, a layer sees activations produced by a quantized prefix. Similar to GPTAQ, it uses activation-aware reconstruction by collecting activations from (i) the full-precision model and (ii) the partially-quantized model to obtain \tilde{X}, then optimizes reconstruction under the more realistic activation distribution.

Optimization is backprop-free: instead of STE/gradient descent, it performs iterative discrete updates over integer codes (a coordinate-style local search). Updates are accepted only if they reduce the overall reconstruction loss, yielding monotonic loss decrease and finite termination (a finite number of accepted updates). The offline budget is controlled by the number of sweeps T, and the per-sweep cost is argued to be comparable to a GPTAQ pass.
