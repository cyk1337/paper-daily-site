9:T419,The paper compares scalar PTQ against vector PTQ. Scalar quantization maps each weight to one of a few discrete levels; at 2 bits this representational set is too limited, and error-aware methods such as GPTQ-style objectives, SpinQuant, or QuaRot can still fail. Vector quantization maps a small weight vector to an entry in a learned codebook, which preserves correlations among dimensions that scalar grids discard. The study uses VPTQ and YAQA under a shared rotated frame. On the data side, calibration affects the Hessian used to weight quantization error, while recovery data is used for short-budget distillation after quantization. A useful engineering idea is to precompute Hessians for individual domains and sweep calibration mixtures by linearly reweighting those Hessians. Recovery freezes the quantized backbone and trains only quantization parameters, such as codebooks or scales, using a full-precision teacher with a mixture of cross-entropy and temperature-scaled KL distillation. This avoids the optimizer-state cost of full QAT.a:T4cb,The experiments cover six dense and MoE models from 3.8B to 32B, including Phi-4-mini, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen3-8B, OLMoE-1B-7B, and Qwen3-32B. Four results stand out. First, representation is the dominant factor at 2 bits: the tested scalar PTQ pipelines fall to near-random performance, while vector PTQ at about 2.3 effective bits retains 89%–97% of full-precision question-answering accuracy without training, using 39–304 times less compute than trained scalar methods. Second, generative degradation is model-dependent. QA retention is relatively stable, but HumanEval/code-generation retention ranges from about 45% for Phi-4-mini and Qwen3-8B to 81% for Qwen3-MoE. Third, calibration and recovery data interact. For Phi-4-mini, generation accuracy moves from 11.1% with web calibration to 45.7% with math calibration plus web recovery, and the best recovery data changes with the calibration choice across all three dense models studied. Fourth, the recommended low-budget recipe is vector representation, task-aligned calibration mixtures, and a short codebook-only recovery stage. With recovery, it narrows the gap to 4-bit models while using 1.4–30 times less compute than the strongest QAT baseline.b:T61c,The method is built around reinterpretation rather than a brand-new benchmark. The authors organize existing benchmarks as probes of latent abilities instead of treating them as isolated tasks. The framework, ACE-Compression, defines four ability dimensions that are explicitly presented as a practical diagnostic taxonomy, not a complete ontology of language-model competence. Ten benchmarks are then selected and mapped to these dimensions. The key idea is to read benchmark outcomes as evidence about capability preservation, and to combine standard task scores with benchmark-native readouts such as multi-turn role-play behavior, consistency signals, and psychometric-style measurements.

The model panel includes six open-weight instruction/chat models: Qwen2.5-7B/14B/32B, Llama-3.1-8B/70B, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B. The compression panel covers mainstream post-training methods: BitsAndBytes 4-bit quantization, AWQ-INT4, GPTQ-INT4, 2:4 structured sparsity, and unstructured sparsity, for more than 35 checkpoints in total. Distillation is deliberately excluded because it changes architecture, supervision, data, and objectives at the same time, making capability preservation hard to attribute to compression itself.

What distinguishes this work from standard compression papers is the level of explanation. Most compression studies answer whether a compressed model remains usable. This one asks which abilities are preserved, which fail first, and whether different compression families reshape the capability profile in systematically different ways.c:T59f,The evaluation is fairly broad: more than 35 compressed and uncompressed checkpoints, 10 diagnostic benchmarks, over 25,000 questions, 1,785 multi-turn role-playing dialogues, and 13 psychometric scales. The model range spans roughly 7B to 70B, and the compression panel includes both low-bit quantization and sparsity-based pruning.

The main findings are threefold. First, compression changes the ability profile rather than producing a simple proportional decline across all dimensions. A model can retain acceptable task-level performance while already losing specific underlying abilities. Second, there is a clear stability hierarchy: coarse, surface-level, or template-like abilities are more robust, whereas fine-grained, context-sensitive, socially situated, or multi-constraint abilities degrade more easily. Third, quantization is generally more robust than pruning in preserving abilities, not just benchmark scores. That is consistent with the intuition that reducing numerical precision while keeping the full parameter topology is often less destructive than removing weights or connections outright.

The material provided includes the evaluation scale and the headline conclusions, but not the full per-benchmark score tables. First, the architecture. HydraLLM uses an MQA backbone and treats the number of active query heads k as the single runtime width-control variable. Embedding width, attention projections, FFN width, and output-head width all scale linearly with k/Hq, and each subnetwork always takes a prefix of the corresponding dimensions. This creates a strictly nested family of subnetworks inside one parameter set. MQA is a deliberate choice: shared KV heads make head-aligned slicing cleaner than in standard multi-head attention.

Second, offline importance-based reordering. A pretrained static checkpoint does not satisfy the monotonicity needed by prefix slicing. Important units may be scattered across channels. The authors therefore collect activation statistics on a calibration corpus, score unit importance, and permute sliceable dimensions so that more important embedding channels, attention units, FFN neurons, and output channels move toward the front. They compare activation magnitude, VxC, and CETT. Their conclusion is that CETT is better aligned with prefix slicing because it measures normalized contribution to component outputs rather than raw activation strength. The permutation must be propagated consistently across linked tensors so that the model function is preserved.

Third, stochastic subnetwork training. After reordering, they adapt the checkpoint by sampling one width k per step and updating only the active subnetwork. Earlier prefix units are active under more sampled widths, so they naturally receive more gradient updates. This reinforces the importance ordering and helps shared parameters support all budgets. The training objective remains standard language modeling; the novelty is in the conversion and training schedule rather than in a new loss. For LVLMs, they add an elastic projection module that maps visual features into text embedding spaces of varying widths, so the same slicing principle extends to multimodal generation.f:T672,The experiments support three main claims. Second, reordering is critical at small widths. At 25% active heads, importance-based reordering improves post-training perplexity by 16.2%. This is one of the most informative numbers in the paper, because it shows that the bottleneck is not only adaptation training; the checkpoint must first be reorganized for prefix slicing to make sense.
