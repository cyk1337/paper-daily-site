18:T62c,The setup is fairly clean. The authors start from 8 tasks in MLE-bench Lite, each with a baseline solution chosen near the middle of the leaderboard so there is room to improve or degrade. An LLM proposes a modification, implements it, runs training, and obtains the actual score. This process is expanded into a small tree: 4 ideas from the baseline, then 2 follow-up ideas from each resulting node, giving 12 ideas per tree. Three models act as proposer-implementers: GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.1, and Kimi K3. Across 8 tasks and 3 proposer models, this yields 288 ideas, with 269 retained after failed runs and unusable predictions are removed. Because the tasks use different metrics and scales, the paper normalizes each realized score change by the within-task standard deviation of realized changes, and flips signs so positive always means improvement. Predicted changes are normalized the same way. This allows pooling across log-loss, ROC-AUC, and RMSE tasks. The evaluation then looks at numerical error, correlation, directional accuracy, the effect of more reasoning or more context, the effect of the implementing model, and self-preference. A particularly good design choice is the improve/worsen prompting split: half the ideas are intended to improve and half to worsen, not to create trivial bad examples, but to avoid a one-sided outcome distribution concentrated near small positive gains. Compared with prior work on pairwise selection, this is closer to real experiment management, where one needs an expected-value estimate rather than a binary preference.19:T561,The dataset is modest in size but informative in structure: 269 executed ideas, 8 tasks, and 5 predictor models. The main result is that current models have limited ability to predict numerical outcomes of ML experiments. They appear to do better than naive guessing and can extract some weak signal about which ideas are more promising, but the errors and calibration issues are large enough that they are not reliable stand-alone experiment filters. The ablations are useful. Increasing reasoning level or adding more prompt context does not produce a robust improvement, which suggests the bottleneck is not simply insufficient deliberation or missing local context. It looks more like the models lack an internalized model of training dynamics and empirical payoff. The identity of the implementing model matters for realized outcomes, but predictor models only partially account for that implementation variance. The paper also probes self-preference, asking whether models are more optimistic about ideas from their own family; this seems secondary rather than the main story. The most useful takeaway is not a single headline metric but the error pattern: models often generate plausible mechanistic explanations for why an idea should help, yet they lack a stable scale for expected gain and struggle with changes that sound reasonable but have little empirical effect.1a:T432,RECLAIM contains 100 reproduction tasks built from NeurIPS 2025 papers. Each task pins one match target before any agent run: the cheapest reported configuration that still supports the paper’s central empirical claim, together with the metric, reported value, evaluation scope, and success tolerance. Tasks are stratified by released artifacts. Run-tier papers provide code, data, and weights. Retrain-tier papers lack weights, so the agent must train the model. Reimplement-tier papers lack code, so the agent must implement the method from the paper. The benchmark does not provide scripted pipelines or prepared environments. Agents must plan the reproduction, install software, resolve dependency failures, manage GPU allocations, and produce execution evidence. Scoring is based on logs and outputs rather than the agent’s own narrative, using a separate language-model grader to audit whether the run supports the pinned claim. The benchmark is designed to be rebuilt yearly from new conference papers, which helps reduce quick saturation of a fixed static suite.1b:T417,The reported success rates are low across all tiers. DeepSeek-V4-Flash, the strongest evaluated agent, reproduces 41% of Run-tier papers, 27% of Retrain-tier papers, and 12% of Reimplement-tier papers. Muse Spark 1.2 reaches 15% on Reimplement-tier, the best result for that tier. A notable finding is that Run-tier remains difficult despite providing all major artifacts. On Retrain-tier, the strongest agent reaches a verified partial result or better in 67% of runs, while the weakest reaches 27%, suggesting that partial reproduction emerges before exact recovery of the reported number. Full recovery is the harder barrier: across four agents at Retrain-tier, success stays between 15% and 27% despite a 1.7x spread in mean audit score. Compute is not the main explanation. In the 96 H100-hour budget band, 48 cells spend only 6.2% of their allocated compute on average, and only one reproduces its claim.
