The teacher, conditioned on q+r, provides token-level supervision over the student’s answer tokens. This forces the student to compress information that was explicit in the teacher’s context into its own parameters.

The paper instantiates this in two settings. In self-distillation, the same model serves as both a thinking teacher and a non-thinking student, isolating whether explicit reasoning can be folded back into parameters within the same architecture family. A second important design is α-suffix masked distillation. Instead of treating reasoning as a binary choice, the teacher trace is split into a prefix and a suffix. The model caches a representation from a deeper middle layer at token t-1 and injects it into an earlier middle layer at token t through a gated fusion pathway. This creates a continuous latent channel across decoding steps, but places that channel inside the part of the network where abstract computation is believed to happen.

Two design choices matter. First, the recurrent signal does not re-enter through the embedding layer. It is fused directly into the middle of the computation graph, so the previous token’s abstract state can continue evolving in the current token’s middle-layer processing. Second, recurrence is localized to a subset of middle layers rather than applied everywhere. The rationale is functional: early layers are more lexical/syntactic, late layers are closer to vocabulary projection, and middle layers are the best place to preserve reasoning state.

Architecturally, T^2MLR defines a write location and a read location. A hidden state from the write layer is cached, then consumed at the read layer of the next token. A gating module controls how much of the current token’s local representation should be mixed with the carried-over latent state. This is more flexible than a plain residual addition because the model can decide contextually when to rely on persistent latent state and when to ignore it.

The training story is a major part of the contribution. Exact temporal recurrence would introduce token-to-token dependencies under teacher forcing and break standard sequence-parallel training. The paper proposes an approximate temporal-parallel training scheme that preserves scalable parallelism while still training the recurrent pathway. This is the practical enabler: without it, the architecture would behave more like an RNN-style serial model and would be hard to pretrain at scale. The approximation introduces a train-time compromise between recurrence fidelity and throughput, and that trade-off is central to evaluating the method.

Relative to prior work, the distinctions are clean. Compared with latent-recurrent methods, recurrence is placed in the middle layers rather than at the input side. Compared with looped/depth-recurrent Transformers, extra computation is propagated across time instead of repeatedly re-running the full stack for one token, so inference overhead stays small. Compared with a standard Transformer, the model adds an explicit continuous hidden-state pathway across tokens, reducing the token bottleneck.11:T84e,The empirical section is broad and well structured.

It first estimates a behavioral boundary from accuracy as a function of task difficulty. It then measures hidden-state susceptibility by adding small noise to the input embeddings and tracking how strongly hidden states respond across layers. The key empirical finding is that raw susceptibility is not a clean reasoning signal because it is strongly coupled to prompt length. In many programmatic and algorithmic tasks, harder examples are simply longer, and the raw response attenuates with length.

To address this, the paper introduces length-controlled susceptibility. Instead of using the raw response, it compares each example to the expected response among inputs of similar length and uses the residual as a corrected activation signal. A positive residual indicates stronger-than-expected internal activation given the length; a negative residual suggests under-activation. SPARK then combines this corrected signal with cross-layer coordination to separate reasoning-active anchors from under-activated hard targets.

The steering stage extracts a reasoning direction from the active anchors, representing the latent shift from a baseline state to an active reasoning state. That direction is injected only into selected layers and only for the under-activated hard examples. This is the main methodological distinction from generic activation steering: intervention is diagnosis-guided, example-selective, and derived from active reasoning cases rather than from a global handcrafted direction.
