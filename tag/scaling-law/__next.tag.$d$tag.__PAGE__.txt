9:T577,The proposed paradigm, Explorative Modeling (XM), factors the training loop rather than the generation procedure. At each training step, the model explores K candidate latent-to-data matches (operationally: generate K candidates / alignments) and trains on the closest match to the datapoint. The motivation is that in multimodal settings there is no canonical assignment between latents (e.g., noise) and specific datapoints; random pairing forces a single predictor to cover incompatible modes, yielding an average solution (blur in continuous domains, incoherent distributions in discrete domains). By searching for the latent/candidate already closest to each datapoint, different candidates can “commit” to different modes, making mode coverage scale with K (their “generative expressivity”).

They position XM in two regimes: (1) as an add-on to existing generative recipes, where exploration acts like an additional scaling knob improving compute/sample/parameter efficiency; and (2) as a standalone route to strict end-to-end reconstructive generation, where inference can be as little as a single forward pass because the model is trained under the same sampling procedure used at test time.

Mechanistically, the key design choice is the closest-match criterion and how the discrete selection/search is integrated with reconstruction losses so training remains stable and scalable.a:T43d,The framework separates three axes of inference-time scaling. Reasoning scaling spends more tokens before producing an answer. Sampling scaling generates more attempts and selects one. Interaction scaling alternates between proposal, external observation, and revision. The paper defines grounded feedback as observations from an instrument that measures the artifact’s actual behavior or form: running tests, rendering a page and measuring element positions, querying an external source, or measuring a layout. It also defines grounded evaluation as using such observations as the score. A coverage principle ties the two together: feedback helps only for defects that the instrument can observe. This explains why a linter may be grounded but ineffective for behavioral bugs, and why a screenshot-reading VLM can fail as a layout judge. The screenshot may crop away overflow, and small overlaps may be below the judge’s effective visual acuity. The contribution is not another agent loop; it is a sharper account of what kind of feedback and metric make interaction scaling real.b:T411,The experiments cover seven artifact-producing modalities: code, web pages, slides, figures, animations, video edits, and research reports. A dense academic-figure case shows the evaluation problem clearly: a standard screenshot VLM judge marks 14 of 15 single-shot renders as perfect, while direct measurement finds only 3 clean outputs. On the benchmark side, the authors deliberately avoid short tasks and instead curate 134 realistic, workflow-like tasks across six capability families, including scientific research and ML, systems and software engineering, combinatorial optimization, formal mathematics, professional knowledge work, and interactive games. Each task runs inside an executable workspace that supports both fast local exploration and slower, more authoritative judge feedback, resembling real workflows where local iteration and external evaluation coexist. Tasks allow at least 12 hours of continuous agent interaction, with some analyses extended to 72 hours. The paper also reports human expert effort, averaging 57.2 hours and reaching 320 hours, to argue that the tasks are not trivially saturable.

On the analysis side, the independent variable is cumulative environment interaction time, and the dependent variable is benchmark-level performance. The central empirical finding is that average performance is fit extremely well by a log-sigmoid curve, with R^2 = 0.998. The authors also propose a theoretical explanation: environment learning is modeled as frontier expansion on latent task graphs. As the agent explores, it gradually expands the reachable frontier of useful states or strategies, producing slow-then-fast-then-saturating progress. On a log-time axis, this yields a stable sigmoid-like form. The value of this theory is not formal rigor so much as a plausible mechanism for why the law is not a simple power law or pure logarithmic trend.

The paper also compares frontier agents across release generations and claims that learning speed has roughly doubled every three months since September 2025.
