18:T5d4,There are two components. First, a layer viability diagnostic. For each candidate layer, the authors build an output-projected pseudogradient for the MLP down-projection W3. Starting from the cross-entropy error on logits, they analytically backpropagate only through the unembedding and final RMSNorm, producing an approximate gradient at the output residual stream. This is then outer-producted with the cached MLP intermediate activation to form a proxy gradient for W3. On a small calibration batch, they compare this proxy with the true gradient using cosine similarity. That cosine becomes a model- and layer-specific indicator of whether forward-pass-only adaptation is likely to work.

Second, Forward-Pass-Only MLP training. During adaptation, the model body is run without constructing an autograd graph. W3 is updated directly from the pseudogradient. W1 and W2 cannot be obtained as cleanly, because their true gradients require propagating through W3^T. The method therefore uses a cached initialization copy, W3_init, as a fixed local feedback matrix to approximate those updates. This is related in spirit to direct feedback or feedback alignment, but it is much more constrained: the feedback is derived from the model’s own output-side structure and is only claimed to work in late MLP blocks. The important comparison is with partial SFT. Partial SFT also trains only late layers, but it still needs full backward through the earlier stack. FPO removes that cost entirely.19:T5a8,The diagnostic is run on six public autoregressive transformers in the 3B to 8B range. In the final 25% of layers, cosine similarity between the pseudogradient and the true gradient is around 0.47 to 0.59, and specifically for W3 it ranges from 0.35 to 0.59. The per-layer pattern is consistent: low and noisy alignment through most of the network, then a sharp rise near the end followed by a plateau. Qwen3-8B has the lowest cosine and is correctly predicted to be the hardest case for FPO.

On efficiency, FPO delivers 2.7 to 3.2 times the throughput of full-network SFT and 2.2 times the throughput of SFT-partial, with about 40% lower peak training memory at fixed batch size. This supports the main systems claim: the gain comes from eliminating graph construction and backward through the model body, not merely from training fewer parameters.

On adaptation quality, the paper evaluates OLMo-2-7B, Qwen3-8B, and Falcon3-7B. FPO achieves useful in-domain improvements while keeping off-domain benchmarks within seed noise of the base model. Full-network SFT and LoRA do not reliably preserve that behavior under the same setup. Partial SFT shows similar preservation, which strengthens the interpretation that the preservation effect is a property of the late-layer-only regime, while FPO is the efficient way to operate in that regime. The ranking induced by the cosine diagnostic also matches the ranking of downstream gains across models.1a:T5c7,The workflow has three parts:
1) Discovery via controlled layer-wise ZO tuning: using the standard two-point SPSA/MeZO-style estimator, they run ZO fine-tuning while updating only one layer at a time (all other layers frozen), keeping the same ZO hyperparameters across layers. This produces a layer-wise “utility profile” for ZO.
2) Characterization: the dominant layer is largely task-agnostic for a fixed model (same layer wins across tasks) but model-specific across families (layer index changes between LLaMA2-7B and Qwen3-8B). In contrast, FO fine-tuning distributes gains more broadly, indicating the dominance is not simply “this layer is universally most important”, but a ZO-specific phenomenon.
3) Pre-training identification: they connect the dominant layer to activation outliers. With a small calibration set and inference-only forward passes, they compute layer-wise activation statistics and pick the first layer exhibiting a clear outlier pattern; empirically this matches the dominant layer.
Their explanation centers on ZO estimator reliability: ZO updates depend on how strongly a parameter perturbation changes the final loss. Perturbations injected earlier into the residual stream propagate through all subsequent layers and can be repeatedly transformed/accumulated, yielding larger and more stable loss differences. The first outlier layer appears to be where this amplification becomes pronounced, making ZO gradient estimates higher-SNR there.1b:T663,AdaMeZO keeps the SPSA/MeZO gradient estimator as the base primitive. At each step, it perturbs parameters along a random direction z_t, computes a finite-difference loss projection from forward passes, and reconstructs the update direction using the same pseudorandom seed. The challenge is that Adam requires m_t and v_t, two full-size state vectors.

The key idea is to replace exact persistent moments with truncated, reconstructable approximations. First, AdaMeZO uses truncated first- and second-moment estimates rather than full EMAs over the entire optimization history. Older gradients are discarded, motivated by the fact that EMA already downweights them exponentially and their contribution is small relative to recent noisy zeroth-order estimates.

Second, the method exploits finer-grained PRNG replay. Instead of materializing full random directions globally, it regenerates them block by block. This allows the optimizer to reconstruct local pieces of the recent moment history on demand from stored scalar projections and PRNG seeds, rather than storing full moment tensors. In effect, it trades lightweight recomputation for memory.

This is the main distinction from prior adaptive ZO methods. HiZOO explicitly approximates diagonal Hessian information. Helene is closer to a direct Adam-style ZO optimizer with explicit state. AdaMeZO instead focuses on eliminating optimizer-state residency while preserving most of the benefit of preconditioning. The paper also provides a non-convex convergence result showing that the rate recovers that of preconditioned MeZO up to approximation terms introduced by truncation.1c:T444,The experiments are designed to show both optimization behavior and practical LLM tuning gains. On 2D toy landscapes, the optimization trajectories illustrate the intended effect clearly: AdaMeZO follows more stable paths toward minima, while MeZO is more prone to oscillation or poor convergence under the same step budget.

The main empirical section fine-tunes RoBERTa, OPT, and LLaMA on the same task suite used by MeZO, which makes the comparison clean. The headline result is efficiency rather than just end accuracy: AdaMeZO almost always reaches the same stopping criterion as MeZO with up to 70% fewer forward passes, while also delivering better final task performance. That matters because forward evaluations are the dominant cost in zeroth-order tuning.

The most informative ablations are the ones around truncation length, block-wise reconstruction granularity, and the stability of gains across model families.
