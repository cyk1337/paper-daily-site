It determines which trajectories enter training, when they become visible to the optimizer, and how stale the policy becomes before updated parameters reach rollout workers. The paper isolates two coupled problems. First, batch-level admission constraints—such as source-mixture ratios and partial-rollout completion quotas—create a train-batch admission barrier if the system waits for a full train batch before admitting any data. (1) Constraint-preserving mini-batch admission. Instead of waiting until an entire train batch satisfies all batch-wide constraints, the system admits mini-batches early. The difficulty is feasibility: early admission from faster sources can consume too much capacity and make the final train batch impossible to complete under source quotas or rollout-progress requirements. To address this, the paper introduces progressive readiness targets and feasibility guards. Progressive targets allow early mini-batches to deviate from the final train-batch composition, as long as rollout progress reaches a gradually tightening target. Feasibility guards check, at each admission step, whether the remaining capacity is still sufficient to satisfy all unmet quotas. This is the key mechanism that breaks the admission barrier without violating end-of-batch constraints. (2) On-demand trajectory data access. The paper argues that sample-level storage is a poor fit for distributed RL training. It stores trajectory payloads as independently addressable atomic units, then reorganizes access according to the actual training topology. Tensor-parallel ranks share inputs, context-parallel ranks consume sequence slices, and multimodal encoders may have different placement from the backbone. Therefore, data should be fetched at the granularity required by each consumer, not by sample boundaries. After admission, retrieval, packing, and delivery proceed incrementally in micro-batch consumption order and overlap with computation. (3) Multimodal encoding deduplication. In GRPO-style RL, multiple trajectories often share the same multimodal input. Shared storage alone does not remove repeated encoder forward passes during training. KnitTrans encodes each unique multimodal input once per mini-batch, reuses the representation across all consuming trajectories, and accumulates their gradients for the encoder update.
