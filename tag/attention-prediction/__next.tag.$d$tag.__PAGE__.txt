9:T40c,The main value for pretraining and multimodal foundation-model research is not just the pruning recipe itself, but the interface insight it reveals. First, encoder outputs already contain a strong signal about how the downstream LM will use audio tokens. That matters for audio tokenization, projector design, and encoder–decoder co-training. Second, the work shifts compression from inside the LM to before the LM, which is the only place where one saves both compute and context budget. That makes the result relevant to long-audio modeling and unified multimodal context allocation. Third, the finding that future all-layer attention is linearly decodable from encoder representations suggests a stable linear structure between encoder features and LM usage. That opens up natural follow-ups: auxiliary pretraining losses, distillation targets, or tokenizer learning objectives aligned with downstream LM salience. This is not a new modeling paradigm, but it is a useful systems-and-representation result with clear transfer value.a:T7e2,The method is simple, and the paper’s strength is in how carefully the problem is framed and validated. For each audio token i, the authors define a target score g_i: the total attention the token receives from audio positions, summed over all heads and all layers of the language model. In their prompt format, the question comes after the audio, so under causal attention the audio positions never see the question. That means g_i is independent of the question and can be used for pre-question pruning.

The key empirical finding is that g_i can be predicted from the encoder output e_i with a linear map, s_i = w^T e_i + b. This predictor, called the prior, is fit in closed form and does not require labels or a separately trained neural predictor. Triage then uses a two-stage pruning strategy. Stage 1 prunes before the LM runs, using the prior ranking; this is the stage that saves both prefilling cost and context slots. Stage 2 is used for multiple-choice settings: after two LM layers, the method refines the ranking using observed attention and prunes again. Importantly, the paper argues that for audio, mid-LM pruning should be a correction step rather than the main mechanism, because early-layer attention is still high and early rankings are not yet reliable.

Several technical details matter. The paper compares the prior against other signals available before the LM runs—position, loudness, self-information, encoder attention, and related waveform/word properties—and finds they are much weaker predictors of future LM attention. It also performs controlled analyses showing that after accounting for position and other confounds, the prior retains 95%–97% of its correlation on Qwen 3B, which argues against a trivial positional explanation. Finally, deletion studies show that removing tokens ranked highly by the prior hurts recognition more than removing energy-matched or position-matched tokens, making the predicted score look functionally meaningful rather than merely correlated.b:T78b,The evaluation spans 13 large audio language models. On 11 of them, the linear prior predicts the all-layer attention ranking with Spearman correlation ρ ≥ 0.69. By contrast, common heuristics such as loudness, self-information, and encoder attention reach at most |ρ| = 0.31 on seven tested models. This is the central empirical result: future LM usage is much more predictable from encoder representations than from standard salience heuristics.

The paper first establishes why image-style pruning intuition does not transfer to audio. On Qwen3-Omni-30B, at layer 2, text positions allocate 16.2% of their attention to audio, versus only 3.6% to images and 3.9% to video. In a controlled comparison using the same RACE passage rendered as speech versus as a page image, audio receives 2.3× the page’s layer-2 attention on Qwen 30B and 2.1× on Qwen 3B. Meanwhile, the agreement between layer-2 ranking and final all-layer ranking is only ρ = 0.39, so early LM attention is not yet a reliable ranking signal for audio.

On transcription, under a conservative budget, Triage keeps WER within 0.04 of full-audio inference. Under an aggressive budget, it achieves lower WER than every baseline, including mid-prefill methods, in all 12 transcription settings. On multiple-choice tasks, at 2.2× to 5× compression, Triage outperforms DART—the strongest baseline on average—by 0.043 mean accuracy. The systems impact is also concrete: on Qwen2.5-Omni-3B, a 2.86× pre-LM cut increases the amount of audio that fits in context from 21.8 minutes to about 62 minutes. On natural recordings of 20–43 minutes, aggressive Triage beats truncation, one-minute window voting, and DART at matched compression. At the most compressive setting, one GPU can serve 4× as many concurrent streams.
