The first major result is the reversal of yes-bias on POPE. AR LVLMs often over-answer “yes” under uncertainty, while many diffusion models lean toward “no.” This changes the precision-recall balance: some diffusion models maintain reasonable precision but lose recall because they become too conservative.

The second result is on CHAIR. Diffusion models can be competitive with strong AR baselines in hallucination rate, yet their linguistic quality is noticeably worse. The paper argues that hallucination and language quality are separable failure dimensions, which is an important evaluation lesson.

The third result comes from FairFace. Some diffusion models show near-zero accuracy on underrepresented racial groups, and gender bias is not even consistent in direction across model families. The fourth result is the strong length bias in visual MCQA. Diffusion models are much more sensitive to answer-option length than AR baselines, and the analysis traces this effect to the first denoising step. That early-step commitment is one of the clearest mechanistic findings in the paper.

The paper also includes analyses under different padding conditions and studies the correlation between confidence trajectories and hallucinated tokens. The exact numbers matter less than the fact that these patterns recur across multiple model families and tasks.c:T5c5,Two failure modes, two training-free inference patches.

(1) Mask Prior Drift: In masked diffusion decoding, all generation positions start from the same mask token ℳ. Over denoising steps, hidden states at different positions drift toward a shared “mask prior direction”, reducing inter-position semantic diversity and yielding repetitive tokens/phrases. Mask Prior Suppression counteracts this by identifying a mask-prior subspace (anchored by the mask token representation / dominant directions) and suppressing the component of generation hidden states along that subspace (a targeted projection/removal or scaling), restoring diversity without changing model parameters.

(2) Positional Attention Collapse: LDVLMs typically use RoPE to support bidirectional attention. RoPE induces a distance-dependent bias (especially via high-frequency components) that favors local interactions. During iterative unmasking, many nearby tokens remain semantically incomplete (masked or partially denoised), so attention gets trapped locally and increasingly ignores distant but informative visual tokens, degrading grounding in long outputs. Monotonic RoPE Scaling reweights RoPE components monotonically to emphasize low-frequency (more stable long-range) components while preserving relative-distance structure, stabilizing attention to distant visual tokens across denoising steps.
