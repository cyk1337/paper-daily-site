19:T4d6,TrustLDM has a static benchmark and an automated evaluation pipeline. The static benchmark covers safety, privacy, and fairness. For safety, it uses 50 harmful questions from AdvBench and misuse behaviors from JailbreakBench. For privacy, it uses 500 examples from prior privacy-evaluation work and frames the LDM as a database manager queried for sensitive information. For fairness, it samples 200 gender-balanced examples from the UCI Adult dataset and asks the model to predict whether income exceeds 50K, then analyzes failure distributions across gender groups. The evaluated models are LLaDA, LLaDA-1.5, LLaDA-MoE, and Dream. LLaDA-1.5 is strengthened through reinforcement learning, while Dream is adapted from an autoregressive backbone. The experiments fix temperature at 0.0 and use 256 generation length, 256 step size, and 64 block size by default on instruct models. The benchmark varies prefix versus suffix context, short versus long context, six static-context categories, six decoding orders, and generation length. TrustLDM-Auto adds a hierarchical search procedure that constructs model-specific adversarial contexts by first identifying vulnerable decoding settings and then narrowing the search for targeted attacks.1a:T8d1,From the provided full-text excerpt, this is better understood as a diagnostic framework paper than as a model-method paper. The technical contribution has three parts.

First, it offers a formal critique of ablation-based multimodal metrics. Multimodal Gain is defined as the performance difference between vision+text and text-without-vision settings. The authors argue that this does not measure faithful integration; it measures how much an extra signal helps after information has been artificially removed. If the image is genuinely required, failure after deleting it cannot be cleanly attributed to an architectural weakness. For Multimodal Leakage, the paper argues that the common formulation with max(0, S_wv - S_t) is biased because it ignores destructive interference cases where multimodal alignment degrades the original text-only reasoning ability of the base LLM.

Second, the paper advocates moving from global average metrics to sample-level diagnostic criteria. The intended shift is from asking whether scores improve on average when images are added to asking, for each sample, whether the model actually extracted necessary evidence from vision, whether the visual input was indispensable, and whether vision introduced confusion rather than useful grounding. The excerpt does not include the full formalism, so the exact implementation details are unclear, but the conceptual move is explicit and central.

Third, the paper ties the evaluation problem back to architecture. It argues that the monolithic vision-encoder–projector–LLM pipeline suffers from a representation bottleneck: visual information is compressed through the projector before entering the LLM, which may encourage the model to default to strong language priors instead of performing grounded visual reasoning. The novelty is not the bottleneck claim itself, which has appeared before, but the attempt to make it the direct target of evaluation rather than an indirect post hoc explanation.

At its current level of detail, the work reads as a position-plus-framework paper. The critique is meaningful, but its practical impact depends on whether the later sections provide a reproducible protocol, concrete sample-level definitions, and convincing validation.1b:T5dd,The experimental details are not fully available in the provided excerpt, so exact numbers should not be asserted. Still, the intended empirical story is clear.

First, the authors aim to show that ablation-based evaluation confounds dataset bias with architectural capability. Strong text-only performance does not necessarily imply leakage in the architectural sense; it may simply mean the dataset can be solved from textual priors. Conversely, a positive vision+text minus text-only gap does not prove faithful multimodal synthesis. The experiments are therefore expected to compare standard MG/ML-style conclusions against the paper’s sample-level diagnostics and show disagreement in attribution.

Second, the paper emphasizes destructive interference: multimodal alignment may damage the base LLM’s original reasoning ability. That suggests experiments comparing the base LLM and the aligned VLM under text-only and multimodal settings, ideally broken down by task type, to demonstrate cases that standard leakage metrics fail to capture.
