18:T79e,The first component is SVEB, a benchmark for state-value estimation. For a given partial sequence, the authors approximate the ground-truth value by large-scale Monte Carlo continuation: sample many suffix rollouts from the same prefix and average the final rewards. This gives a reference estimate for V^pi(s), against which different estimators can be compared directly. Using SVEB, they show that PPO critics in LLM RL tend to regress toward the group-average baseline rather than learning informative token-level values.

The second component is Numca, a domain-specific heuristic for mathematical reasoning. The paper reframes math problem solving as sparse-reward goal reaching and observes that numerical spans in intermediate reasoning often act as natural milestones. Numca groups disjoint rollouts using these numeric anchors and uses them to estimate future return from intermediate states. Conceptually, it borrows the spirit of hindsight-style relabeling, but adapted to text reasoning through easily parsable numbers. It is simple and cheap, but clearly tied to domains where numbers are meaningful intermediate structure.

The third component is Hista, the general solution. Hista represents each generation step with the model's hidden state and estimates the value of a state by probability-weighted averaging of returns from semantically similar states found in disjoint rollouts. It is a non-parametric estimator built on the intuition that similar hidden states should imply similar future rewards. Importantly, it requires neither extra rollouts nor additional critic training. The paper also provides a theoretical result showing that, under its assumptions, Hista yields lower estimation error than the group-average baseline. Compared with PPO critics, Hista avoids the difficulty of fitting token-level values from sparse terminal rewards. Compared with process reward models or MCTS, it avoids extra labels and heavy search.19:T545,The experimental story has three parts. First, on SVEB, the paper compares group-average reward, PPO critics, Numca, and Hista. The central finding is consistent: PPO critics behave much closer to the group-average baseline than their token-level formulation would suggest. Numca improves estimation substantially in math domains, while Hista is stronger and more stable across domains. The introduction does not expose all table values, but the full-text claim is clear and repeated: PPO value estimates regress toward the average, whereas Hista outperforms the baselines across all tested domains.

Second, the authors plug these estimators into RL training. Hista-based training outperforms strong baselines such as DAPO and CSIPO, and Numca improves math RL over simple group-average baselines. This is important because it shows that better value estimation is not only a benchmark artifact; it changes policy optimization in practice.

Third, the paper includes ablations and mechanism checks around the stability of critic collapse across datasets, the dependence of Numca on numeric milestones, and the sensitivity of Hista to hidden-state representations, weighting rules, and the number of candidate rollouts.
