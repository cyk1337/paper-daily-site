9:T689,DAG-MoE replaces the standard weighted-sum aggregation with a learned directed acyclic graph over the selected top-K experts. Routing remains unchanged: a conventional sparse router picks the top-K experts for each token. The new part is a lightweight DAG-learning module that assigns structural roles to those K experts and aggregates them through iterative updates along the learned graph.

The motivation is straightforward. Weighted summation cannot express ordered compositions such as “expert A processes first, expert B refines based on A.” It also lacks intermediate states, since every expert output is projected directly into the final mixture. By introducing a DAG, the model can form short computation chains inside a single MoE layer, where some expert outputs become intermediate nodes consumed by later experts.

The theoretical argument is that, with experts and router fixed, structural aggregation enlarges the effective combination space. The same set of selected experts can now realize different functions depending on graph structure, whereas weighted sum collapses all permutations into one. The paper further argues that this enables a form of approximate multi-step reasoning within one layer, especially for compositional problems such as dynamic-programming-like computations.

Implementation-wise, the design is intentionally local. The graph is learned only among the selected K experts, not over all N experts. This keeps the extra cost tied to K rather than to the full expert pool. That design choice is central: the paper is not trying to replace sparse routing, but to replace the overly simple aggregation that follows sparse routing.a:T60b,The experiments cover language modeling and downstream tasks, and the consistent message is that DAG-MoE improves over standard MoE when the router and expert backbone are otherwise kept comparable. The key claim is not a single headline number, but that changing only the aggregation mechanism can produce gains under the same expert-selection setup.

The most informative part is the ablation section. Several takeaways matter:
1. DAG aggregation still helps when expert granularity cannot be pushed further or when routing overhead is already a bottleneck, suggesting it is a genuinely different scaling axis.
2. Compared with simply increasing expert count, active experts, or FFN depth, structural aggregation appears to offer a better trade-off under controlled routing complexity.
3. Removing DAG learning or replacing it with unstructured/fixed aggregation degrades performance, which suggests the gain is not merely from extra computation steps but from learned inter-expert dependencies.
4. The advantage is more visible on tasks with stronger compositional structure, which is aligned with the paper’s “single-layer multi-step reasoning” argument.
