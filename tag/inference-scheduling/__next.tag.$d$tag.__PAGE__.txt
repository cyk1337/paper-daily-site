9:T516,Decoding is modeled as a stopped stochastic process: for a fixed prompt x and sampling configuration, the generated length Y is a stopping time determined by the appearance of stop tokens/strings. The discrete hazard p_t and survival S_t characterize the length distribution, but the hazard is context-dependent and random because it depends on sampled continuations.

To explain why lengths look approximately Gaussian (despite strong token-level dependence), the paper introduces a segment-based mechanism: a response is a concatenation of M semantic segments (sentences, list items, reasoning steps). Conditional on M, segment lengths L_i are treated as i.i.d., and Y=\sum_i L_i. Normality then follows from a CLT at the segment level, with an explicit Berry–Esseen-type error term.

From the normal approximation, the paper derives architecture-agnostic lower bounds on achievable point-prediction error (e.g., MAE close to \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}) and pass-rate bounds, plus an excess-risk decomposition that separates an irreducible “system” term from a “model” term. It also explains the empirically observed affine variance law \sigma=\alpha\mu+b and interprets \alpha via segment-count variability, linking predictability to generation dynamics rather than to a particular regressor head.a:T4e9,The empirical section is designed to validate the statistical claims and to show that many predictor architectures converge to the same ceiling.
