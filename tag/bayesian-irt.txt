18:T76c,The method has two main components: Facet-Probe and ODI.

Facet-Probe is a five-facet audit of ordering effects: option order, evidence-chunk order, document-rank order, image-set order, and mixed-modality order. This is a stronger design than prior work that only studies one ordering axis, because real multimodal systems inherit ordering from many sources: retrieval rank, chunk packing, image arrangement, and interleaving of text and visual evidence. The benchmark spans 12 datasets and four task families, aiming to cover both standard multiple-choice reasoning and multi-evidence aggregation settings.

ODI, or Ordering-Decomposed Item-Response Theory, is the statistical layer that separates different sources of answer flips. The authors argue that observed answer changes across permutations can come from true ordering bias, but also from decoder stochasticity or fragile decision boundaries. To disentangle these, they use a Bayesian 2PL IRT-style decomposition that estimates per-item ordering noise and per-facet systematic bias. A key design choice is the same-ordering control: the exact same input is decoded multiple times to estimate the stochastic floor, and only the excess flip rate under actual reordering is attributed to ordering effects. This control is essential; without it, raw flip rates are hard to interpret.

The paper also includes mechanism analysis and mitigation tests. One recurring mechanism is content rationalization: the model is nudged toward an answer by the ordering, then generates a plausible explanation around whichever evidence appears salient in that arrangement. For mitigation, the authors test training-free prompt modifications, but find that these are modality-conditional. A prompt trick that helps text-heavy settings does not necessarily transfer to visual reasoning or mixed-modality tasks, and different mitigations do not compose cleanly.19:T664,The empirical study is large: 18 models, including 6 frontier closed-source systems and 12 open-weight models; 12 datasets; and more than 400,000 trials. The model set includes Gemini, Claude, and ChatGPT variants, along with Qwen3.5-VL, InternVL3.5, Kimi-VL, and MedGemma families. Closed models are queried with temperature 0 where possible, and open models are greedily decoded, specifically to reduce the chance of mistaking ordinary decoding randomness for ordering effects.

Several findings stand out. First, none of the audited MLLMs is order-invariant. Screened panel-mean flip rates across facets range from 24% to 50%, and even the best model still flips on 13.4% of trials. Second, stronger models are generally more stable, with a reported Spearman correlation of about -0.95 between capability and flip rate, but capability does not eliminate the issue. Third, self-reported confidence substantially underestimates ordering risk, by 9 to 62 percentage points, so calibration alone is not a sufficient reliability signal. Fourth, the same-ordering control on Gemini shows that even at temperature 0, the excess flips under reordered inputs remain well above the same-input stochastic floor, supporting the claim that the effect is a real systematic bias rather than just decoder noise. Fifth, prompt-level mitigations can help, but only in a modality- and task-specific way.

When reading the results, it is more useful to compare the five facets than to focus only on the overall flip rate, because option-order, document-rank, and image-set sensitivity likely arise from different mechanisms and imply different remedies.1a:T585,Read this as an evaluation-method paper rather than a modeling paper. Start with the introduction and the high-level setup to understand that the target is order invariance under semantically equivalent permutations, not generic prompt sensitivity.

Then go straight to the definition of the five Facet-Probe axes. Each facet corresponds to a realistic source of ordering in deployed systems, and understanding those mappings helps interpret the results.

Next, read the ODI section carefully. This is the most important technical part because it determines whether the reported instability is actually attributable to ordering. Focus on how the paper separates ordering noise, systematic bias, and the decoder stochastic floor. Raw flip rates without this decomposition would be much less convincing.

In the experiments, first scan the main summary tables, then read the capability-versus-flip correlation plots, the same-ordering control results, and the mechanism analysis. If you care about practical fixes, read the mitigation section, but the main lesson is not a ready-made recipe; it is that shallow prompt repairs do not solve the deeper invariance problem.

A 30–40 minute pass should be enough for the main ideas.
