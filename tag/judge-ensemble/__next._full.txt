18:T6ef,The paper has three methodological layers.

First, it measures shared errors directly. Across multiple preference datasets, the authors collect decisions from both open-weight and frontier judges, compute pairwise error correlations, and summarize the amount of independent information using effective sample size. They use the design-effect approximation: once average correlation is nonzero, a bank of n judges behaves like a much smaller number of independent judges in variance terms. The paper is careful to note that this does not mean 10 judges can literally be replaced by 3.5 judges; it means the consensus contains only that much independent statistical information.

Second, it studies how dependence changes conclusions. The paper compares a pooled-vote significance test, which treats each judge vote as an independent observation, against an item-level test that accounts for correlated votes on the same examples. This isolates a common evaluation mistake: inflating evidence by counting redundant votes as if they were independent samples.

Third, it distinguishes different structures of shared failure. The paper defines global co-failure, where many judges fail on the same items, and vulnerable-subgroup failure, where errors are concentrated within a subset of judges. The key point is that average correlation alone is not enough to distinguish these cases, yet they affect majority voting differently and call for different filtering rules. To address this, the authors propose CorrFilter, which discounts agreement among judges with correlated errors, and a bias-cluster filter, which ranks examples by support from judges outside the vulnerable subgroup. A small trusted set is then used to choose among majority voting and these specialized filters.19:T731,The main quantitative result is straightforward and important. In the primary ten-judge bank, the average pairwise error correlation is 0.21. Under the design-effect approximation, this gives a variance inflation factor of about 2.85 and a variance-effective sample size of only n_eff ≈ 3.5. So ten judges do not provide ten units of independent evidence.

The paper repeats this analysis on two additional datasets and 73 alternative judge-bank compositions spanning prompt styles, model families, providers, and random seeds. The dependence is therefore not a one-off artifact of a single ensemble.

The frontier-judge result is even more striking. Three high-accuracy judges from different providers each reach 91% to 93% accuracy, yet their average error correlation is 0.56, and they make the same mistake together at 7.7 times the rate expected under independence. This directly challenges the naive intuition that stronger judges automatically make consensus more trustworthy. The paper also finds that judges from different providers are about as correlated as judges from the same provider, suggesting that shared blind spots are not just a same-family effect.

At the evaluation level, ignoring dependence changes significance conclusions in up to 28% of system comparisons: a pooled-vote test declares one system significantly better, while an item-level test that respects correlated votes does not. For filtering, the paper shows that no single fixed rule works best across both global co-failure and vulnerable-subgroup failure. A selector based on a small trusted set is tested on eight external factuality datasets, and in those transfers it always chooses plain majority voting.
