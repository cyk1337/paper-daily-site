1b:T5ae,The benchmark construction pipeline is the main technical contribution. Instead of directly scraping product demos or manually inventing tasks, the authors use a multi-stage process. They first identify AI-native startups with evidence of funding and real adoption, using that as a proxy that users genuinely delegate these workflows to AI. They then interview deep users to recover actual usage contexts, task goals, inputs, constraints, expected deliverables, and acceptance criteria. Domain experts convert those validated scenarios into benchmark instances that are reproducible, answerable, and evaluable. A final quality-control stage checks realism, answerability, evaluability, and discriminative difficulty. The result is a 97-task benchmark spanning six major domains.

The evaluation protocol is equally important. Each task is scored with multiple independent rubrics covering functionality, structure, formatting, and domain-specific quality, rather than a single holistic score. The reason is straightforward: real work products must satisfy several coupled requirements at once, and a single score can hide severe failure on one dimension. The authors also evaluate representative models under a unified agentic harness to reduce confounding from system-specific tooling. The novelty here is not an algorithmic method but the coupling of task sourcing, task abstraction, and deliverable-level evaluation into one coherent benchmark design.1c:T4c7,The headline result is that even the strongest model completes only about 30% of tasks under the unified harness. That is a strong signal that current general-purpose agents are still far from reliably producing professionally usable deliverables. Performance varies substantially by domain, with Finance, STEM & Computer Science, and Education & Humanities emerging as especially difficult. This suggests that high-constraint, expertise-heavy, and strongly structured tasks remain major weaknesses.

The paper’s analysis points to a few recurring failure modes. One is complex instruction following: models often capture the broad objective but fail to satisfy many detailed constraints simultaneously. Another is domain-specific expertise: the issue is not generic fluency, but instability in professional content, terminology, conventions, and reasoning. A third is deliverable completion: systems can often produce something plausible-looking, yet still fall short of a directly usable final product.
