9:T516,The method operates in a strict query-only setting. Given only a query q, it uses no benchmark responses, preference labels, reference answers, human-written rubrics, or model training. The framework has three LLM roles: a rubric proposer, response generators, and a rubric-blind pairwise judge. A deterministic controller maintains the active rubric set, a tree structure, and evolution memory. At each step, the proposer emits either an Add patch for one atomic rubric or a Split patch for a bundled rubric. The central design choice is to avoid asking an LLM to compare two abstract rubric sets. Instead, each candidate rubric is tested in response space. The local pair starts from a strong answer satisfying the background rubrics and the candidate rubric, then minimally edits it to violate the candidate. This tests whether violating the rubric makes an otherwise similar answer worse. The alternative pair is generated from scratch: it first creates the strongest answer that satisfies the background rubrics while violating the candidate, then minimally repairs it to satisfy the candidate. This catches over-specific, optional, or style-only rubrics. The judge compares both pairs in both presentation orders. A candidate is accepted only if the answers satisfying it win in both comparisons.a:T423,The authors decompose constitutional preference reconstruction into a three-stage pipeline: a discoverer proposes candidate principles from pairwise comparisons, an annotator votes A/B/N/A per principle per example, and an executor combines these votes into final predictions. A response representation h is decomposed into approximately orthogonal components such as style, factual content, and cultural/contextual content. Human scoring is modeled as a rubric-dependent weighted combination over these components: objective rubrics put most weight on factual dimensions, while subjective rubrics spread weight across cultural and contextual dimensions as well. An LLM judge, by contrast, is modeled as an orthogonal projector P_J with relatively small rank k, and the score is derived from the energy of the projected representation.

The intuition is that next-token pretraining and common post-training objectives make judges especially sensitive to fluency, common factual regularities, and model-familiar directions, but relatively insensitive to culturally grounded or community-specific dimensions. If multiple judges are trained on similar corpora and aligned with similar objectives, they can project onto similar low-rank subspaces. That yields high judge-judge agreement even when all of them remain far from the human scoring subspace.

From this framework, the paper derives four diagnostics. First, score-spread compression compares judge variance to human variance; strong compression indicates that judges flatten distinctions humans care about. Second, effective rank measures how many principal components are needed to explain most score variance; lower rank for judges indicates fewer active evaluation dimensions. Third, principal angle to the human subspace directly measures whether judges and humans are looking in similar directions. Fourth, stacked correlations and null-space fraction quantify whether judge-judge agreement systematically exceeds judge-human agreement and how much human variation lies in the judges’ blind space.

A strong design choice is that the paper does not stop at raw judges. It evaluates in-context prompting, supervised fine-tuning, DPO, and post-hoc demonstration-anchored calibration, asking whether training can actually rotate judges toward the human subspace rather than merely rescale outputs. Another strong choice is the dataset regime: Indic multilingual, culturally situated, community-grounded tasks, where the subjective/objective distinction is real rather than synthetic.c:T7f3,The study covers four community-built Indic datasets, eight Indic languages, and 41 LLM judges. The main findings are consistent across datasets. On subjective rubrics, LLM judges use much less of the human score range, with sigma_J / sigma_H around 0.3 to 0.5. Geometrically, judge subspaces are nearly orthogonal to the human subspace: principal angles are around 87 to 89 degrees, whereas human-human subspace angles are around 78 to 81 degrees. So judges are farther from humans than humans are from each other. Correlation patterns show the same asymmetry: judge-judge Pearson correlations are roughly 0.35, while judge-human correlations are around 0.1 or lower. The gap closes only on the rubric with a verifiable factual answer, which supports the paper’s boundary-condition story: objective rubrics have lower intrinsic dimensionality, while subjective rubrics require more dimensions than the judges effectively use.

The intervention results are especially informative. SFT and DPO often restore some score spread, so judges stop compressing scores as severely, but they barely improve the principal angle to the human subspace. In some cases they even hurt certain rubrics. That means training can make judges look more expressive without making them more human-aligned. Post-hoc demonstration-anchored calibration is the only intervention that improves all four community-health rubrics together, but it still falls short of human reliability.
