18:T5a8,The paper introduces a three-level taxonomy of STC. Benchmark Metadata Leakage refers to cases where the agent’s search is steered toward benchmark-related artifacts such as dataset pages or mirrored repositories. Question-Context Leakage refers to retrieved content that reveals the source, framing, or near-duplicate context of the benchmark item without necessarily exposing the final answer. Explicit Answer Leakage is the strongest case, where the correct answer or uniquely identifying answer evidence is directly retrieved.

Methodologically, the work is about contamination detection and impact measurement rather than building a new agent. The authors log full search trajectories, including queries, visited URLs, page contents, and turn-level behavior, and then apply detection algorithms to identify leakage at both the question level and the turn level. This allows them to ask not only whether contamination occurred, but also when it occurred and whether the agent’s reasoning trajectory shifted after the leaked evidence was observed.

A useful conceptual distinction in the paper is between training-time contamination and inference-time contamination. STC is not primarily about memorized test data inside model parameters. It is about benchmark artifacts being pulled into the context window through tool use during evaluation. That makes it especially relevant for deep research agents built on ReAct-style iterative search.19:T4fb,The study covers six public benchmarks, with a strong focus on medical and clinical settings because those questions are widely exposed across educational websites, forums, repositories, and exam-prep platforms. All evaluated benchmarks show some degree of STC. Several numbers stand out. On MedMCQA, nearly one quarter of the questions have answers retrievable from the web. On the biological and chemical subsets of Humanity’s Last Exam, STC-induced score inflation reaches up to 4%. Once an explicit leaked answer is retrieved, the agent’s accuracy rises above 95% regardless of task difficulty, and its trajectory rapidly shifts away from its earlier reasoning path toward the leaked answer.

The paper also inspects commercial systems. For Gemini Deep Research, in a sample of 100 MedQA questions, the agent visited pages containing both the original question and its answer in 60% of cases. This suggests the issue is not limited to academic prototypes.

The most informative part of the experiments is not just the contamination rate, but the behavioral effect after contamination. Answer leakage leads to both higher accuracy and faster convergence, which supports the claim that retrieval is replacing reasoning rather than merely providing weak auxiliary hints.1a:T411,This is a skim paper. Read the introduction, the three-way STC taxonomy, the detection procedure, and the case studies. Around 20 minutes should be enough to capture the main contribution.

Two questions are worth keeping in mind while reading. First, how cleanly does the paper separate metadata leakage, context leakage, and explicit answer leakage? Second, does the detection only check whether leaked content appeared on a page, or does it also examine whether the agent’s behavior changed after retrieval? The latter matters more because seeing leaked content and actually exploiting it are not the same thing.

If you only inspect one part of the experiments, prioritize MedMCQA, the HLE subsets, and the commercial-agent examples. Those sections make the evaluation fragility most concrete.
