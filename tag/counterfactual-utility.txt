1a:T687,The method, GRASP, stands for Geometry-aware Residual Alignment for Scalable Pretraining Data Attribution. It starts from a one-step smoothness lower bound on utility improvement under weighted subset interventions. This leads to a surrogate with two parts: a linear train-target relevance term and a quadratic train-train geometric penalty. The linear term measures how aligned a training example is with the evaluation target. The quadratic term captures interactions inside the selected subset, which is the key departure from additive attribution. When expanded, it naturally penalizes redundant examples and rewards complementary coverage.

For scalability, the paper avoids exact influence-function style computation. Instead, it uses the outer-product structure of last-layer gradients in causal language models. For a token, the local gradient can be written as hidden state h times next-token residual ξ = p - y. Sequence-level gradients are sums of such token contributions. GRASP then compresses these high-dimensional objects into low-dimensional feature sketches, which are used to approximate both train-target alignment and train-train geometry. This is a clear engineering trade-off: less exactness, but reusable artifacts and fast subset scoring at pretraining scale.

Another strong point is the evaluation protocol. The paper fixes component weights and retain/delete modes on development environments before testing, instead of tuning per task. That matters because many attribution methods look stronger than they are once hidden oracle tuning is removed. Here the intended object is a reusable surrogate, not a benchmark-specific fitting procedure.1b:T4dc,The main evaluation target is counterfactual fidelity under subset retraining, measured by Linear Datamodeling Score (LDS), a rank correlation between predicted subset utility and realized utility after retraining. The paper reports that GRASP decisively outperforms scalable baselines such as influence-style methods, TRAK, DataInf, and LESS in subset-level prediction. The headline numbers in the paper are that GRASP more than doubles task-level LDS rank correlation over prior scalable baselines, reduces artifact construction time by nearly an order of magnitude, and can score 100,000 candidate subsets in about 5 seconds.

The experimental design emphasizes two failure modes of additive attribution: duplicate-heavy or highly clustered data, where additive methods overvalue redundant examples, and complementary slices, where interaction-aware scoring should better capture joint utility. The ablations focus on whether the quadratic term is necessary, how sketch dimensionality affects the accuracy-speed trade-off, and whether the method is stable for both retain and delete interventions. The main takeaway is that most of the gain comes from modeling interactions, not merely from swapping in a better train-target relevance proxy.1c:T452,Start with the introduction and problem setup to understand why the paper moves from pointwise attribution to subset-level utility prediction. After that, inspect the pipeline figure and the implementation details for residual alignment, geometric penalty, and feature sketching.

In the experiments, prioritize the subset retraining protocol, the LDS definition, the main comparison tables, and the analyses on duplicates versus complementary coverage. Pay attention to whether hyperparameters are truly fixed before test evaluation, whether baselines are converted fairly into subset predictors, and whether the interaction term helps consistently across settings.
