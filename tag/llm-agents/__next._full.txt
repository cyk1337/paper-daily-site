18:T515,A three-stage simulation pipeline:
1) Data-driven persona building: start from anonymized, aggregated behavioral signals (activity patterns, engagement, inferred demographics) and use an LLM to synthesize a structured persona profile (summaries, patterns, narrative descriptors) used as conditioning context.
2) Structured elicitation: present control/treatment variants (visual stimuli) plus a target metric (CTR/subscription) via carefully designed structured questions; each persona-conditioned agent outputs numeric scores per variant and persona-grounded rationales. Prompt format is treated as a key hyperparameter.
3) Statistical aggregation: aggregate per-persona score differences into a simulated treatment-effect distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{\delta}_s, \hat{\sigma}_s)\), analogous to the normal approximation of the real experiment’s relative effect \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{\delta}_{rel}, \hat{\sigma}^2_{rel})\). Quality is mainly measured by sign agreement (improve/harm/no effect). The paper further studies persona-pool subsampling to reduce inference cost.
The main methodological distinction from prior systems is grounding personas in real behavioral signals (vs screenshot-derived synthetic personas) and systematically analyzing prompt format + persona depth/diversity + sampling.19:T551,LifePlanner is built around (1) a realistic environment and (2) a structured evaluation protocol.
1) Environment: a ~10 km^2 urban region with ~3,600 deduplicated places (spatial anchors) built from AMap API + OpenStreetMap. Each anchor is enriched with weakly-structured local social media evidence from RedNote: up to 20 notes per place via name search, and up to 10 comments per note, retaining full text, timestamps, engagement stats, and tags—yielding ~200k notes/comments. Ambiguity and irrelevant content are intentionally preserved.
2) Attribute distillation for case construction: Gemini-2.5-Flash extracts static tags (facilities/offerings) and dynamic states (temporary closures/new arrivals). Crucially, these distilled “dynamic memory” fields are used to construct tasks but are hidden from evaluated agents to prevent leakage.
3) Access separation + MCP tools: during evaluation, agents must retrieve and interpret raw posts/comments through an MCP toolset, alongside map/routing utilities.
4) Evaluation protocol: 667 synthetic yet realistic queries across four task families (Place Perception, Nearby Discovery, Routing, Trip Design) and three difficulty tiers: L0 single retrieval; L1 multi-retrieval + calculation; L2 decision-making under additional user constraints. Agents output structured JSON for multi-aspect scoring and analysis.1a:T408,This is not a direct pretraining-method paper, but it is highly relevant to post-training agents, long-context modeling, trajectory representation, and multi-model interfaces. The key reason is that it isolates a capability that standard next-token evaluation barely measures: continuing trajectories produced by another policy. For foundation-model research, that suggests two things. First, current models may be well adapted to extending their own trajectories but poorly adapted to inheriting foreign ones, which points to a missing training distribution over handoff-style contexts. Second, repository state and natural-language/tool trajectories are not symmetric in value: strong models are often burdened by weak-model trajectories, while weak models benefit from strong-model trajectories. That has direct implications for agent memory compression, handoff protocols, and multi-model pipelines. The contribution is mainly empirical and diagnostic, but the scale and clarity make it a strong baseline for future method work.1b:T9bf,The paper does not propose a new agent architecture. Its main contribution is a carefully controlled experimental framework for studying handoff direction, timing, and interface. Experiments are run on SWE-bench Verified using the same mini-swe-agent scaffold, tools, and prompts across conditions, with only the model pairing and handoff strategy changed. Within each family, the authors define LC and HC models, representing lower-cost/lower-capability and higher-cost/higher-capability variants, and instantiate this setup for both Claude and GPT families.

The central design variable is the handoff interface: what exactly is transferred to the receiving model. Four interfaces are defined. Raw passes the full prior trajectory. Compact pre asks the outgoing model to summarize the trajectory and passes only that summary. Compact suf lets the receiving model read the trajectory, compress it itself, and continue from the summary. Traj-drop removes the trajectory entirely and preserves only the working tree, namely the edited repository state on disk. This decomposition is important because it separates process information from environment state and allows the study to ask whether the receiver needs reasoning trace, current code state, or both.

Timing is also handled carefully. Instead of switching after a fixed number of steps, the paper defines switch points as percentiles of the starting model’s step-count distribution. That avoids unfair comparisons caused by variable trajectory lengths across tasks and models. For escalation (LC to HC), the authors add two restart controls: Abort + HC fresh and LC-full + HC-full. These controls charge the cost of LC work already spent but discard its trajectory, and in some cases its edits, before restarting HC from the original task state. This directly tests whether continuing a weak-model trajectory is actually better than abandoning it and starting over.

The technical value lies in the factorization of the problem and the experimental controls rather than in a new algorithm. The paper treats handoff as a distribution-shift problem: the suffix model no longer conditions on contexts sampled from its own policy, but on contexts sampled from another model’s policy. Raw continuation therefore is not just “more context”; it is continuation under foreign planning assumptions. The compaction and trajectory-drop variants are designed to identify which parts of that inherited context help and which parts contaminate.1c:T870,The study is large. The main setup includes 58 configurations per model family, 58,000 runs in total, and about 36 billion processed tokens. Evaluation is done on SWE-bench Verified, using executable pass/fail issue resolution and cost-quality tradeoffs as the main outcome.

Three findings stand out. First, in escalation settings, the default Raw continuation recovers less than half of the HC quality advantage in both model families. The Claude family is especially striking: even after charging for the LC prefix, abandoning the run and restarting with HC is both cheaper and more accurate than continuing the LC trajectory. That suggests weak-model trajectories can be so contaminated that a stronger model cannot efficiently repair them.

Second, in downshift settings, Raw continuation is often a reasonable strategy. When LC inherits an HC trajectory, it retains substantial quality gains over pure LC while remaining cheaper than running HC end to end. The balance differs by family: Claude preserves more of LC’s cost advantage, while GPT preserves more of HC’s quality advantage. In other words, strong-model-first and weak-model-finish can work, but the exact tradeoff is family dependent.

Third, the handoff interface matters a great deal, and the effect is directionally asymmetric. In escalation, reducing LC trajectory information usually helps: compaction improves savings, while trajectory removal improves quality recovery. This implies that detailed LC reasoning is often a liability for HC. In downshift, removing HC trajectory information while keeping only the working-tree edits hurts quality across both families, showing that LC depends on HC’s process guidance and cannot rely on final code state alone.

The full paper also sweeps seven switch points and calibrates them by task difficulty, which makes the conclusions more robust than a single arbitrary handoff time. Across roughly 340 to 400 decision stops over 20 in-game years, the agent drafts players, trades, negotiates contracts, invests in facilities and youth development, sets lineups, and manages finances under board pressure.
