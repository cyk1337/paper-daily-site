19:T55b,The method is a deterministic short-window probe. Starting from the last two tokens, the model predicts the next token by argmax; the window is rolled forward and the process is iterated, producing a discrete dynamical system. From 96 random two-token starts, the authors measure the fixed-point fraction φ, the proportion of trajectories that terminate in a fixed point. They also classify trajectory geometry into funnel, none, fragmented, and borderline, which gives a coarse structural summary of the attractor landscape. Prefixes are added as wrappers to every forward pass without changing the estimator, so the observed effect is attributed to the conditional distribution rather than to the readout definition. The paper compares raw, BOS, and ordinary text prefixes across six models and sweeps window size. The interesting part is that the paper is organized around failed factorizations. Prefix length is not a monotone driver. Two proposed content-side factors fail after widening the sample. Model-side hypotheses such as a universal direction, bidirectionality as a model property, and instruct-resistance also fail. Even the nearest mechanistic account, attention-sink strength, matches the sign of the fixed-point shift in only 2 of 5 models. What remains is pairwise and model-conditioned: the same prefix can push four models toward 0 and two toward 1.1a:T492,CARD has three steps. First, for each axis such as belief, intent, or knowledge, the authors train L2-regularized logistic probes on per-layer, per-head activations at the final input token. Each example contains a matched statement and its negation, and the learned unit vector points from the negated statement toward the matched one. Second, they select the top K=56 layer-head pairs by held-out probe accuracy, roughly 7% of all heads, and use forward hooks to add a scaled version of the probe direction only at the final-token position and only in those head slices. The steering scale is alpha=+10 or alpha=-10 in units of the activation standard deviation along the probe direction. Third, they measure a different prediction axis after steering, for example steering belief while evaluating action. The main statistics are the true-belief versus false-belief accuracy gap shift and a mean-accuracy shift. The accompanying Relay Chain benchmark is a cooperative grid-world setup with belief, knowledge, intent, and action questions, plus success and counter partitions that separate constant-prior behavior, world-state tracking, and belief-conditional behavior.1b:T616,The central move is to treat interpretability as a measurement-design problem. The unknown \(x\) can be an effect vector, a local gradient map, or an interaction matrix, depending on the chosen basis and intervention family. Each row of \(A\) corresponds to an intervention, \(\tilde y\) is the observed response, and \(w\) collects what the linearized measurement model misses. Importantly, the framework does not force all methods to estimate the same target; instead, it provides a shared language for how measurements are obtained and how their errors should be analyzed.

The paper then studies when such measurements are useful. It analyzes recoverability conditions, the role of intervention scale and mask density, and the point at which first-order local measurements must be replaced or augmented by interaction-aware ones. A key concept is calibration dimension: the smallest declared correction family needed so that a baseline map can predict finite-scale held-out interventions. If a low-dimensional correction suffices, a local map may still be operationally useful after calibration. If not, the residual indicates missing interactions, a poor basis, or an inadequate measurement family.

Another important concept is the observer: an estimate that is meant to guide interventions. The paper argues that an observer should be judged not by whether it reconstructs an appealing internal story, but by whether it predicts intervention effects at the scale where control will actually be applied. This links interpretability directly to control.1c:T45b,The experiments are designed to validate the framework rather than to win a benchmark. The most informative quantitative result is in the finite-scale correction setting: at a larger intervention scale, a one-dimensional fitted gain improves mean held-out attribution-patching \(R^2\) from 0.818 to 0.960, close to sparse recovery at 0.969. This supports the paper’s claim that some local maps are not fundamentally wrong, but need an explicitly declared finite-scale correction.

A second key result comes from the two-HMM wind-tunnel control experiment. With controller and actuator fixed, closed-loop target error tracks observer error across ten observer variants with Spearman correlation 0.95. This shows that interpretability error is not merely descriptive; it propagates into control error. The specificity test adds an important caveat: improving the target state alone is not enough, because an observer can still induce larger movement in a nuisance state. If your interests are closer to mechanistic analysis of LLMs, two issues are worth deeper attention. One is basis choice: the framework starts after a basis is proposed, but whether the target is sparse or recoverable depends heavily on whether the basis is heads, neurons, residual directions, or learned features. The other is intervention scale: many attribution methods are valid only infinitesimally, while practical steering and editing use finite interventions. The paper’s held-out intervention tests are aimed exactly at that gap.1e:T5da,The method combines activation patching and attention analysis across 29 languages, five open-source model families, and six checkpoints. The experimental design is deliberately controlled. The authors build verb inventories from morphological resources such as UniMorph, then create zero-shot prompts with a common template like “Conjugation of the verb X in present tense: pronoun conjugation,” adapted to each language. This is less natural than free-form text, but it preserves token-position alignment between clean and corrupted prompts, which is important for causal patching. They distinguish two recovery metrics: a broader target-form recovery metric, which asks whether patching a head helps restore the context-appropriate target token, and a stricter minimal-pair inflection recovery metric, which isolates restoration of the inflectional contrast itself. For each language, they identify the attention heads causally implicated in agreement, then compare these head-level signatures across languages. The key claim is not just that overlap exists, but that overlap varies systematically with overt inflection. Languages with clear person/number marking show stronger circuit similarity. English behaves as a bridge case: its third-person singular cases align more with strongly inflecting languages, while its non-inflecting cases do not. The paper also examines attention patterns of overlapping heads and finds that they play similar routing roles, not merely similar locations.1f:T4f8,The study covers 29 languages, five model families, and six checkpoints. Second, languages with weak or absent person-based inflection are much less similar to that shared signature, which argues against a simple “all multilingual processing is shared” story. Third, model family, size, and instruction tuning affect the absolute pattern but do not overturn the main trend. Instead of choosing one analysis pipeline, the authors enumerate a large grid of defensible specifications, with every setting taken from published implementations rather than invented for the study.

A specification includes multiple analytic axes: discovery objective, ablation operator, corruption distribution, evaluation metric, size threshold, prompt variant, seed, and granularity. The testbed is GPT-2 small on the indirect object identification task, using factorized-graph circuit discovery. Each specification returns a circuit C(s), a subset of model edges.

The key move is that the paper does not stop at graph overlap. It introduces a deterministic claim map φ that converts each discovered circuit into a structured Annex IV-style statement. This shifts the evaluation target from “do the circuits look similar?” to “would the resulting regulatory explanation be the same?” The paper then defines flip rate, modal share π*, and conditional within-group variants, and proposes a filability criterion: evidence is filable at tolerance α only if π* ≥ 1 − α.
