1a:T5da,The method combines activation patching and attention analysis across 29 languages, five open-source model families, and six checkpoints. The experimental design is deliberately controlled. The authors build verb inventories from morphological resources such as UniMorph, then create zero-shot prompts with a common template like “Conjugation of the verb X in present tense: pronoun conjugation,” adapted to each language. This is less natural than free-form text, but it preserves token-position alignment between clean and corrupted prompts, which is important for causal patching. They distinguish two recovery metrics: a broader target-form recovery metric, which asks whether patching a head helps restore the context-appropriate target token, and a stricter minimal-pair inflection recovery metric, which isolates restoration of the inflectional contrast itself. For each language, they identify the attention heads causally implicated in agreement, then compare these head-level signatures across languages. The key claim is not just that overlap exists, but that overlap varies systematically with overt inflection. Languages with clear person/number marking show stronger circuit similarity. English behaves as a bridge case: its third-person singular cases align more with strongly inflecting languages, while its non-inflecting cases do not. The paper also examines attention patterns of overlapping heads and finds that they play similar routing roles, not merely similar locations.1b:T4f8,The study covers 29 languages, five model families, and six checkpoints. The initial lexical inventory spans 34 languages, with the number of verb lemmas per language ranging from 56 to 23,782 and averaging about 1,750. The evaluation focuses on four person/number contrasts: 1sg↔2sg, 1sg↔3sg, 1pl↔3pl, and 3sg↔3pl; English has overt inflection in only two of these. The main findings are threefold. First, languages with overt subject-verb agreement show substantial overlap in the heads implicated in agreement, especially under the stricter minimal-pair inflection metric. Second, languages with weak or absent person-based inflection are much less similar to that shared signature, which argues against a simple “all multilingual processing is shared” story. Third, model family, size, and instruction tuning affect the absolute pattern but do not overturn the main trend. The provided text does not include the full numerical tables, so exact similarity values or head counts should be taken from the original figures rather than inferred here. The most important empirical artifacts to inspect are the language-by-language similarity matrices, the comparison between broad recovery and inflection-specific recovery, and the English bridge-case breakdown.1c:T5cf,The core method is a preregistered multiverse analysis. Instead of choosing one analysis pipeline, the authors enumerate a large grid of defensible specifications, with every setting taken from published implementations rather than invented for the study.

A specification includes multiple analytic axes: discovery objective, ablation operator, corruption distribution, evaluation metric, size threshold, prompt variant, seed, and granularity. The testbed is GPT-2 small on the indirect object identification task, using factorized-graph circuit discovery. Each specification returns a circuit C(s), a subset of model edges.

The key move is that the paper does not stop at graph overlap. It introduces a deterministic claim map φ that converts each discovered circuit into a structured Annex IV-style statement. This shifts the evaluation target from “do the circuits look similar?” to “would the resulting regulatory explanation be the same?” The paper then defines flip rate, modal share π*, and conditional within-group variants, and proposes a filability criterion: evidence is filable at tolerance α only if π* ≥ 1 − α.

A second methodological contribution is statistical. When different specifications produce circuits of different sizes, pooled stability statistics can reverse or mislead. The authors therefore analyze pooled results, size-controlled results, and claim mappings with size information removed. That point generalizes beyond this specific task.1d:T56c,The experimental sweep is large: 15,840 preregistered specifications on GPT-2 small and IOI, of which 7,561 produced a claim through the deterministic map.

The main findings are:
1. Claim instability is high. The reported flip rate is 0.7316. With 9 observed claim classes, the theoretical ceiling is 0.8889, so the observed value is 82.3% of the maximum possible. In practical terms, two defensible specifications often yield different regulatory statements.
2. The modal share is too low to satisfy the proposed filability criterion under reasonable tolerances, meaning current circuit evidence would struggle to qualify as stable filing material.
3. The instability survives aggressive controls. It remains even when one analytic axis is standardized, when circuit size is removed from the claim map, and when circuit size is held fixed.
4. The discovered circuits are not random noise. At fixed circuit size, they are more stable than a size-matched random null: 0.2746 versus 0.4230. But functional instability is still substantial: 0.3972 pooled and 0.4777 for the smallest circuits. Cohen’s kappa is only 0.0146, indicating that circuits found under different specifications are functionally almost uncorrelated.

Taken together, the evidence supports a restrained conclusion: circuit discovery contains signal, but in its current form it does not provide audit-grade stability.1e:T514,Do not read this one from the abstract alone. Start with the introduction and especially the section titled “What this paper is not claiming.” The authors are careful about scope, and that section prevents the common misreading that the paper is claiming mechanistic interpretability is useless.

Then read the formalism closely. Three objects matter most: the specification tuple, the deterministic claim map φ, and the filability criterion. The novelty is less about a new discovery algorithm and more about connecting analytic variation to the stability of downstream explanatory claims.

In the experiments, prioritize:
1. the seven analytic axes and where each setting comes from;
2. flip rate, modal share, and within-group stability metrics;
3. robustness checks with one axis fixed, circuit size controlled, and size removed from claims;
4. comparisons to random nulls and the kappa analysis.

If the goal is only the main message, about 45 minutes is enough. For deeper reading, two questions are worth pursuing: whether the claim map itself amplifies or dampens instability, and whether the same multiverse protocol could be extended to larger models, harder tasks, or other interpretability tools such as activation patching, feature attribution, or sparse autoencoder-based analyses.1f:T494,The behavioral evaluation uses a core100 subset from HOB, stratified over a 4-by-5 taxonomy of heuristics and constraints. Fourteen models are evaluated, spanning Claude, GPT, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi, Llama, Qwen, and GPT-OSS families. The quartet metric separates over-activation and under-activation. Several models give constraint-heavy answers even when the constraint is removed, with CBI around +0.13 to +0.29. GPT-OSS-20B and GPT-OSS-120B show under-activation, with CBI around -0.10 to -0.19. Mechanistic tests on open-weight models show a split: in Qwen3-14B, patching constraint information from an Explicit prompt into a failing Active prompt shifts the gold-shortcut gap by +6.4 nats without causing the removed controls to flip; in GPT-OSS-20B, a layer-20 probe decodes the constraint at about 88% accuracy, but patching changes the answer by only about -0.07 nats. Prompted mitigations, including chain-of-thought, precondition listing, goal decomposition, and counterfactual checking, mostly increase removed-pair harm. Across 40 model-strategy cells, mean active gain is only +0.01 to +0.04 CBI, while mean removed harm is about +0.44 to +0.47 CBI.20:T776,The setup is simple but careful. The authors freeze model weights and intervene only at inference time. They study Qwen 3.5-2B (24 layers, d=2048) and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (16 layers, d=2048).

The language axis is built layer by layer. First, they collect hidden states from 20k held-out bilingual sentence pairs and subtract the layerwise mean. Second, they run PCA on the centered activations and keep the top 150 components. Third, instead of taking PC1, they score each principal component by Cohen’s d between the two language distributions and select the component with the largest absolute separation, orienting it so the positive direction corresponds to the target language. This gives one unit vector per layer. Compared with a raw mean-difference vector or a probe weight, this procedure constrains the search to high-variance subspaces and then explicitly selects the component that best separates the language pair.

They then perform two intervention types. Steering adds a scaled offset along the language axis during the forward pass. Ablation removes the projection onto that axis. Steering tests sufficiency: can the model be pushed into another language? Ablation tests necessity: does removing the axis impair the original language behavior? The paper also includes strong controls, especially equal-magnitude random perturbations, to rule out the trivial explanation that any large perturbation can flip the output.

Two technical findings stand out. First, the geometry is pair-specific rather than a single universal “language direction”: English-Chinese and English-Spanish require distinct axes. Second, the effect is strongly layer-dependent. Mid layers often resist steering, while later layers are more sensitive, suggesting that language realization is not decided only at the final logits, but surface-form commitment becomes much easier to alter in later representations.21:T58f,The experimental sweep is fairly large for an activation intervention paper. The main evaluation uses FLORES-200, with 500 English-Chinese pairs and 100 English-Spanish pairs. Axis calibration uses 20k held-out sentence pairs from MultiUN.
