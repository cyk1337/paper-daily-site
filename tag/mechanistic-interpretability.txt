18:T402,The key method is parameter-trajectory crosscoding. Instead of fitting sparse dictionaries to activations, the method fits one sparse dictionary to the readout rows of all tokens across all checkpoints. Each token has a shared sparse code, while each feature has checkpoint-specific decoder directions and gates. This keeps feature identities aligned over training and avoids the unstable matching problem that would arise from training a separate sparse autoencoder at every checkpoint. Rows are centered and scaled per checkpoint, so feature norms track structural reorganization rather than only global norm growth. The second diagnostic is readout swapping: hold hidden states fixed and decode them with readouts from other checkpoints. Together with linear probes, this separates hidden-state availability from native readout expression. The logit is treated as a dot product between hidden state and readout row, so changes in logits can be attributed to changes in the representation, the readout, or their interaction.19:T428,This matters for pretraining research in several ways. First, it shows that having the full context in the prompt does not remove working-memory-like bottlenecks; the bottleneck can move from storage to readout. Second, it narrows the scope of a common explanation—'attention is not sharp enough'—to a much smaller regime. That explanation appears valid for very small single-layer transformers, but not for modern multilayer pretrained LLMs, where content interference dominates. Third, it has direct implications for long-context modeling and retrieval-style architectures. If failures come mainly from readout contamination rather than target localization, then sharper attention, better positional schemes, or more exact retrieval may not be the main fix. The more relevant design question becomes how representations preserve item identity across layers and how downstream computation avoids mixing competing content. The contribution is not a new model class; it is a careful mechanistic disambiguation with cross-family, cross-scale, and causal support.1a:T809,The experimental design is unusually clean. The authors present N-back as an in-context task to 15 pretrained LLMs, using the log-probability difference between 'm' and '-' as the decision variable and calibrating a fixed threshold. They report d-prime rather than raw accuracy, which is the right choice here because it separates sensitivity from threshold effects.

The key contribution is the mechanism test. First, they identify the attention heads that actually carry the task instead of averaging over all heads. They then inspect where those heads attend on lure-error trials. Under the precision account, attention should systematically miss the target on error trials; under the interference account, attention should still land on the target most of the time.

Second, they test decodability from internal activations. Even when the model outputs the wrong answer, can a simple classifier still recover whether the repeated letter occurred at the target position versus a lure position? If yes, the relevant information is present in the representation and the failure lies downstream in readout rather than retrieval.

Third, they run causal interventions. One intervention sharpens attention. The other suppresses or erases the content of competing items. If sharpening helps little while removing competitors helps a lot, the bottleneck is not target localization but content interference. That is the pattern they report.

Fourth, they use small transformers trained from scratch to establish boundary conditions. A single-layer transformer behaves much more like the precision account predicts, because one softmax has to do both localization and content aggregation. Adding a second layer changes the picture: the model can first localize coarsely and then reorganize or clean up the representation, so precision is no longer the dominant limitation. This controlled comparison explains why earlier analyses on tiny models could attribute N-back errors to softmax precision while that explanation does not transfer to modern pretrained LLMs.1b:T654,The study covers 15 pretrained LLMs from three families, spanning 70M to 14B parameters, plus minimal transformers trained from scratch. Each sequence has length 24, letters are drawn from 20 consonants, each test sequence contains four lures, thresholds are calibrated on 200 sequences, and evaluation uses 1,000 held-out sequences per model and per N. The paper first reproduces the expected behavioral pattern: d-prime declines as N increases, and errors cluster around nearby lures such as N±1.

The more important findings are mechanistic. On the heads that actually support the task, attention on error trials still tends to peak at the target rather than shifting systematically to the lure, which weakens the precision account. A simple classifier can decode target-versus-lure information from internal activations even on trials where the model ultimately makes the lure error, showing that the needed information survives inside the network. In causal tests, removing competing item content improves performance much more than merely sharpening attention, which supports a readout bottleneck. The scale analysis shows that this conclusion is stable across roughly a 200x parameter range; larger models distribute the computation across more heads, but the bottleneck does not move. The small-model comparison shows that the precision account does hold in a single-layer transformer, while a second layer is enough to substantially relax that limitation. The value of the experiments is less about one headline number and more about the consistency of these patterns across families, sizes, and interventions.1c:T493,For a quick pass, focus on three parts. First, read the introduction carefully where the two accounts are formalized and where they make opposite predictions about attention on error trials and intervention outcomes. Second, inspect the figures on task-carrying heads and on decodability of target versus lure information from internal activations; these are central to the argument. Third, read the causal intervention section comparing attention sharpening with competitor-content erasure. That comparison carries most of the paper's weight.

If you have 20–30 minutes, read the introduction, the main mechanism figures, Section 4, and Section 5, then skim Section 6 on the single-layer versus two-layer transformer contrast. If you want to go deeper, examine how task-carrying heads are defined and whether that selection could bias the conclusion. Also ask what exactly 'interference' means computationally here: value mixing inside attention, accumulation in the residual stream, or a later linear readout failure. That question connects directly to long-context failures in modern LLMs, especially cases where retrieval succeeds but answer formation still breaks.1d:T475,The experiments use SmolLM3-3B with 36 layers and OLMo3-7B with 32 layers, including 16 training checkpoints for each model family. In OLMo3-7B, after layer 16 of 32, the logit-lens direction is about 75 degrees away from the model's own lexical readout, and the Jacobian lens is about 77 degrees away; even four layers before the output, both are still at least 40 degrees off.
