9:T672,LRD consists of three complementary measurement families.

The first is the Frenet family, which captures global subspace motion. For each layer, the representation matrix is centered and reduced to its dominant singular subspace. The sequence of layers is then treated as a discrete path on the Grassmann manifold. Adjacent-layer Grassmann geodesic distance serves as a notion of speed, while Menger curvature over triples of consecutive layers measures whether the path bends. This family answers whether the dominant global directions are still changing.

The second is NRS, the Neighborhood Retention Score. It measures how much local nearest-neighbor structure is preserved from one layer to the next. This is important because global subspace stability does not imply local neighborhood stability, and vice versa.

The third is GFMI, Graph Filtration Mutual Information, which measures how well an intermediate layer aligns with the final layer in terms of structural organization. Instead of using a simple similarity score, it compares graph-based filtrations and computes mutual information, aiming to capture relational or topological agreement with the final representation.

A notable implementation choice is that for each model-task pair, the subspace dimension r is fixed as the smallest rank explaining 95% of the variance of the final layer, and the same r is used for all layers. This improves comparability across depth, though it also makes the final layer the reference point. The main idea is that the three families target different aspects of representation dynamics: global motion, local retention, and final-layer alignment.a:T648,The empirical study covers 31 off-the-shelf models and 30 MTEB tasks: 25 embedders, both encoder-based and decoder-based, plus 6 base LLMs. Tasks include classification, retrieval, semantic textual similarity, reranking, and duplicate detection. The breadth is sufficient to support architecture-level and task-level observations.

The first result is descriptive but important: LRD exposes differences that final-layer representations alone do not reveal. Different model families show distinct trajectories in speed, curvature, neighborhood retention, and alignment-to-final-layer, and these trajectories also vary by task type. Representation formation is therefore structured, not just a monotonic march toward the last layer.

The second result is about label-free model selection. The paper aggregates layer-wise measurements into model-level scores and finds positive correlation with downstream performance, with d_{0,L} being the strongest signal. The implication is that the overall representational displacement from input to final layer may serve as a useful unsupervised proxy for model quality.

The third result is on layer pruning. GFMI outperforms random pruning at the 15% and 20% budgets and achieves the best median change at every budget considered. In other words, preserving layers that are structurally important for final-layer alignment is more reliable than dropping layers at random.

For foundation-model work, the key takeaway is not a single number but the fact that layer-wise dynamics can be turned into actionable selection signals rather than remaining purely interpretive.b:T4a2,For a quick but useful read, start with the definitions of the three measurement families and make sure their roles are distinct in your mind: Frenet for dominant subspace motion, NRS for local neighborhood preservation, and GFMI for structural alignment with the final layer. Then move to the result figures organized by model family and task type.
