Its taxonomy covers 28 operation types across four branches: joint audio-visual editing, speech editing, video-only editing, and audio-only editing. Source videos are collected from existing video datasets, web videos, and generated videos, then filtered for valid audio tracks, audio-video alignment, clear subjects, clear sound sources, and well-defined editable targets. Instructions are generated from structured source-video descriptions through a human-in-the-loop pipeline: modality-aware LLM generators propose candidate instructions, a critic model filters unnatural or infeasible cases, and humans perform final verification. Evaluation is decomposed into Instruction Following, Fidelity Preserving, Realism, and Editing Intent. MLLM-based checklists judge edit execution and preservation, a separate MLLM rubric evaluates perceptual realism, and automatic metrics track audio, video, and cross-modal quality. The paper also introduces AVE-Agent, which decomposes free-form instructions into task topology graphs and uses self-reflection to inspect intermediate editing states.a:T86b,The paper has two technical components: a controlled perturbation benchmark and an evaluation protocol designed to separate visual reading from language-driven rewriting. The authors sample raw text from mC4 in English, Chinese, and Korean, apply character-level perturbations directly in text space, and then render the perturbed text into single-page document images. This is a strong design choice because the ground truth becomes the perturbed text itself, making it possible to tell whether a model transcribes what is present or silently corrects it.

They use three perturbation families. Scramble preserves the first and last character while shuffling the middle, probing reliance on lexical priors. Random substitution replaces characters with random same-script alternatives, creating severe corruption. Visual substitution replaces characters with visually similar ones, probing the competition between visual evidence and language prior. English and Korean are perturbed at the word level; Chinese uses non-overlapping 4-character windows. The perturbation rate is controlled, with clean documents retained as a baseline.

The evaluation spans three system classes: general-purpose VLMs, OCR-specialized VLMs, and traditional OCR pipelines. This taxonomy is central to the paper’s argument because it roughly orders systems by the strength of their language prior. General VLMs have broad multimodal and text pretraining and are most likely to hallucinate corrections. OCR-specialized VLMs are constrained by document extraction fine-tuning. Traditional OCR systems operate much closer to character recognition and have little generative prior. The authors also avoid adding anti-correction instructions to the prompt, so the model’s default behavior is exposed rather than suppressed.

Beyond aggregate error rates, the paper probes Qwen3-VL-4B layer by layer. The mechanistic claim is that rewriting tends to occur when the internal representation of a perturbed word remains close to the representation of the original clean word. They also report a word-length effect: short words are much more likely to be rewritten than longer ones.b:T606,The benchmark contains 1,455 single-page documents across English, Chinese, and Korean, and the evaluation covers 15 systems. The main result is clean and consistent: general-purpose VLMs degrade the most under perturbation, with up to 4.5 WER points on English; OCR-specialized VLMs degrade by about 0.2 to 2 points; traditional OCR pipelines degrade by less than 0.6 points. This strongly supports the paper’s central claim that stronger language priors increase the risk of sacrificing faithfulness on imperfect text.

A particularly important finding is the non-local effect. Corrupting only around 5% of words can increase errors on the untouched remainder by 5–10x relative to the clean baseline for general VLMs. That means the issue is not just local character confusion. The model’s page-level generation process is being pulled toward a different textual interpretation, which is much more concerning for unified document models.

The multilingual results are also informative. The same qualitative pattern holds across English, Chinese, and Korean, but the most damaging perturbation type differs by script. This suggests that rewriting is shaped both by language priors and by script-specific visual and tokenization structure. The probing analysis on Qwen3-VL-4B further shows that rewriting is more likely when the perturbed token remains representationally close to the original word. First, a data pipeline builds around 100K high-quality interleaved samples. These include authentic interleaved sequences extracted from video and synthetic sequences produced by multimodal LLMs. The point is not just to enlarge image-text pairs, but to expose the model to actual modality switching patterns such as text-image-text-image and more general N-to-M mappings.

Second, the model uses a unified decoder-only Transformer with modality-aware components. Visual understanding and visual generation are still handled by different continuous encoders: a ViT for semantic features and a VAE encoder for low-level visual details. The Transformer backbone uses shared self-attention, while QKV projections and FFNs are modality-specific, similar in spirit to mixture-of-transformers designs. Special tokens such as BOI, EOI, and EOS mark modality boundaries. The attention mask is the key design choice: across segments, the model follows a generalized causal structure where the current segment can attend to all previous segments; within a segment, attention depends on modality, with causal attention for text/control tokens and bidirectional attention for vision tokens. This preserves holistic spatial modeling inside image segments instead of forcing everything into a pure autoregressive token stream.

Third, the training objective mixes language modeling and flow-based image generation. Text and control tokens are trained with cross-entropy, while vision generation uses a rectified-flow-style MSE objective. So the model is neither purely AR nor purely diffusion-like; it is a hybrid unified backbone with discrete text prediction and continuous visual flow matching. The paper also uses progressive training and self-adaptive objectives to improve stability and gradually build free-form interleaved generation ability. Facet-Probe is a five-facet audit of ordering effects: option order, evidence-chunk order, document-rank order, image-set order, and mixed-modality order. This is a stronger design than prior work that only studies one ordering axis, because real multimodal systems inherit ordering from many sources: retrieval rank, chunk packing, image arrangement, and interleaving of text and visual evidence. The benchmark spans 12 datasets and four task families, aiming to cover both standard multiple-choice reasoning and multi-evidence aggregation settings.

ODI, or Ordering-Decomposed Item-Response Theory, is the statistical layer that separates different sources of answer flips. The authors argue that observed answer changes across permutations can come from true ordering bias, but also from decoder stochasticity or fragile decision boundaries. To disentangle these, they use a Bayesian 2PL IRT-style decomposition that estimates per-item ordering noise and per-facet systematic bias. A key design choice is the same-ordering control: the exact same input is decoded multiple times to estimate the stochastic floor, and only the excess flip rate under actual reordering is attributed to ordering effects. This control is essential; without it, raw flip rates are hard to interpret.

The paper also includes mechanism analysis and mitigation tests. One recurring mechanism is content rationalization: the model is nudged toward an answer by the ordering, then generates a plausible explanation around whichever evidence appears salient in that arrangement. For mitigation, the authors test training-free prompt modifications, but find that these are modality-conditional. A prompt trick that helps text-heavy settings does not necessarily transfer to visual reasoning or mixed-modality tasks, and different mitigations do not compose cleanly.10:T664,The empirical study is large: 18 models, including 6 frontier closed-source systems and 12 open-weight models; 12 datasets; and more than 400,000 trials. The model set includes Gemini, Claude, and ChatGPT variants, along with Qwen3.5-VL, InternVL3.5, Kimi-VL, and MedGemma families. Closed models are queried with temperature 0 where possible, and open models are greedily decoded, specifically to reduce the chance of mistaking ordinary decoding randomness for ordering effects.

Several findings stand out. First, none of the audited MLLMs is order-invariant. Screened panel-mean flip rates across facets range from 24% to 50%, and even the best model still flips on 13.4% of trials. Second, stronger models are generally more stable, with a reported Spearman correlation of about -0.95 between capability and flip rate, but capability does not eliminate the issue. Third, self-reported confidence substantially underestimates ordering risk, by 9 to 62 percentage points, so calibration alone is not a sufficient reliability signal. Fourth, the same-ordering control on Gemini shows that even at temperature 0, the excess flips under reordered inputs remain well above the same-input stochastic floor, supporting the claim that the effect is a real systematic bias rather than just decoder noise. Fifth, prompt-level mitigations can help, but only in a modality- and task-specific way.
