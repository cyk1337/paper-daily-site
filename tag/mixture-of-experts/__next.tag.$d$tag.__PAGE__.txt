9:T481,MoEvolve is a bidirectional distillation framework with a recovery-then-improvement schedule. The original low-budget model M_k and the expanded model M_k′ start from the same parameters and differ only in the number of experts activated per token. First, M_k is used as the teacher to recover M_k′ through on-policy distillation. The student samples trajectories from its current policy, and the teacher provides dense token-level next-token distributions along those trajectories; the KL objective uses stop-gradient teacher logits. This stage repairs the behavioral disruption caused by increasing k. Second, the recovered M_k′ is improved with RLVR, using SAPO to reduce instability from the mismatch between rollout and training policies. Third, the direction is reversed: the strengthened M_k′ becomes the teacher for the original M_k, transferring the gains back to the low-budget inference setting. Compared with external-teacher OPD, the teacher is fully compatible by construction. Compared with self-distillation using privileged hints, the method does not require hand-designed intermediate signals or ground-truth partial solutions.a:T708,The paper first develops the exploration-dilemma argument. In a shared-expert MoE layer, every token always receives the shared-expert contribution, while only a subset of routed experts is selected conditionally. During pretraining, the shared branch tends to dominate the learned token representation because it captures common knowledge for all tokens. Once that happens, changing the routed experts often produces only limited variation in the final features. Alternative routes become harder to distinguish, so the router receives weaker signals for meaningful exploration.

At the same time, fine-grained expert segmentation increases both E and K, which explodes the number of possible expert subsets. The paper gives a concrete example: moving from (E, K) = (8, 1) to (64, 8) increases the routing space from 8 possibilities to about 4.4 × 10^9 combinations. So the architecture demands more strategic exploration exactly when shared experts make exploration less effective.

MirrorMoE is proposed to resolve this mismatch by adaptively shrinking the routed-expert exploration space on a per-token basis. The method is derived by mirroring routing logits through sign negation. The intuition is not to add more randomness, but to suppress routed experts adaptively so that the effective search space better matches the model’s exploration capability under shared-expert dominance. This is different from simply reducing K globally, injecting noise, or tweaking load-balancing losses. The paper frames MirrorMoE as a way to improve trainability and route discoverability, not as a way to increase theoretical expressiveness. It also claims a theoretical characterization of the adaptivity, suggesting the suppression behavior is tied to routing structure rather than being a heuristic knob.b:T76c,The experiments are built around a clear question: when does adding shared experts help, and when does it interfere with the benefits of larger K? The authors train matched MoEs with and without shared experts at four scales, from 100M to 2.4B total parameters. At each scale, they fix the total number of experts E and vary the number of activated experts K over {6, 8, 10}. This is a good stress test because larger K simultaneously increases expressiveness and enlarges the routing search space.

Three results stand out. First, the benefit of shared experts diminishes as K increases. In some settings, shared-expert MoEs even get worse when K becomes larger. That is the empirical signature of the exploration dilemma: more combinatorial capacity does not translate into better pretraining because the model cannot explore the larger routing space effectively. Second, MirrorMoE consistently improves this regime. The clearest numbers given in the provided content are on the 2.4B model: +0.67 points in pretraining performance and +1.64 points on downstream performance. Third, MirrorMoE restores the expected gains from increasing K and allows shared-expert MoEs to consistently outperform their non-shared counterparts again. That is important because it suggests the issue is not shared experts per se, but the training mismatch created when shared experts are combined with a large routing space.

One, pruning can improve local LM metrics because removing low-contribution experts concentrates routing mass onto the remaining experts, which may sharpen token assignment enough to lower perplexity while still harming task performance. Two, any global importance score washes out domain-specific micro-signals: an expert that is mildly useful for math but not code will look average once scores are aggregated over all tokens.

The second part is MESA, Minimax Expert Score Allocation. Instead of assuming a single globally valid expert ranking, MESA computes domain-aware scores and iteratively boosts experts associated with the currently worst-affected domain under the tentative pruning plan. The objective is explicitly minimax: reduce the largest domain-specific degradation rather than optimize average retention. This is the main conceptual difference from frequency-based or activation-aware pruning methods such as REAP.
