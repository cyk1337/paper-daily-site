18:T91c,RubricRM is a pairwise generative reward model. The input is an instruction q and two candidate images I_A and I_B; image editing additionally includes a source image. Instead of directly predicting which image is better, the model generates a structured rubric and dimension-level assessments for both candidates in one pass. The rubric includes task intent, atomic evaluation dimensions d_i, weights w_i over those dimensions, and scoring criteria L_i for each dimension. The model then assigns per-dimension scores s_i^k to each image, aggregates them with the learned weights, and derives the final preference from the weighted totals.

The key design choice is not merely to produce free-form rationales, but to turn explanation into a structured scoring protocol. That makes the output more controllable and allows training signals to operate at the dimension level rather than only at the final pairwise label.

Training has two stages. In the SFT stage, the model learns rubric trajectories synthesized by a teacher: how to decompose the instruction into intent, dimensions, weights, criteria, and then score the candidates. In the RL stage, the paper uses dimension-level GRPO. The objective aligns predicted score differences with reference score differences on each rubric dimension, and penalizes preference-direction reversals more strongly. This indicates that the authors care not only about final pairwise accuracy but also about the internal correctness of the dimension-wise judgment process.

Data construction is a major part of the contribution. The paper argues that dynamic rubric learning requires broad instruction coverage; otherwise the model will learn biased rubric templates and generalize poorly. To address this, the authors aggregate multiple open-source preference datasets, analyze instruction types for text-to-image generation and image editing, define classification criteria, and use DeepSeek-V3.2 for automatic taxonomy annotation. They then perform balanced sampling, identify underrepresented categories, synthesize additional prompts for those categories, generate candidate images, collect expert preference annotations, and finally synthesize rubric trajectories for each preference sample. The resulting training sets contain 31.8K text-to-image samples and 30.7K image-editing samples.19:T54f,The experiments train dedicated models for text-to-image generation and image editing on Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B backbones. The headline numbers reported in the text are: RubricRM-Gen-9B reaches 72.00 on MMRB2, 74.12 on GenAI-Bench, and 84.45 on GenAI-Bench-Verified; RubricRM-Edit-9B reaches 75.40 on MMRB2, 46.40 on EditReward-ERB, and 85.64 on EditScore-ERB. The paper states that these results outperform existing reward-model baselines across both generation and editing.

The most informative parts of the experimental section are likely not just the main table, but three kinds of analysis: (1) the effect of the two-stage pipeline—SFT only, preference optimization only, and SFT plus GRPO; (2) the effect of data coverage—whether balanced sampling and long-tail supplementation improve generalization on underrepresented instruction types; and (3) the value of the rubric structure itself—how much is lost when replacing dynamic rubrics with fixed criteria or a plain scalar preference head.

The provided excerpt does not include the full ablation tables or per-baseline gains, so it would be unsafe to fabricate exact margins. Still, the reported numbers already show two useful facts: the method works on both generation and editing, and the reward model is trained as a generative multimodal model rather than a conventional scalar scorer.1a:T496,This is a skim paper. The main value is in the task formulation, data design, and training decomposition rather than mathematical novelty. Start with the introduction to see that the proposal is not just an explainable reward model, but a model that first generates a rubric and then scores under that rubric. Then read the task definition carefully to understand the four rubric components and how final preference is aggregated from dimension-level scores. After that, focus on the two-stage training setup: what SFT teaches, and what GRPO corrects. Finally, read the data section, especially the instruction taxonomy, balanced sampling, and long-tail supplementation pipeline, because dynamic rubrics only make sense if the training distribution is broad enough.

A practical reading order is: task examples/cases → rubric structure → SFT objective → RL objective → data construction → main results → ablations. Around 20–35 minutes should be enough for the main ideas.
