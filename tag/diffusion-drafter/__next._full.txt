The first is on-policy distillation, designed to reduce domain-level variance. Instead of relying on purely offline teacher-student distillation, the drafter is adapted on requests sampled from the live serving distribution, with the target model providing the supervision signal. Two design choices matter. First, the distillation objective uses reverse KL rather than the more common forward KL or plain cross-entropy. That choice is aligned with speculative decoding: reverse KL is more mode-seeking, so it encourages the drafter to concentrate probability mass on tokens the target strongly prefers, which is exactly what improves acceptance under the p/q ratio. A drafter that spreads mass too broadly over low-target-probability tokens hurts acceptance. Second, the paper introduces entry-wise divergence clipping to stabilize online updates. This is more targeted than generic gradient clipping: it limits the effect of a small number of badly mismatched token entries that would otherwise dominate the update and destabilize adaptation.

The second component is an adaptive length head, aimed at token-level variance. Instead of using a fixed candidate length k, the drafter predicts how long the draft should be for the current prefix and confidence pattern. The idea is simple but important: if acceptance probability is likely to decay quickly over the drafted span, stop early and avoid wasting target-side verification on unlikely tokens; if the context is easy, draft longer and exploit the one-pass parallelism. This turns candidate length into a conditional compute-allocation decision rather than a static hyperparameter.

The system layer also matters. The paper adds an asynchronous training-inference pipeline so online adaptation does not block serving, and an adaptive request scheduler inside the serving engine. That is important because many speculative methods look good in isolated latency measurements but degrade under queueing pressure and high concurrency. AdaFlash is explicitly designed for that regime.1c:T6ee,The evaluation spans eight benchmarks and three foundation models, including both dense and MoE targets. Against prior speculative baselines, including EAGLE-3, multi-token prediction, and DFlash, the method also reports up to 66% higher throughput at high concurrency.

The most important results are likely not the average speedup alone, but whether the proposed controls actually reduce variance. There should be three result groups worth checking closely. First, acceptance-rate distributions across domains: AdaFlash should narrow the cross-domain spread that diffusion drafters otherwise exhibit. Second, the effect of the adaptive length head on verification cost: the accepted length may drop only slightly, while the number of target-side verified tokens drops more, yielding better throughput. Third, the high-concurrency serving curves: many speculative systems lose their gains once scheduling overhead, verifier contention, and extra kernels become dominant; AdaFlash claims to remain net-positive there.

For the length head, the right comparison is fixed-k versus dynamic-k in terms of accepted tokens per unit of target compute, not just raw acceptance.
