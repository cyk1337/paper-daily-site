18:T818,The paper has two technical components. The first is a mechanistic diagnosis of what the authors call the consensus–termination gap. They generate one main reasoning trajectory per problem, freeze it, and then append probes offline to different prefixes of that same trajectory. This frozen-trajectory design is crucial because it ensures that different probing schedules and stopping rules observe identical underlying reasoning rather than perturbing it. On top of this substrate, they run three direct measurements.

First, wording sensitivity: for the same frozen prefix, they vary the wording of the probe and check whether the extracted current answer changes. If early prefixes are highly sensitive to probe wording, then the answer is being elicited by the query rather than read out from a settled internal state. Second, a hand-labeled taxonomy of wrong stops: among cases where the rule stops and the committed answer is wrong, they distinguish trajectories that had genuinely converged to a wrong answer from trajectories that had not converged at all and were merely forced into a temporary placeholder by the probe. Third, non-terminal commitment analysis: they measure how often a consensus stop commits to an answer that the original trajectory later abandons, and whether the truncated continuation would have corrected that answer.

The second component is a preregistered exhaustive sweep over a family of windowed consensus rules. The search space includes window size, agreement-share threshold, and operational knobs, for a total of 3,520 rules. These are evaluated on frozen trajectories from two models and three benchmarks with problem-grouped splits and pre-fixed acceptance gates. This is a strong design choice: instead of showcasing a few hand-picked operating points, the paper asks whether the rule family contains any robustly usable point at all. A non-consensus baseline, DEER, is run through the exact same pipeline and token accounting, which isolates whether the failure comes from the signal or from the early-exit objective itself.19:T858,The empirical case is quite tight. On the mechanism side, re-probing the same frozen prefix with different wording yields different answers 54% of the time in the first tenth of a trajectory, versus 16% near the end. Early extracted answers are therefore highly probe-dependent. In a hand-labeled set of 134 wrong-stop cases, more than half are situations where the model had not actually converged and the probe forced a premature answer, while only about a quarter are cases where the model had truly settled on a wrong answer. The non-terminal commitment analysis is even more telling: at a consensus rule that still saves 32% of tokens, one stop in nine commits an answer that the trajectory later leaves behind. Among 216 such non-terminal stops, 155 cut off a correction the trajectory would have made, while only 16 banked a correct answer from a trajectory that would later end wrong. In other words, the computation being removed is mostly useful corrective computation.

The rule sweep then shows that this is not a threshold artifact. Across 3,520 preregistered consensus rules, none clears the pre-specified safety-and-savings gates on development, so none is carried forward. Yet 478 rules clear a gate in-sample on the training split, which explains how a less disciplined selection protocol could have produced optimistic conclusions. The frontier reproduces strongly on the held-out test split (r = 0.98) and on unseen models, including one at 4× scale and with a different architecture, so the negative result is stable. The contrast with DEER is the key control: under the same protocol, DEER clears all three gates and on unseen models can lose as little as 0.3 percentage points while saving 28% of tokens. At matched savings, consensus is much more expensive: at 28% net saving, the cheapest consensus rule still costs 6.0 points of accuracy, and at 33% saving it costs 8.3 points, versus 0.5 and 2.0 points for DEER. The paper also notes that a shipped operating point, CertaIndex (CoT), loses 56–70 points on the same trajectories, which underscores how brittle these heuristics can be under strict accounting.1a:T492,Although labeled skim, this deserves a careful skim if you work on reasoning inference. Start with the introduction to pin down the target: this is not self-consistency across independently sampled trajectories, but repeated probing of a single partial trajectory. Then go straight to Section 4, which contains the strongest evidence. The three pieces to focus on are probe-wording sensitivity, the wrong-stop taxonomy, and the non-terminal commitment analysis. Together they support the central claim that agreement only shows persistence under a fixed elicitation procedure, not reasoning termination. After that, read Section 5 for the preregistered sweep design: acceptance gates, problem-grouped splits, frozen-trajectory replay, and token accounting are what make the negative result credible.

If time is short, prioritize Figure 1, the matched-savings accuracy comparisons around 28% and 33%, and the key counts 54% versus 16% and 155 versus 16. About 30 minutes is enough to get the main argument. If you are building early-exit systems, verifiers, or reasoning-boundary detectors, the protocol details in the experiments and appendix are worth reading in full.1b:T426,The headline findings are straightforward and useful. First, augmentation and automation rankings are only moderately correlated overall, with a model-level rank correlation of about 0.48. Second, the correlation varies sharply by task, from -0.04 on travel planning to 0.85 on tax preparation, showing that the overlap between being a strong solver and a strong assistant is task dependent. Third, assistance is not always beneficial. The paper reports that GPT-5-mini ranks first on average in both regimes, but in augmentation the unaided worker baseline, where GPT-3.5-Turbo works without any external help, achieves the second-best average rank. That means some assistance harms performance, likely because it is too complex, poorly calibrated, or mismatched to the worker. The task-level reversals are especially informative: for example, Claude-Opus-4.8 is among the strongest direct solvers on market trends analysis with mean rank 2.05, yet among the weakest assistants on the same task with 8.15, while GPT-4.1 shows the opposite profile on counseling.1c:T78e,The core contribution is a corrected protocol for measuring cross-lingual action-policy retention. Under a fixed scaffold, models operate over a shared five-tool alphabet and emit executed action traces alongside answers. The study covers 8 models, 6 parallel benchmarks, 41 languages, and 2.38 million rollouts. A naive trace-similarity metric fails for five reasons: short traces look artificially similar, empty traces score perfectly, unrelated traces overlap by chance, same-language reruns are not identical, and without a within-language baseline the quantity is not identifiable.

The estimator addresses each issue explicitly. Every language-task cell is run twice with identical decoding settings and token budgets, changing only the serving seed. Same-language agreement, I_within, compares the two replicates in one language. Cross-language agreement, I_cross, compares traces across languages, also cross-seed. The normalized retention metric is \tilde{I}=I_cross/I_within, which measures how much of a model’s own reproducible policy survives a language change. Comparisons are length-matched in both directions, pairs are dropped if either trace is empty, and uncertainty is estimated with task-level bootstraps. This design makes language the only systematic difference while keeping decoding noise symmetric.

The paper then probes mechanism. The evidence points to an English pivot. Translate is the most frequently used tool across adapted benchmarks, and reasoning text is about 99% ASCII even for non-Latin inputs such as Devanagari. Removing the translation tool lowers agreement in proportion to a model’s prior reliance on translation; forcing translation helps in a pre-registered ordering across models. The implication is that many multilingual agents do not execute the same policy directly in the target language. They often map the task into an English-centered internal workflow and plan from there.1d:T697,The empirical scale is substantial: 8 models, 6 parallel benchmarks, 41 languages, and 2.38M rollouts. Three results stand out. First, under greedy decoding, all 24 main cells show a positive cross-lingual policy gap, and every bootstrap interval on the raw gap excludes zero. The effect is stable rather than anecdotal. Second, every correction increases the measured effect instead of shrinking it. That is important because it means the problem was previously hidden by bad measurement, not because it was weak. Third, after normalization, four otherwise very different models converge to nearly the same policy-retention value under greedy decoding: about 71–73%. The regularity holds at the cell level, not just in model averages, and model identity explains only about 5.7% of the variance.

The temperature ladder is also informative. As sampling temperature rises, cross-language agreement changes little, while same-language self-consistency drops sharply. Without normalization, sampling noise masks the true cross-lingual policy loss.
