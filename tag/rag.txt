18:T665,Methodologically, this is a controlled reproduction plus comparison study. The authors adapt the planting probe from Wallat et al. to a setting built around Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and three RLVR agents derived from it.

The procedure has three stages. First, the model answers a question given five retrieved passages, with exactly one citation per sentence. Second, a short span from the original claim is copied into a passage that was retrieved but not cited for that claim, and the model is run again on the doctored context. Third, the evaluation checks whether the citation for the same claim shifts to the doctored passage.

Several design choices matter. Since Qwen2.5 does not expose exact grounding spans, the planted span is selected deterministically as the four consecutive words in the claim with the most characters. Because the regenerated answer may be paraphrased, the authors do not simply ask whether the doctored citation appears anywhere. Instead, they align the new answer to the original claim using Jaccard overlap and score a claim-aligned outcome. The most important addition is the no-planting control. Rather than reporting raw planted rates, the paper asks how much citation movement is actually caused by the plant relative to a baseline where nothing is planted. This avoids conflating genuine post-rationalization with ordinary answer variability or self-repetition.

For the RLVR comparison, the setup is intentionally clean: one shared base model, fixed retrieval corpus, fixed prompt, and fixed scorer. That makes differences easier to attribute to training rather than to model family or infrastructure.19:T4f9,The experiments span four QA datasets. Three findings stand out.

First, post-rationalization is common even without RL. On Wikipedia-style datasets, roughly one citation in seven is unfaithful, around the 14% range. So the baseline instruction-tuned model already treats citation partly as a surface-matching task rather than strict evidence attribution.

Second, RLVR does not fix the problem. Under fixed-context inference, Search-R1 and ReSearch stay within a couple of percentage points of the base model, and R-Search is slightly worse. The authors also provide a detection bound: with their sample sizes, they would likely have detected an improvement of about seven percentage points, so a large hidden gain is unlikely.

Third, the control experiment matters. Even with no planting at all, the selected four-word span reappears in 60%–74% of regenerated answers, simply because models often reuse their own wording. This means evaluation protocols that condition on span recovery or report only raw planted rates can overstate the effect.

A useful detail is the distinction between claim-aligned scoring and cited-anywhere scoring. The latter is kept only as a sensitivity check and is shown to be looser. That strengthens the paper’s measurement credibility.1a:T407,F²DR evaluates DeepSearch along three axes. The Content axis checks whether retrieved information covers key knowledge points and is factually grounded. The Trajectory axis evaluates the search process, including intent understanding, search atomicity, planning consistency, gap resolution, planning comprehensiveness, and iterative novelty, while penalizing redundant reasoning and plan-execution mismatch. The Answer axis combines subjective criteria such as intent match, coherence, and conciseness with objective coverage of core information. For benchmark construction, the authors sample complex queries from an enterprise search platform, extract requests that invoke deep thinking, and use DeepSeek-V3 to remove trivial or single-hop cases. They then run a full DeepSearch pipeline with DAG-based planning and reflection, search engines and MCP tools for evidence acquisition, and cross-turn evidence aggregation for final response generation. Preference pairs are filtered for discriminability and then checked by humans.1b:T54f,The benchmark uses roughly 5,000-token retrieved contexts with paired factual edits. Direct questions ask for the edited fact itself, while derived questions require a two-hop inference through unchanged downstream text. The evaluation covers Llama, Qwen, and Mistral model families, plus a 1,099-item distance-controlled variant where the answer-carrying text is moved 250 tokens downstream. At the main budget K=32, all methods nearly saturate on direct cases, but derived cases separate the policies. EditLocal recovers 0.94–1.01 of the post-edit answer margin on held-out data, with flip rates of 0.95–1.00, outperforming alternatives by 0.46–1.00 margin recovery after Holm correction with p ≤ 3×10^-9. The result depends on adjacency: moving the answer-carrying text 250 tokens downstream drops EditLocal recovery to 0.01–0.09, and stale-query attention helps only for one model family. Answer-relevant edits almost always break the stale cache, with base rate at least 0.988, and cheap features poorly predict severity, with best out-of-fold rho of 0.17. First, the task is framed in the CiteGuard environment, where the model can take bounded actions such as search, inspect, request more context, and select a paper. The training objective is tied to recovering the true cited paper, not merely generating plausible reference strings. A key design choice is to separate target retrieval from final selection, which lets the authors localize where RL helps. Compared with standard RAG-style supervised fine-tuning, this setup trains search behavior in an open retrieval space.
