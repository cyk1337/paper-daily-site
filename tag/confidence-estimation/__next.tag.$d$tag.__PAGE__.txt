9:T585,The paper studies three families of confidence estimation for LLM classifiers:
- Verbalization-based: prompt the LLM to output a numeric confidence (with variants like CoT/self-probing/top-k verbalization).
- Sampling-based: sample multiple predictions and aggregate (e.g., consistency frequency; mean of sampled verbalized confidences).
- Logit-based: use token log-probabilities (e.g., probability of the class-label token) when the API exposes logprobs.
Main analysis: verbalization yields highly discrete confidence values due to strong preferences for certain numeric tokens, producing only a few distinct thresholds.
Evaluation pitfall: AUARC requires integrating a curve defined by discrete operating points. Linear interpolation effectively invents intermediate operating points, which can over-credit sparse methods; stepwise interpolation better matches the semantics of thresholding (metrics should not change until the threshold crosses an actually attainable confidence value). The authors advocate standardizing stepwise interpolation.
Proposed method: verbalization logprobs. Instead of treating the single generated confidence number as the estimate, it uses the token probabilities (logprobs) of the verbalized digits/values to compute a probability-weighted (continuous) confidence score, reducing sparsity without additional sampling cost (assuming logprobs are available for generated tokens).a:T6f8,The study is built around a cross-runner audit rather than a single benchmark run. Fifty-three runners produced overlapping evaluations, each comparing two controlled conditions along one axis, with K=50 samples per case at temperature 1.0. The three axes are: model tier within the gpt-4.1 family (nano vs mini), prompting strategy (mini zero-shot vs mini chain-of-thought), and mid-tier versus frontier (mini vs gpt-4.1). Because multiple runners evaluate overlapping cases and each runner uses their own prompt wording, the setup creates natural replication across prompts and execution styles. That is what allows the authors to distinguish stochastic confident errors from recurring confident errors.

The analysis centers on explicit agreement and accuracy statistics, including per-case self-consistency strength and majority-vote outcomes. The paper uses hierarchical runner-clustered bootstrap with multiplicity control, which is important because the data are not cleanly iid: runners share a course context and may have shared code or discussion. The statistical framing is more careful than in many benchmark-style papers.

Two controls are especially informative. First, the recurrence analysis asks whether the same wrong answer reappears across runners and prompts, which is evidence for systematic bias rather than sampling noise. Second, the GPQA option-shuffle control tests whether apparent confidence is partly driven by answer-position priors. This is a strong design choice because multiple-choice tasks can artificially inflate agreement through constrained answer geometry. The paper also includes an exploratory cross-family check with Claude tiers, asking whether shared confident errors extend across providers rather than only within a single model family.b:T868,The main experiments use GPQA Diamond and AIME, which intentionally span opposite ends of the answer-space spectrum. GPQA is four-way multiple choice, so agreement has a nontrivial floor even under weak reasoning. AIME uses integer answers from 0 to 999, so high agreement is much harder to obtain without actual reasoning competence. This benchmark pairing is central to the paper’s argument because it shows how the usefulness of agreement depends on answer-space structure.

Several findings stand out. First, majority-vote self-consistency still improves accuracy, which is consistent with prior work. But the stronger claim people often make—that the degree of agreement itself is a reliable confidence score—is only weakly supported. The signal is somewhat useful in unsaturated mid-tier regimes, where agreement still varies meaningfully with competence. It becomes much less useful for the frontier model, which is more consistent but not proportionally more accurate.

Second, chain-of-thought prompting improves task accuracy but only marginally improves the agreement-correctness relationship. That is an important distinction. Better reasoning does not automatically imply better calibration of agreement as confidence.

Third, a confidence-routed cascade performs poorly and is dominated by simply always using the mid-tier model. This is one of the most practically relevant negative results in the paper: if routing relies on a weak confidence proxy, sending examples to a more expensive frontier model can be counterproductive.

Fourth, the option-shuffle control on GPQA shows that some of the apparent confidence is positional rather than epistemic. That result is especially important for multiple-choice evaluation and for any judge pipeline that interprets repeated agreement too literally.

Finally, the exploratory Claude-based replication is directionally consistent with the GPT-family findings. The paper reports that smaller GPT and Claude tiers choose the same wrong answers more often than a marginal-preserving null would predict, suggesting that cross-model agreement can also reflect shared bias rather than truth.c:T792,The method is built around the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle. A reasoning trajectory X is the input, a compressed representation Z corresponds to the trajectory with stepwise confidence weights, and final correctness Y is the target. The goal is to retain the parts of the trajectory that are most informative about correctness while discarding superficial variation and redundancy.

The first instantiation is a non-parametric method, NIBS. For a given problem, it aggregates multiple correct solution traces and extracts consensus steps or logical invariants. Each step in a candidate trajectory is then scored by how well it aligns with this consensus structure. Steps that match shared reasoning milestones receive high confidence; steps absent from or deviating from the consensus receive low confidence. The key idea is functional alignment rather than literal overlap. In IB terms, the consensus set approximates the relevant information, and the selected sparse subset approximates the compressed representation.

The second instantiation is GIBS, a Graph-IB variant. The authors argue that a pure sequence view is brittle when correct solutions reorder steps or use more graph-like reasoning structures. They therefore represent trajectories as graphs and learn a differentiable mask to select a subgraph most predictive of correctness. Confidence is then derived by aligning this selected subgraph with the correctness signal. This makes the method more tolerant to structural variability and closer to extracting a logical backbone than scoring a linear chain.

A practical extension is self-correction. Instead of telling the model only that the final answer is unreliable, SCA highlights low-confidence steps and uses them as repair targets. That explains why step-level confidence can outperform answer-level feedback: the model gets a localized signal about where to revise rather than a single global failure flag.d:T6ff,The empirical section has four main parts. First, step-level diagnostic quality. The paper compares SCA against answer-level confidence estimation and other black-box confidence baselines, and shows that both IB-based variants identify erroneous steps more accurately, especially when correct traces vary substantially in wording or order. The graph-based version is reported to be more robust under stronger structural variation.

Second, self-correction. This is the most consequential result in the paper. Using step-level confidence to guide correction improves correction success rate by up to 13.5% over answer-level feedback. That result matters because it shows the signal is not only more interpretable, but also operationally useful.

Third, ablations. The authors study the role of consensus extraction, the IB formulation, and graph structure. Removing consensus modeling makes the method more likely to confuse surface similarity with valid logic. Removing graph structure hurts robustness to step permutations and alternative reasoning layouts. They also analyze label availability and the number of correct traces needed to form a stable consensus, showing that dense step annotations are unnecessary but some pool of correct trajectories is still important.

Fourth, robustness and generalization. The paper evaluates different reasoning formats, domain shift, and limited-label settings. The main takeaway is that SCA transfers beyond a single CoT format, while GIBS is stronger when reasoning structure varies more substantially.
