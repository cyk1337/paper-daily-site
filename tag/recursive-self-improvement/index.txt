18:T62c,The setup is fairly clean. The authors start from 8 tasks in MLE-bench Lite, each with a baseline solution chosen near the middle of the leaderboard so there is room to improve or degrade. An LLM proposes a modification, implements it, runs training, and obtains the actual score. This process is expanded into a small tree: 4 ideas from the baseline, then 2 follow-up ideas from each resulting node, giving 12 ideas per tree. Three models act as proposer-implementers: GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.1, and Kimi K3. Across 8 tasks and 3 proposer models, this yields 288 ideas, with 269 retained after failed runs and unusable predictions are removed. Because the tasks use different metrics and scales, the paper normalizes each realized score change by the within-task standard deviation of realized changes, and flips signs so positive always means improvement. Predicted changes are normalized the same way. This allows pooling across log-loss, ROC-AUC, and RMSE tasks. The evaluation then looks at numerical error, correlation, directional accuracy, the effect of more reasoning or more context, the effect of the implementing model, and self-preference. A particularly good design choice is the improve/worsen prompting split: half the ideas are intended to improve and half to worsen, not to create trivial bad examples, but to avoid a one-sided outcome distribution concentrated near small positive gains. Compared with prior work on pairwise selection, this is closer to real experiment management, where one needs an expected-value estimate rather than a binary preference.19:T561,The dataset is modest in size but informative in structure: 269 executed ideas, 8 tasks, and 5 predictor models. The main result is that current models have limited ability to predict numerical outcomes of ML experiments. They appear to do better than naive guessing and can extract some weak signal about which ideas are more promising, but the errors and calibration issues are large enough that they are not reliable stand-alone experiment filters. The ablations are useful. Increasing reasoning level or adding more prompt context does not produce a robust improvement, which suggests the bottleneck is not simply insufficient deliberation or missing local context. It looks more like the models lack an internalized model of training dynamics and empirical payoff. The identity of the implementing model matters for realized outcomes, but predictor models only partially account for that implementation variance. The paper also probes self-preference, asking whether models are more optimistic about ideas from their own family; this seems secondary rather than the main story. The most useful takeaway is not a single headline metric but the error pattern: models often generate plausible mechanistic explanations for why an idea should help, yet they lack a stable scale for expected gain and struggle with changes that sound reasonable but have little empirical effect.1a:T854,方法上，LM2LM 把自动化 LM 开发形式化为一个 sequential decision problem。每个任务对应一个目标模型家族、参数规模、训练预算和 reference config；proposer 负责生成自然语言 proposal，builder 把 proposal 转成可执行修改，执行后得到相对 reference 的验证损失改变量。训练目标不是直接预测实验结果，而是用 RL 提升 proposer 生成高收益 proposal 的能力。

为了解决真实奖励昂贵的问题，作者引入 Research World Model, RWM。它不是单独训练的回归器，而是一个冻结的大语言模型，输入包括任务描述、reference 配置、历史实验记录以及候选 proposal，输出该 proposal 相对 baseline 的预期 gain。也就是说，RWM 通过 in-context experimental records 来做 outcome prediction，再把预测 gain 变成 RL reward。这样避免了为每个 proposal 都跑一次完整训练。

但作者的重点不只是“用 LLM 当 reward model”，而是处理分布漂移。随着 proposer 经 RL 更新，它会越来越倾向于生成 RWM 评分高、但历史记录覆盖不足的 proposal。固定记录下的 RWM 会在这些区域系统性高估，RL 随后放大这种偏差。为此，LM2LM 引入 on-policy calibration：周期性地从当前策略生成的 proposal 中，挑选 RWM 排名靠前的一部分做真实执行，把测得的真实 gain 追加进 RWM 的上下文知识，再继续训练。这个设计有两个关键点。其一，不是随机补数据，而是专门校准当前策略最可能 exploit 的高分区域；其二，不重新训练 reward model，而是通过更新 in-context records 让冻结 LLM 的判断重新锚定到真实结果。

Each environment specifies a target model family, size, training budget, and a reference configuration. The proposer is trained with RL to generate proposals with higher expected gains.

The key scalability device is a Research World Model (RWM). Instead of executing every proposal, LM2LM uses a frozen LLM to predict the gain of a candidate proposal conditioned on the task description, the reference setup, and a set of prior experimental records provided in context. These predicted gains become RL rewards. This is not just a generic reward model. The design relies on the frozen LLM’s ability to reason over structured experimental history and produce useful relative rankings among proposals.

The more important contribution is how the paper handles reward drift. As the proposer improves, it shifts toward proposals that score highly under the RWM but may lie outside the support of the initial experimental records. A fixed surrogate then becomes vulnerable to systematic overestimation, and RL amplifies that error. LM2LM addresses this with on-policy calibration: at intervals during training, it takes a subset of high-scoring proposals from the current policy, executes them for real, and appends the measured outcomes to the RWM context. This keeps the surrogate grounded on exactly the region of proposal space that matters for the current policy.

That choice is deliberate. Randomly collecting more experiments would be wasteful. Re-training a separate reward model after each batch would also be heavier.
