18:T6ba,The core artifact is MCP-Injector, a proxy-style middleware inserted between the agent and the MCP server. It does not require modifying the agent runtime, the MCP client, or the tool servers. Instead, it intercepts the call_tool boundary, which gives a clean experimental handle on semantic tool-call failures rather than lower-level deployment noise.

The fault model has three dimensions. The first is failure mode: timing failures versus content failures. Timing failures include delays, hangs, and missing responses; content failures include wrong values, malformed payloads, and schema/type violations. The second is persistence: permanent, transient, and intermittent faults. This is an important design choice because agent recovery behavior depends heavily on whether an error is persistent or bursty. The third is the injection schedule, which specifies when faults occur and is seedable for reproducibility.

A strong point is that the fault mix is calibrated rather than hand-crafted. The authors mine 57,240 issue and pull-request threads from 1,774 open-source MCP repositories and estimate the observed distribution over failure mode × persistence. The injected scenarios are therefore tied to reported ecosystem incidents instead of synthetic worst-case examples.

The evaluation is a full-factorial design: three agent stacks, two backbone sizes, three fault conditions, 25 tasks, and three seeds, for 1,350 experimental cells. The stacks include a custom multi-round plan-and-execute system, OpenAI Agents SDK, and another mainstream framework. This setup is meant to separate model scale effects from framework-level error-handling effects such as retries, exception propagation, and output validation.19:T448,Three findings matter most. First, under calibrated MCP faults, the 120B backbone does not show better fault tolerance than the 20B backbone. It does not retain a larger fraction of its fault-free score and descriptively completes fewer tasks. That is the paper’s central result and the reason for the title. Second, differences across agent stacks are substantial, suggesting that framework-level handling of tool failures matters more than backbone scale in this setting. Third, MCP-Injector itself appears to provide a reproducible and low-intrusion way to evaluate agent robustness under permanent, transient, and intermittent failures without modifying the tested systems.

The paper emphasizes retained share of fault-free performance, task completion counts, and degradation patterns under different fault conditions rather than a single average score. The 1,350-cell design gives the result some breadth. The key takeaway is not a specific percentage point but the stability of the negative conclusion: realistic MCP faults do not become much easier just because the backbone is larger.1a:T8ac,The method has two complementary intervention paths: lesioning and steering.

In the lesion path, the authors first train an aphasia subtype classifier on clinical data from AphasiaBank. The classifier labels generated text as resembling different aphasia profiles, such as Broca-like or Wernicke-like behavior. They then systematically ablate attention heads in frozen LLMs and use the classifier to identify which ablations induce which subtype-like deficits. The key point is that the intervention is not evaluated only by generic quality drop, but by the type of linguistic impairment it produces. This lets the authors associate some language functions, such as grammatical fluency or semantic coherence, with relatively discrete structural circuits.

In the steering path, they compute subtype-specific intervention vectors from residual-stream activation differences between processing human aphasic transcripts and healthy-control transcripts. Adding these vectors at selected layers during generation pushes the intact model toward the corresponding aphasia profile. They also test whether subtracting such directions can partially reverse lesion-induced deficits. Conceptually, lesioning targets structural hardware, while steering targets representational state.

The most important result is not a simplistic localization story. The paper finds a dual picture: some language functions can indeed be induced or disrupted through relatively specific circuits and directions, but those functions are also strongly entangled. Different aphasia subtypes share substantial overlap in their lesion-sensitive components, and their steering vectors have high cosine similarity. So the internal organization looks neither cleanly modular nor uniformly diffuse. It looks like a densely distributed system with vulnerable critical substructures.

A notable design choice is the use of a clinically informed classifier as the main diagnostic interface instead of standard LM metrics. The benefit is a structured taxonomy of degradation types. The cost is dependence on the classifier’s training distribution, which the authors acknowledge and partly offset by showing stronger generalization for steering interventions.1b:T6e4,The experiments use AphasiaBank and focus on two elicitation tasks: Cinderella story retelling and sandwich-making instructions. These provide narrative and procedural speech settings with different linguistic demands. The model suite includes 11 LLMs from four families: GPT-2 XL, Llama-2-7B/13B-chat, Pythia 1B/1.4B/2.8B/6.9B, and Qwen3.5 0.8B/2B/4B/9B. Generation uses greedy decoding with a repetition penalty of 1.25 to avoid looping in base models.

The reported findings are mainly qualitative-structural rather than centered on one benchmark score. First, classifier-guided lesioning induces target aphasia-like subtypes more reliably than unguided or weaker intervention strategies, suggesting that some attention heads are more tightly linked to specific language failure modes. Second, steering can robustly induce corresponding aphasic behavior in intact models and appears to generalize better across tasks and prompts than structural lesioning. Third, there is substantial overlap across subtypes, both in the structural components implicated by lesioning and in the similarity of steering directions, supporting the paper’s dense-entanglement claim.

For a pretraining-oriented reader, the useful takeaway is that the authors turn language degradation into a structured, classifiable object and validate the pattern across multiple model families and scales. That said, the scope is limited. The study focuses mainly on attention-head ablations and residual-stream steering, not MLP circuits, multi-component pathways, or training-stage evolution. The clinical classifier may also conflate some stylistic distortions with aphasia-like deficits. So the results are better read as mechanistic probes than as strong evidence of cognitive equivalence.1c:T514,Start with the introduction and method. The key framing is that aphasia is used as a taxonomy for LLM failure modes, not as a medical application.
