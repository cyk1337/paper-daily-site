9:T51c,SCEval keeps the question, answer space, gold label, and modality channels fixed, and perturbs only the internal structure of one or more modalities. Text corruption means deletion or shuffling rather than removing the text channel. Visual corruption means noise, blur, or occlusion rather than replacing the image/video. Audio corruption means muting, distortion, or segment removal rather than dropping the audio stream. The benchmark uses 14 curated operators: four for text, seven for vision, and three for audio. It evaluates single-, dual-, and tri-modal corruption settings. For stochastic operators, the protocol averages over up to three random variants and also records the worst-variant gap, which is a useful way to expose seed-level fragility without making the main metric depend on one unlucky sample. The dataset is built from 300 candidates sampled from Social-IQ, OmniBench, and VALOR, then filtered by a third-party team down to 273 human-verified tri-modal examples. The filtering criteria are important: the clean item must be valid, the answer must be tri-modally supportable, and the corrupted inputs must remain interpretable to humans. Because the design is sample-paired, clean-versus-corrupted comparisons are made on the same underlying example rather than across shifted subsets.a:T433,The main results are behavioral diagnostics. First, structural corruption consistently reduces clean all-modality accuracy, showing that many apparent omni-modal gains are fragile to internal evidence damage. Second, text-vision joint corruption emerges as the most stable shared fault line, suggesting that this interaction is especially brittle across models. Third, degradation is non-additive and model-dependent: the effect of dual- or tri-modal corruption is not a simple sum of single-modality drops, which implies that different models rely on different evidence pathways. The most striking empirical pattern is that under fixed heavy visual noise, 14 of 15 models perform worse with mildly corrupted text (t30) than with heavily corrupted text (t70). The paper is careful not to over-interpret this, but the result strongly suggests that partially damaged text can be more misleading than obviously broken text. In terms of scale, the benchmark contains 273 human-verified base examples, each expanded into clean, single-, dual-, and tri-modal corruption conditions.b:T792,GRAIN has three main components. First, it uses an explicit pipeline. Rather than letting the model mix extraction, graph construction, and computation inside unconstrained chain-of-thought, the task is decomposed into semantic parsing of the graph and task arguments, followed by tool execution with the appropriate graph algorithm. The motivation is straightforward: shortest path, matching, coloring, and related computations are deterministic; the real weakness of LLMs is the language-to-structure mapping.

Second, the method enforces structural invariance during training. The authors construct multiple isomorphic views of the same underlying graph: node identifiers may be random, semantic, aliased, or noisy, and the task description may range from standardized templates to more realistic narratives. The model is trained to recover the same intermediate graph despite these surface changes. This is more than ordinary augmentation, because the reward directly checks whether the predicted intermediate graph matches the ground-truth topology. That gives denser and more targeted feedback than final-answer supervision alone.

Third, GRAIN uses single-agent RL as a form of robustness distillation. The goal is not to build a multi-agent system, but to absorb into one model the robustness that would otherwise require repeated decomposition and verification across multiple agents. The RL objective jointly rewards correct structure recovery and correct tool invocation. The intended effect is to learn identifier-invariant and formulation-invariant parsing rules instead of memorizing templates.

The accompanying benchmark, GRIT, is also a substantial part of the contribution. It covers six graph problems across 31 real-world prototypes, with controlled multi-view variants for naming and narrative style. That makes it possible to separate failures of structure extraction from failures of downstream algorithmic solving.c:T6da,The experiments are built around robustness diagnosis rather than a single leaderboard number. The first result is that the robustness gap is real: under identifier shift and task-form shift, performance varies significantly even when the underlying graph is unchanged. Closed models tend to retain stronger average accuracy but still show unstable variance across naming schemes. Open models degrade much more sharply under non-canonical identifiers and realistic narratives. This is an important finding because it shows that strong performance on sanitized graph benchmarks does not imply reliable real-world graph reasoning.

Against baselines, GRAIN is reported to outperform standard CoT, SFT, and more expensive multi-agent approaches in out-of-distribution robustness while keeping latency and token cost lower. The most informative results are likely the breakdowns by naming scheme, narrative type, graph size, and task complexity. If the gains are consistent across those slices, that supports the claim that the reward is improving the language-to-structure stage rather than exploiting a narrow template.

A key ablation concerns the structural reward itself. The paper argues that rewarding intermediate graph correctness is better than rewarding only the final answer, and better than static supervision alone. The reason is intuitive: final-answer rewards are sparse and can be accidentally correct even when parsing is wrong, while structural rewards directly supervise semantic parsing quality. Although the abstract does not list all numbers, the central empirical message is that GRAIN yields more stable performance under surface perturbations and converts part of the robustness of complex multi-agent pipelines into a single model.d:T49e,A good skim has three stops.
