9:T545,The paper has two parts: characterization and mitigation. In the characterization part, pigeonholing is operationalized through three behaviors. One is mistake repetition: the model copies or extends an incorrect answer from context, especially in verifiable domains such as math and coding. Another is stance flipping: the model changes its original answer to align with an incorrect in-context claim. The third is mode collapse: even when the in-context example is not wrong but merely one valid option among many, the model becomes much less likely to explore alternatives and instead reproduces the provided pattern. This broadens the phenomenon from simple accuracy loss to distributional narrowing.

The mitigation strategy is synthetic error augmentation. Instead of changing the architecture, the authors inject erroneous conversational contexts into RLVR and DPO training data, so the model is explicitly optimized to recover when the conditioning history is already corrupted. This is the key distinction from vanilla RLVR or vanilla DPO, which are trained on cleaner or more standard preference-style data. The paper’s argument is that the failure is not just task incompetence; it is misconditioning under bad context. Therefore the training distribution must include corrupted histories if one wants robust recovery at inference time.a:T567,The empirical coverage is broad. The study evaluates frontier closed models including GPT-4.1, Sonnet 4.6, and Gemini 2.5 Pro, plus seven open-weight models in the 2B–9B range. Tasks span 10 settings, including coding, math, persuasion, and open-ended user interactions, with real-user queries drawn from PRISM, Infinity Chat 100, and Community Alignment. Additional ablations are run on six benchmarks covering logical, commonsense, and social reasoning.

The headline results are clear. Even strong frontier models degrade substantially under erroneous context: around 38% performance drop in math and 40% in coding. The degradation is often monotonic with the number of turns, which suggests cumulative context poisoning rather than isolated prompt brittleness. On the mitigation side, RLVR with synthetic errors improves recovery by 43%–60% over vanilla RLVR on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks. DPO with synthetic errors improves by 34%–35% over vanilla DPO. A key negative result is that vanilla RLVR and vanilla DPO do not reliably fix mistake pigeonholing at test time.

Another strength of the experiments is that they do not only report task accuracy. They also track repeated mistakes, stance changes, and reduced answer diversity, which makes the phenomenon more convincing as a distributional failure mode rather than a narrow benchmark artifact.b:T419,Starting from the properties of KL divergence, the work compares the mode-seeking property of reverse KL and the mode-covering property of forward KL, pointing out that forward KL minimization is the theoretically optimal solution to avoid mode collapse. However, directly optimizing forward KL is computationally intractable, as it requires sampling from a global reward-weighted distribution over trajectories. Accordingly, DMPO (Distribution-Matching Policy Optimization) is proposed to approximate forward KL minimization at the group level: for each group of G trajectories sampled per query, a Boltzmann distribution is constructed as the target distribution based on rewards. A distribution-matching regularization term is added to the standard GRPO objective to align the policy distribution with this intra-group target distribution. This method is naturally compatible with GRPO's existing group-level advantage normalization logic, and can be implemented with only minor code modifications, requiring no additional model or data overhead.c:T46a,Evaluation benchmarks include two categories.
