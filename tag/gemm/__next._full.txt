1b:T436,CurveFP is a block-scaled closed-product codebook family: 1) Quantized magnitudes are distributed across K interleaved logarithmic curves; each nonzero element stores a sign, two's-complement exponent, and curve index under a shared per-block power-of-two scale. 2) A rational radix r=2^(p/q) parameterizes the logarithmic lattice: p/q<1 contracts range and improves local resolution, while p/q>1 expands range. 3) Uniform curve spacing makes multiplication exactly closed in the code domain: sign XOR, curve-index addition, and exponent addition with carry — no lookup table, projection, or general multiplier is needed to form the product code. 4) A finite phase count qK/gcd(p,qK) is derived to explicitly map representation choices to the number of fractional-exponent classes that accumulation must combine, yielding an explicit quality-vs-complexity contract. 5) One algebra is instantiated as CurveFP8 for training (E4C3 for weights/activations, E5C2 for gradients, mirroring the range split of FP8 E4M3/E5M2) and CurveFP7 (E3C3, 12.5% narrower than FP8) for inference.1c:T72b,The core proposal is the Spense format. Instead of forcing the entire weight matrix into standard 2:4 sparsity, the matrix is split into two contiguous regions: one remains dense, the other is pruned with the standard 2:4 pattern. The practical advantage is immediate: the dense region can be executed with existing dense GEMM libraries such as cuBLAS, and the sparse region with existing 2:4 sparse GEMM libraries such as cuSPARSELt. This avoids custom compiler work and avoids activation expansion or expensive runtime transformations used by prior relaxed-sparsity methods.

The reason this contiguous split is possible is the permutation invariance of the intermediate dimension in GLU-style MLPs. If the same permutation is applied consistently across the gate, up, and down projections, the MLP function is unchanged. That lets the method choose a subset of intermediate channels to keep dense, reorder them into a contiguous block, and prune the rest into the 2:4 region.

On top of this format, the paper proposes two one-shot pruning methods. SpenseGPT is the simple baseline: keep the first p% of intermediate indices dense and apply SparseGPT to prune the remaining region into 2:4 sparsity. SpenseGPT+ improves this by selecting dense indices using an activation-aware importance score inspired by Wanda, combining each intermediate index’s contribution across the gate, up, and down projections. SparseGPT is then used on the remaining weights to minimize layer output reconstruction error.

A central empirical point is that dense-index selection matters enormously. The paper reports that with the same dense budget, AIME can vary from 76.67 down to 8.89 depending on which indices are kept dense. So the method is not just about relaxing sparsity; it is about identifying the right subspace to preserve in dense form.1d:T4a6,The evaluation uses Qwen3-32B and Seed-OSS-36B, with benchmarks including AIME, GPQA, LiveCodeBench, and IFEval. The main claim is that SpenseGPT+ preserves quality across these tasks while achieving up to about 1.2× end-to-end decoding speedup over the dense baseline on B200 GPUs with FP8 precision in vLLM.

One of the most useful analyses is the sparse-GEMM speed study. The paper shows that sparse kernels only approach their theoretical benefit when the GEMM M dimension is large enough to amortize overhead. When M is small, sparse GEMM can even be slower than dense GEMM. This is an important reality check for autoregressive decoding, where many operations run in regimes that are not ideal for sparse kernels.

Another key result is the dense-index selection ablation. Even with the same dense fraction, different index choices can lead to dramatic accuracy differences, which directly motivates the activation-aware selection in SpenseGPT+.
