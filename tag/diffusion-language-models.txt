19:T460,The study evaluates LLaDA 8B and DREAM 7B on logic puzzles with four common text-noise types. Confidence is measured from the geometric average of answer-token certainties at the steps where tokens are revealed. The evaluation separates calibration from selective prediction: calibration checks whether confidence values match empirical accuracy, while selective prediction measures whether confidence can rank examples for safe skipping, mainly through AUROC. The paper then analyzes several fixes. Temperature-style score adjustment changes scale but not ranking. Matching training aligns noisy predictions with clean predictions, but clean predictions are already highly confident, so confidence ranking remains poor. Question-level error detectors produce nearly the same signal for all candidate answers to a question, so they also cannot reorder answers. To show that answer-specific correctness information still exists internally, the authors introduce Latent Correctness Readout, a lightweight probe over hidden states at answer-word positions. It freezes the base model and requires no extra generation passes.1a:T7e9,The method has three layers. First, the authors train per-layer MLP probes on residual-stream hidden states to regress the current denoising progress τ during inference. They do this on two representative masked DLMs, LLaDA-1.5 and Dream, and vary which token positions are used: masked tokens only, non-masked tokens only, or all tokens. This is an important design choice because it tests whether the signal is localized to visibly masked positions or distributed more globally across the sequence representation.

Second, they move from readout to intervention. From the learned time-related representation, they identify a low-dimensional subspace associated with inferred denoising progress and steer activations along that subspace. The key point is that they do not alter the external unmasking schedule or the observed mask ratio; they only perturb internal states. If generation behavior shifts in a systematic way, that is stronger evidence that the model is using an internal notion of progress rather than merely exposing a correlational artifact.

Third, they analyze the geometry of the recovered signal across layers. The paper argues that denoising progress is encoded in a relatively low-dimensional subspace rather than as a diffuse weak statistic spread across the full hidden state. That matters because a compact control-like representation is easier to interpret as an internal state variable. The layerwise analysis also helps distinguish whether the signal is strongest in early, middle, or late computation and whether similar structure appears across different DLM families.

Relative to prior DLM interpretability work, this paper is narrower and more central: it does not focus on attention sinks, harmful mask manipulations, or generic SAE features, but on the internal representation of denoising progress itself. It also differs from theoretical work that links mask ratio to diffusion time analytically; here the question is whether the model actually instantiates such a variable internally.1b:T6d3,The empirical study is built around LLaDA-1.5 and Dream. The main takeaways visible from the full text are:

1. Probe results: on LLaDA, layerwise probes recover τ with R² above 0.5 over a substantial range of layers, indicating that denoising progress is not only weakly present or confined to a single stage. The token-subset comparisons are especially important. If non-masked tokens also carry strong τ information, that supports the interpretation of a global latent state rather than a trivial reflection of visible mask positions.

2. Steering results: perturbing activations along the recovered time subspace systematically modulates the model’s effective notion of denoising stage. The abstract states this qualitatively; the main text should be checked for the exact quantitative readouts used to support the claim, such as changes in unmasking behavior, entropy, confidence, or output characteristics. The key thing to verify is whether the effect is monotonic and robust across layers and prompts.

3. Geometric analysis: the paper argues that the time signal lives in a low-dimensional subspace and studies how that structure evolves across depth. The most useful details to extract while reading are: how low-dimensional the signal is, whether the dominant directions align across layers, whether there is a middle-layer peak, and whether LLaDA and Dream show similar patterns.

From a pretraining-research perspective, the strongest part of the experimental package is the progression from decodability to causal steering to representational geometry. The main limitation is that the paper stops at analysis; it does not yet turn the discovered latent clock into a better training objective or a more efficient decoding policy.1c:T573,Start with the definition of τ and the three research questions in the introduction. Then read the probing section carefully, especially the setup behind Figure 2. That is the foundation of the paper. Pay attention to whether the authors rule out trivial shortcuts such as direct dependence on visible mask statistics, token identity, or positional artifacts.

Next, focus on the steering section. The important questions are how the time subspace is constructed, whether intervention is applied at one layer or multiple layers, and whether the perturbation is a simple additive shift or something more structured. This determines how strong the causal claim really is.

Then read the geometry section and the appendix. The appendix is likely where the probe architecture, training details, token-subset definitions, and the relation between τ and the continuous corruption variable s are spelled out. For a quick pass, the main figures on probing, steering, and layerwise geometry are enough in about 20–30 minutes. For a serious mechanistic read, including appendices, budget around an hour.

Two follow-up questions are worth keeping in mind while reading. First, can this latent clock be used for adaptive decoding, where the model decides how aggressively to unmask? Second, is the recovered signal only a proxy for mask ratio, or does it encode a richer notion of residual uncertainty?1d:T523,The method has a training component and an inference component. On the training side, Multi-block Teacher Forcing concatenates a clean prefix with a bounded group of consecutive noisy blocks, instead of corrupting only the current block. Noisy blocks inside the group can attend to one another through a Group-Aware Dual-Stream Mask, while preserving block-causal generation constraints. The dual-stream design separates the prediction view from the conditioning content view, so the model can use neighboring noisy blocks without leaking the target token. MultiTF also uses a more aggressive and randomized noise scheduler inside each group, creating heterogeneous slot-wise noise patterns similar to inference: front slots may be nearly solved while later slots remain heavily masked. Blocks are partitioned into groups with varying sizes so the model sees different running-set sizes and relative slot positions. On the inference side, the Block Buffer keeps a fixed number of physical block slots. Future blocks enter by activating idle slots, and completed front blocks are committed to the KV cache before their slots are reused. This keeps input shape static, supports CUDA Graph replay, preserves prefix caching, and turns higher TPF into wall-clock throughput rather than only a nominal parallelism metric.1e:T63b,This is a unified evaluation and controlled-analysis paper. The authors evaluate eight modern DLMs, covering both pure diffusion and block-diffusion families, and compare them against strong autoregressive baselines under a shared protocol. The key contribution is not a catalog of model internals, but a decomposition of the main degrees of freedom that govern DLM behavior. First, they run a unified downstream evaluation across eight benchmarks spanning general knowledge, reasoning, coding, and machine translation, instead of letting each model be judged on a different task mix. Second, they treat inference budget as a first-class variable: denoising steps, sequence length, block size, and unmasking ratio are explicitly analyzed rather than hidden as implementation details. Third, they run controlled small-scale scaling experiments under matched training conditions to compare perplexity and scaling behavior with fewer confounds from data and compute differences. Fourth, they add a systems view by reporting peak memory and FLOPs not only for a single forward pass but for full generation. The most informative comparison is between pure diffusion and block diffusion. Pure diffusion offers stronger global parallel refinement in principle, but often requires many denoising rounds. Block diffusion tries to recover some of that flexibility while reducing total generation cost through local blockwise generation and conditioning. The paper’s contribution is to place these design choices into one experimental framework and study how they affect quality, efficiency, and scaling.1f:T566,The experiments appear in three main groups. The authors emphasize comparable inference budgets rather than paper-specific best-case settings. The second group is hyperparameter and decoding ablations: they study how performance changes with more denoising steps, where diminishing returns begin, how sequence length and block size affect both quality and cost, and how parallel unmasking trades off throughput against output quality.
