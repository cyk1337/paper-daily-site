1b:T40c,Visual tool-use is formulated as a structural causal graph that separates three paths to the final answer: the direct path from raw input, observation-mediated paths (where visual content of tool outputs genuinely affects the answer), and action-induced shortcuts (where only the text of tool calls influences the answer). Three intervention protocols with increasing granularity are designed: 1) Policy-level: compare overall performance between tool-use and direct inference; 2) Trajectory-level: corrupt all returned observations at runtime and measure answer changes; 3) Step-level: replace a single observation with a counterfactual crop and compute Visual Evidence Gain (corresponding to Natural Indirect Effect) to judge whether the visual content truly alters answer preference. Based on intervention results, each rollout is categorized into four groups: Calibrated, Calling Without Looking (CWL), Looking Without Planning (LWP), and No-call; policy-level accuracy improvements are then decomposed into per-group contributions.1c:T4a5,Proposes PaDoc, a layout-grounded parallel document parser built on a region-sufficiency assumption: conditioned on the full page image and a region's layout specification, content generation for that region is independent of content from other regions. The joint parsing distribution is factorized into a serial layout prediction stream, and parallel content generation branches spawned immediately after each layout is predicted, reducing logical decoding depth from the sum of all region content lengths to the longest single layout-content path. During training, an ancestor attention mask restricts each content branch to attend only to the shared page image and historical layout prefix, with no access to sibling region content. This is implemented with packed variable-length sequences, requiring no auxiliary prediction heads or extra losses, and retaining the standard next-token prediction objective. During inference, PaDoc runs on vLLM with global shared KV cache for the page image and layout prefix, decoding each region content branch as a concurrent request. The model is initialized from Qwen3-VL-2B, adapted via continual pre-training followed by supervised fine-tuning.1d:T813,The central method is TokenSwap. Starting from a text-only sample, the pipeline identifies visualizable concepts that matter for the task, replaces them with semantically aligned natural images, and keeps the surrounding text unchanged. This is different from rendering the whole prompt as an image or building a conventional image-question pair. The replacement happens at the concept or phrase level, so the text-only and multimodal versions remain structurally close. That gives a cleaner controlled test: the main change is that some semantic units move from text tokens to visual tokens.

TokenSwap-Bench is built on top of MMLU. The construction has three main stages. First, Gemini-2.0-Flash is used to identify concepts in questions and answer options that can be visually grounded. Second, each concept is turned into an image with a text-to-image model. Third, the benchmark applies several filters to ensure that measured gaps are not artifacts of poor substitutions. The important filters are:
1. Faithfulness verification: the generated image must correctly represent the intended concept in context.
2. Task relevance verification: if removing the replaced concepts from the original text does not affect prediction, the sample is not useful for measuring a meaningful gap and is filtered out.
3. Semantic recoverability: the substituted image is captioned and the caption is inserted back into text form to check whether the original semantics can be recovered.

The training-side intervention is data-centric rather than architectural. The authors augment training with image-interleaved counterparts of text data, and they test this both in pre-training and post-training settings. The intuition is simple and plausible: repeated exposure to semantically matched text and image realizations should force the model to learn more stable cross-modal representations and more consistent reasoning under token-type substitution. Compared with generic image-text alignment losses or caption-style supervision, this directly targets substitution invariance.1e:T6bc,There are four main empirical takeaways.

First, the modality gap is pervasive and large. Across 42 MLLMs, replacing text spans with aligned images causes performance drops ranging from 4.2% to 47.4%, with an average decrease of 19.6% and a reported 95% confidence interval of ±3.3%. This is not a small robustness issue; it is a systematic reliability problem.

Second, reasoning-oriented models show much smaller gaps. The paper reports an average gap of about 10.1% for reasoning models versus 25.5% for non-reasoning models. That suggests the difference is likely due to training-time mechanisms rather than prompting alone, such as stronger process supervision, more multimodal reasoning data, or post-training recipes that implicitly enforce consistency.

Third, common prompting strategies do not reliably solve the problem. Chain-of-thought and few-shot prompting provide limited benefit. This matters because it shifts the diagnosis away from inference-time reasoning and toward representation learning and training distribution.

Fourth, scaling compute helps only marginally. A 10× increase in training FLOPs reduces the gap by only about 2.8%. So this is not something current scaling trends fix automatically.

On the mitigation side, TokenSwap augmentation reduces the modality gap in both pre-training and post-training while preserving text-only and standard vision-language performance, with slight gains in some cases. The excerpt does not include all table values, so the exact effect sizes should be checked in the full results. The key things to verify are consistency across model sizes, sensitivity to augmentation ratio, and whether the claimed no-regression result on text-only tasks really holds.1f:T54b,Read it in four passes: problem definition, benchmark construction, empirical diagnosis, and training mitigation.

For replication decisions, the benchmark filtering details and the training ablations deserve a close read.20:T508,FlowGuard runs the target MLLM in three modes on the same example: text-only, vision-only, and full multimodal inference. It then compares the resulting predictive distributions and compresses their relationships into a low-dimensional FlowVector. The features are designed to capture three effects: how far the fused prediction moves away from each unimodal prediction, how much the two unimodal predictions agree with each other, and whether fusion reduces or amplifies uncertainty. The paper interprets these as proxies for modality-specific, redundant, and synergistic information. In practice, this is not a full partial-information decomposition; it is an operational approximation built from computable statistics such as divergence measures and entropy changes. Detection is framed as one-class classification trained only on benign examples, so the model learns a manifold of normal multimodal fusion and flags deviations at test time. Compared with prior defenses, the key difference is where the signal comes from: not input heuristics, not post-hoc output verification, but distributional behavior induced by multimodal integration. The trade-off is extra inference cost from multiple forward passes and a dependence on stable, comparable distributions across the three modes.21:T44d,The headline result is that FlowGuard reduces attack success rates from above 90% to below 15% across multiple MLLMs and unseen multimodal jailbreak settings, while keeping benign utility within 3% of the baseline and running about 6x faster than diffusion-based verification methods. The more informative part is the ablation story. Input- or output-level confidence features alone are clearly weaker than the FlowVector, which supports the claim that the anomaly lies in the fusion relation rather than in isolated uncertainty.
