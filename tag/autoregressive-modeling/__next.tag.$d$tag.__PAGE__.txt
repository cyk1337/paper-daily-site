9:T736,The method, NAPE, starts from a log-mel spectrogram, splits it into non-overlapping patches, and projects each patch into a latent embedding. This yields a 2D grid of patch embeddings. Since the backbone is a causal Transformer over a 1D sequence, the grid must be serialized with a scanning order. The model then predicts the embedding of the next patch from the prefix, using a lightweight predictor head. Training uses negative cosine similarity to a stop-gradient target embedding, rather than L2 regression or classification over discrete acoustic tokens.

The most interesting technical point is that scanning order is treated as a first-class design choice. In images, rasterization is often a convenience. In audio spectrograms, time and frequency axes play very different roles, so the serialization order changes both the available causal context and the inductive bias. The paper studies four scanning strategies and shows that this choice materially affects transfer performance and training behavior. The trade-off is intuitive: if the order destroys temporal continuity, the model no longer follows natural acoustic evolution; if it only marches along time, it may underuse local time-frequency structure. Making this axis explicit is one of the paper’s main contributions.

Architecturally, the setup is deliberately minimal: a single causal encoder plus a predictor head, with no teacher branch, no EMA target network, no reconstruction decoder, and no extra variance/covariance regularization. This is the key difference from much of prior audio SSL. The model is not trained to reconstruct masked content or to match a teacher representation. The model caches a representation from a deeper middle layer at token t-1 and injects it into an earlier middle layer at token t through a gated fusion pathway. This creates a continuous latent channel across decoding steps, but places that channel inside the part of the network where abstract computation is believed to happen.

Two design choices matter. First, the recurrent signal does not re-enter through the embedding layer. It is fused directly into the middle of the computation graph, so the previous token’s abstract state can continue evolving in the current token’s middle-layer processing. Second, recurrence is localized to a subset of middle layers rather than applied everywhere. The rationale is functional: early layers are more lexical/syntactic, late layers are closer to vocabulary projection, and middle layers are the best place to preserve reasoning state.

Architecturally, T^2MLR defines a write location and a read location. A hidden state from the write layer is cached, then consumed at the read layer of the next token. A gating module controls how much of the current token’s local representation should be mixed with the carried-over latent state. This is more flexible than a plain residual addition because the model can decide contextually when to rely on persistent latent state and when to ignore it.

The training story is a major part of the contribution. Exact temporal recurrence would introduce token-to-token dependencies under teacher forcing and break standard sequence-parallel training. The paper proposes an approximate temporal-parallel training scheme that preserves scalable parallelism while still training the recurrent pathway. This is the practical enabler: without it, the architecture would behave more like an RNN-style serial model and would be hard to pretrain at scale. The approximation introduces a train-time compromise between recurrence fidelity and throughput, and that trade-off is central to evaluating the method.

Relative to prior work, the distinctions are clean. Compared with latent-recurrent methods, recurrence is placed in the middle layers rather than at the input side. Compared with looped/depth-recurrent Transformers, extra computation is propagated across time instead of repeatedly re-running the full stack for one token, so inference overhead stays small. First, the model defines a continuous target for chain-of-thought. Given supervised examples with prompt q, explicit textual CoT d1:L, and answer x1:N, an encoder compresses the textual rationale into a continuous sequence e1:K of length K and dimension D. This e-space is optimized for semantic compression, but it is not necessarily easy to model autoregressively conditioned on the prompt.

Second, the paper introduces an LLM-facing thought space u1:K that is easier to sample and score causally. Instead of modeling e directly, NF-CoT maps e to u through an invertible transformation Fθ. In the u-space, the thought sequence follows a causal Gaussian density: each latent thought token is generated left-to-right with mean and variance conditioned on the prompt and previous thought tokens. Because the transformation is invertible, the exact density of the original continuous thought can be computed via the change-of-variables formula. This is the key design choice. The flow is not used merely as a generic density estimator; it bridges a semantically meaningful compressed space and an autoregressively tractable space.

Third, the model jointly generates latent thoughts and textual answers using a shared causal LLM backbone. On top of the backbone, one head models the continuous thought density and another head models the answer tokens. Training maximizes the joint likelihood of latent thoughts and answers. Since the thought likelihood is tractable, the same interface also supports policy-gradient optimization directly in the continuous reasoning space.

Relative to prior work, the distinctions are sharp. Compared with Coconut-style methods, NF-CoT is stochastic and likelihood-based rather than deterministic hidden-state rollout. Compared with diffusion-based latent reasoning, it supports one-pass left-to-right sampling instead of iterative denoising. Compared with two-stage VAE-plus-generator pipelines, it more tightly couples latent-thought generation with the causal LM backbone and answer generation.16:T65d,The evaluation is conducted on code reasoning and program synthesis benchmarks: MBPP, MBPP+, HumanEval, HumanEval+, and LiveCodeBench v6. NF-CoT is compared against explicit textual CoT and prior latent-reasoning baselines. The headline result is that NF-CoT improves pass rates while reducing intermediate reasoning cost, because the latent thought sequence K is much shorter than the token length of textual CoT.

The more informative results are in the analyses. One set of ablations studies the effect of latent thought length, showing a real trade-off between compression and generative ease rather than a monotonic benefit from longer latent traces. Another set examines likelihood-based sampling and reranking: because the latent reasoning path has an explicit probability model, the system can sample and score reasoning trajectories in a way that many latent-CoT methods cannot. The policy-gradient refinement experiments further show that the tractable density is operationally useful, not just mathematically elegant: continuous thought trajectories can be optimized with RL and yield additional gains.

The exact numbers matter less than the closed loop the paper demonstrates: explicit CoT supervision is compressed into continuous thoughts; those thoughts are modeled with exact likelihood; inference remains causal and one-pass; and the same latent space can be refined with policy gradients.
