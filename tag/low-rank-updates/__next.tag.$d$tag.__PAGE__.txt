a:T93b,The work has two layers: explanation and exploitation. On the explanation side, the authors start from a shared base model and compare RL and OPD through their parameter deltas, ΔW. They then run several analyses. First is norm-controlled scaling: keep the final update direction fixed and scale only its magnitude by a factor α, then evaluate models of the form W_base + αΔW. OPD delivers larger reasoning gains than RL at the same update norm, which implies that RL contains more task-weak components in its update. Second is module-level marginal utility analysis: they inspect sensitivity and accumulated update norm across layers and modules, asking where the model changes a lot without corresponding performance benefit. Their finding is not that RL and OPD care about completely different modules, but that RL spends much more update budget on low-utility regions, whereas OPD suppresses those regions early and concentrates on more reasoning-critical modules, especially in middle layers. They call this Functional Redundancy Avoidance. Third is spectral and subspace evolution analysis: they study singular value concentration of updates across checkpoints and measure alignment between early dominant subspaces and the final update subspace. OPD shows stronger low-rank concentration, earlier stabilization, and much earlier alignment with the final solution. RL remains more diffuse and keeps rotating its update directions later into training. Fourth is early-checkpoint recoverability: after module-wise norm scaling, an OPD checkpoint at roughly 10% of training recovers about 80% of the final reasoning performance. This is a strong empirical sign that the main structure of the final solution is already present very early.

On the exploitation side, they propose EffOPD. The idea is simple: if OPD directions are already close to the final direction early in training, then one can linearly extrapolate along the current update direction and reduce the number of optimization steps and samples. This is not a new architecture or loss; it is a plug-and-play acceleration strategy built on the observed trajectory stability. The important point is not linear extrapolation by itself, but the condition under which it works: the training path must be directionally stable enough. The earlier spectral analysis is used to justify that condition.b:T61b,The experiments span models from 1.5B to 32B parameters, including pretrained, SFT-tuned, and thinking-style models. RL baselines include PPO, GRPO, and DAPO. OPD uses pattern-aligned teachers, typically stronger models from the same family. The main empirical results are fourfold. First, under fixed update direction and matched update norm, OPD yields substantially larger reasoning gains than RL, supporting the claim that OPD updates are more task-relevant rather than merely smaller or larger. Second, module-level analysis shows that RL accumulates more redundant updates in low-marginal-utility modules, while OPD concentrates more on modules that matter for reasoning. Third, spectral analysis shows stronger low-rank concentration and earlier final-subspace alignment for OPD; RL updates are more dispersed and continue to drift later in training. Fourth, EffOPD delivers about 3× average training acceleration across multiple settings from 1.5B to 32B while maintaining final performance comparable to vanilla OPD. One especially important number appears early in the paper: an OPD checkpoint at only around 10% of training can recover roughly 80% of final reasoning performance after module-wise norm scaling. That is the clearest quantitative evidence for early directional lock-in.

When reading the full paper, it is worth checking how robust these findings are across tasks, teacher-student gaps, and RL variants.
