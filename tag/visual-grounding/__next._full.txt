18:T4cf,ExBind is built around a representation-independent latent binding space and deterministic compilation/evaluation:
1) Sample a latent binding instance containing entities, relations, hierarchy, attributes, ordered roles (e.g., directed edge endpoints), and a unique canonical target.
2) Compile the same latent instance into heterogeneous executable surfaces (SVG, DOM, canvas, tree, graph, table), each with its own surface-specific executable identifiers.
3) The model outputs only the reference required by the environment. The evaluator maps the prediction back into the latent space deterministically, then reports:
- validity (is the output a valid candidate under the declared vocabulary/format?)
- exact binding correctness (does it match the gold target?)
- which structural constraints are violated; for families with an ordered constraint decomposition, a concise first-violation diagnosis.
4) The benchmark explicitly distinguishes outcome stability from diagnostic-type stability via candidate-order perturbations.
The dataset is intentionally small to preserve identifiability: unique gold targets, deterministic mappings, structural-oracle recovery, and shortcut checks are prioritized over population-scale ranking.19:T481,Key observation: lower entropy of early-layer image–text attention correlates with better grounding coverage and higher VQA accuracy.
1) Image–text entropy: from layer k, obtain visual tokens and text tokens; compute a prompt-relevant distribution via temperature-scaled softmax over CLIP similarities, then compute Shannon entropy H_S = -\sum p\log(p+\epsilon). High entropy indicates dispersed attention, consistent with fragmented or noisy prompt-relevant regions.
2) ECS (Entropy-based Cropping Strategy, inference): evaluate a small set of candidate crops/aspect ratios; compute H_S using very early layers (k=1 in ECS) and pick the crop with minimal entropy; combine the selected crop with the original image as the final input.
3) ERT (Entropy Regularization Training, fine-tuning): add entropy as a regularizer to the standard next-token loss: L = L_NTP + \alpha H_S (k=2 in ERT). The paper argues this sharpens attention on relevant tokens (p_i > 1/e) while suppressing irrelevant ones (p_i < 1/e), loosely analogous to negative-sample suppression in contrastive learning. Implementation is LoRA-based with only ~0.14% trainable parameters.1a:T4ef,The method is deliberately lightweight and training-free. Instead of training a separate verifier, it reads how much each generated token attends back to visual tokens and uses that as a proxy for visual reference usage. The scoring has two levels. First, token-level calibration: each token is scored using both its generation likelihood and its visual lookback strength. This matters because plain likelihood over-rewards generic fluent tokens, while many visually decisive words are object, attribute, or relation tokens. Second, response-level aggregation: token scores are not simply averaged. They are aggregated under a visual relevance distribution that gives more weight to tokens with stronger visual reference usage. This separation is one of the paper’s better design choices. It distinguishes whether a token is visually supported from how much that token should matter for the final response score. Compared with prior approaches, LookBack avoids extra reward models, preference labels, and additional verification passes. Compared with pure confidence scoring, it explicitly injects image sensitivity. Compared with vision-only heuristics, it still preserves the model’s own uncertainty signal rather than replacing it with raw attention alone.1b:T61d,The method has two layers. The first is PriVE-Bench, a counterfactual benchmark built around prior-versus-evidence conflicts. Each example contains an original image and a counterfactual image. The original conforms to common expectations, while the counterfactual violates a canonical attribute, count, part, or modality expectation. The benchmark spans five domains: object existence, counting, fashion attributes, industry/common-object attributes, and medical modality consistency. Each item defines both a visually correct answer and a prior-consistent biased answer, so model outputs can be categorized as correct, biased, or other rather than only correct or incorrect.

The second layer is PriVE-Tools. Instead of evaluating a free-form visual agent, the paper converts common visual operations into controlled evidence conditions: bounding-box overlays, crops, zoom panels, and contour overlays. The key design choice is to keep the underlying image, question, correct answer, biased answer, and scoring rubric fixed while changing only the evidence presentation. That isolates whether the evidence view itself helps the model use visual information.

The evaluation protocol includes original-only, counterfactual-only, and paired-image inputs, and supports yes/no, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions. Metrics include accuracy, prior-following error rate, and other-response rate. That metric split is important because a drop in accuracy alone cannot tell whether the model is following a prior, becoming uncertain, or producing unrelated outputs.1c:T464,The main findings are clear even from the available full text. First, counterfactual inputs induce systematic prior-following errors rather than merely generic confusion. Many wrong answers land specifically on the prior-consistent biased option. Second, paired-image inputs sometimes help because they make the visual discrepancy easier to compare, but they do not reliably remove prior-following. Third, tool-derived evidence views are model-dependent: in the reported evaluations, closed-source VLMs benefit more consistently from crops and zoom panels, while several open-source models show weak or even negative aggregate effects.

The important interpretation is that the bottleneck is not only visual access. In many cases, the relevant evidence is made more explicit, yet the model still does not revise its answer. That suggests a failure in evidence weighting or decision integration, not just a failure of raw perception. The metric to watch is prior-following error rate, not just overall accuracy.1d:T4bf,Latent-VC inserts a small set of latent cache tokens into the decoder. The video is encoded by the frozen visual tower and visual merger into video-prefix tokens. Then S special <|lvc|> tokens are placed between the prompt and the answer. Their hidden states act as non-verbal visual memory rather than normal vocabulary tokens. The answer likelihood is still defined over discrete answer tokens, while the latent slots serve as deterministic recurrent computation states. Training has two stages. During SFT, a supervised contrastive cache-alignment objective projects cache states back into the visual space and aligns them with key-frame embeddings. This gives the cache a direct visual training signal and prevents it from becoming an empty placeholder. During RL, the paper uses vision-grounded GRPO with answer, format, temporal, and latent grounding rewards. The latent reward measures key-frame coverage over completion-token hidden states, encouraging the reasoning trajectory to remain visually grounded. A practical design choice is that the cache operates on native decoder hidden states, which keeps training and inference aligned and avoids the distribution shift seen in some latent-reasoning setups.1e:T842,The method, VEPA (Visual Evidence Pre-Alignment), factorizes answering into two steps. First, the MLLM generates textual visual evidence conditioned on the image and the question. Second, a frozen blind reader—an LLM that does not see the image—receives only the question and the generated evidence, and tries to recover the ground-truth answer. In probabilistic terms, the paper rewrites direct answering as a decomposition through a latent bottleneck e: an evidence policy P(e|v,q) followed by a reasoning policy P(a|e,q). The bottleneck is intentional. It forces the model to externalize the visual content that is actually needed for answering.

The optimization target is built around two properties. Sufficiency means the evidence should contain enough information for the blind reader to infer the answer. Visual dependence means the evidence must genuinely come from the image rather than from question priors or direct answer leakage. To train the evidence policy, the paper uses GRPO. For each sample, the model generates a group of candidate evidence sequences, and learning is driven by relative rewards within the group.

The reward design is the critical part. The paper does not simply reward the reader for getting the answer right. It adds constraints to prevent degenerate solutions: penalties against answer leakage, so the evidence cannot collapse into a disguised answer channel; penalties against repetitive or low-information evidence, so RL does not reward verbosity or templated outputs; and a decoupling between grounding and final answer generation, so the optimization pressure stays on evidence quality rather than downstream response style. This is the main difference from direct answer-level RL, where the model can still exploit shortcuts from language priors. Compared with replacing captions by dense descriptions, VEPA is also different in supervision structure: the evidence is question-conditioned, not a static attempt to describe the whole image. That makes it closer to actual inference-time information needs and more bandwidth-efficient than generic dense captioning.1f:T55f,From the available full-text content, the paper reports consistent gains over the standard training recipe across several visually demanding settings, including knowledge-intensive VQA, compositional VQA, fine-grained perception, and broader multimodal evaluation. The improvements are said to hold across different backbones, which matters because the claim is about training strategy rather than a backbone-specific trick.

The excerpt does not include the full result tables, model sizes, or dataset scales, so precise numbers should not be invented. Still, the experimental story is clear. First, the frozen blind reader is important because it turns answer supervision into a sufficiency test for evidence, instead of letting the same model self-validate.
