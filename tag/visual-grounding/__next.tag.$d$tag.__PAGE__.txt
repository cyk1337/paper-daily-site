9:T702,GroundBench introduces a six-condition benchmark with a branch-and-merge structure instead of a single monotonic prompt-strength chain. The conditions are: image_only, object_named, part_named, localization_only, localization_shown, and mechanics_shown. The critical design choice is that from object_named, the benchmark branches into part_named and localization_only. This lets the evaluator measure the effect of target identity and target region separately. The two branches merge again at localization_shown, and mechanics_shown adds a bundled mechanics descriptor containing joint type and motion axis.

This factorization matters because localization_only is not just a weaker version of localization_shown. It is the control that asks whether knowing where the relevant region is, without knowing what that part is called, helps at all. Conversely, part_named asks whether the semantic identity of the part is already enough to drive the answer even without explicit localization.

The paper also adds a counterfactual re-ask setup: the model is given a real but noncanonical part name from the same image and object, and the evaluator checks whether the output follows the changed part request or defaults to the canonical action. The authors are careful not to overclaim here. They frame it as a prompt-compliance test, not proof of visual attention.

A less flashy but important contribution is the evaluator design. The benchmark explicitly guards against two measurement errors found in the companion evaluation: fixed thresholds that let trivial constant baselines score surprisingly high, and incorrect action-label rules. GroundBench derives thresholds and baselines from the scored data rather than fixing them in advance, which makes the reported differences more trustworthy.a:T55f,The experimental scale is modest but targeted: 1,068 predictions across three OpenAI models. The main findings are clear.

First, localization_only, which provides the target region while withholding the part identity, gives essentially no gain over a constant baseline. This suggests that merely telling the model where to look is not enough if the model does not know what semantic part category that region corresponds to.

Second, the gains appear mainly in conditions that provide target-part identity, such as part_named and localization_shown. The authors’ interpretation is that the improvement comes from supplying a category variable that already determines the answer within the curated object set, rather than from stronger grounding or mechanical reasoning.

Third, adding the mechanics descriptor yields limited additional benefit, implying that joint type and motion axis are not the dominant bottleneck in this benchmark. The larger bottleneck remains part identity and its association with action labels.

The paper repeatedly contrasts these findings with the companion evaluation, where naming the target part improved action accuracy by 0.32 to 0.63 across eight VLMs and nearly recovered push-action performance. Instead of training a separate verifier, it reads how much each generated token attends back to visual tokens and uses that as a proxy for visual reference usage. The scoring has two levels. First, token-level calibration: each token is scored using both its generation likelihood and its visual lookback strength. This matters because plain likelihood over-rewards generic fluent tokens, while many visually decisive words are object, attribute, or relation tokens. Second, response-level aggregation: token scores are not simply averaged. They are aggregated under a visual relevance distribution that gives more weight to tokens with stronger visual reference usage. This separation is one of the paper’s better design choices. It distinguishes whether a token is visually supported from how much that token should matter for the final response score. Compared with prior approaches, LookBack avoids extra reward models, preference labels, and additional verification passes. Compared with pure confidence scoring, it explicitly injects image sensitivity. Compared with vision-only heuristics, it still preserves the model’s own uncertainty signal rather than replacing it with raw attention alone.e:T61d,The method has two layers. The first is PriVE-Bench, a counterfactual benchmark built around prior-versus-evidence conflicts. Each example contains an original image and a counterfactual image. The original conforms to common expectations, while the counterfactual violates a canonical attribute, count, part, or modality expectation. The benchmark spans five domains: object existence, counting, fashion attributes, industry/common-object attributes, and medical modality consistency. Each item defines both a visually correct answer and a prior-consistent biased answer, so model outputs can be categorized as correct, biased, or other rather than only correct or incorrect.

The second layer is PriVE-Tools. Instead of evaluating a free-form visual agent, the paper converts common visual operations into controlled evidence conditions: bounding-box overlays, crops, zoom panels, and contour overlays. The key design choice is to keep the underlying image, question, correct answer, biased answer, and scoring rubric fixed while changing only the evidence presentation. That isolates whether the evidence view itself helps the model use visual information.

The evaluation protocol includes original-only, counterfactual-only, and paired-image inputs, and supports yes/no, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions. Metrics include accuracy, prior-following error rate, and other-response rate. That metric split is important because a drop in accuracy alone cannot tell whether the model is following a prior, becoming uncertain, or producing unrelated outputs.f:T464,The main findings are clear even from the available full text. First, counterfactual inputs induce systematic prior-following errors rather than merely generic confusion. Many wrong answers land specifically on the prior-consistent biased option. Second, paired-image inputs sometimes help because they make the visual discrepancy easier to compare, but they do not reliably remove prior-following. Third, tool-derived evidence views are model-dependent: in the reported evaluations, closed-source VLMs benefit more consistently from crops and zoom panels, while several open-source models show weak or even negative aggregate effects.

The important interpretation is that the bottleneck is not only visual access. In many cases, the relevant evidence is made more explicit, yet the model still does not revise its answer. That suggests a failure in evidence weighting or decision integration, not just a failure of raw perception.
