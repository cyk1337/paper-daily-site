9:T53f,Localization-Head Alignment (LHA) has three key pieces:
1) Identify localization heads: within Qwen3.5-4B’s full-attention layers (128 heads), find a small subset that performs evidence selection. Causal importance is validated via head ablations (ablating four localization heads hurts much more than four random heads).
2) Supervise at fact tokens inside the chain: instead of supervising attention at the final answer, apply supervision at the token positions where the model states a visual fact (e.g., when predicting the object word in “the kite is blue”). This matches the observation that perception happens when facts are written, and that attention fades later.
3) Contrastive box-based attention loss: using grounding boxes, train those heads to attend more to the evidence region than to an equal-area background. The loss is formulated as an evidence-vs-background ratio so the softmax normalizer cancels, emphasizing where attention goes rather than how much total visual attention is used.
The same auxiliary loss is used in both SFT (on the model’s own correct trajectories) and GRPO-style outcome RL (on correct rollouts). A gradient decomposition is used to argue outcome rewards select trajectories, while the attention term provides a consistent routing gradient. Inference is unchanged (no extra zoom/crop passes).a:T568,Backbone: Qwen3.5-4B in thinking mode (32K token budget, temperature 1). Evaluation: 1,396 high-res fine-grained MCQs (V* 191, HR-Bench 4K 800, TreeBench 405). Answers are rule-parsed; an LLM judge (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) is used only when parsing fails, and judge decisions are manually audited.
Diagnostics:
- Evidence masking: masking the ground-truth region costs 22.1 points more than masking an equal-area distractor region; the smaller the evidence, the larger the drop.
- Localization heads are causal: ablating four localization heads causes an additional 13.2-point drop vs ablating four random heads; their evidence focus decays with longer reasoning.
Main results (as reported):
- LHA reaches 76.6% overall (Figure 1), the best single-pass Qwen3.5-4B variant in their comparison.
- Beats inference-time interventions including ViCrop (two extra passes) and outperforms the attention-supervised RL baseline EASE by 3.0 points.
- Controlled gains: on identical self-generated trajectories, alignment SFT improves +1.4 over CE-SFT; after identical outcome RL, the gap grows to +1.8 (95% CI [0.5, 2.9]). Adding the attention term during RL yields an additional +0.9.

EVAN estimates scaled image information gain (scaled-IIG) for each training instance, measuring how much the image contributes to answer correctness. Only visually dependent examples are selected for intervention, which avoids injecting noise into cases that can already be solved from language alone. To prevent the selected pool from collapsing into a narrow subset of tasks, the paper uses a mixed-ranking strategy that balances visual dependence with data diversity.

Second, evidence ablation. For each selected example, the method identifies query-critical image patches that support the verified response. These patches are then masked to create an evidence-ablated image. The key design choice is that the perturbation is not coarse or task-agnostic; it removes exactly the visual evidence that keeps the answer grounded. Sampling from the current policy on this ablated image tends to produce responses that remain fluent and plausible, but are now visually ungrounded. These become EVAN’s hard negatives.

Third, RL optimization. The negatives are not optimized under the ablated image. Instead, they are brought back under the clean image condition, so the model is explicitly trained to suppress language-prior failures when the relevant evidence is actually available. The training recipe includes several stabilization mechanisms: filtering out low-probability or overly long ablated responses, bounding negative advantages so negatives do not dominate the update, and combining the method with DAPO-style clipping and dynamic sampling for stable long-form reasoning optimization. Compared with prior multimodal RL methods that use image perturbations, the main difference is that EVAN treats perturbation as a tool for constructing informative negatives, not as the end objective.c:T5fe,The experimental story is not just about end-task accuracy; it is also about grounding behavior. On a set of challenging multimodal reasoning benchmarks, EVAN improves over standard RLVR/GRPO baselines, with larger gains on subsets where visual evidence is more necessary. That pattern matches the method design: it is meant to correct language shortcuts, not to uniformly help every example.

The ablations are the most informative part. Removing scaled-IIG-based selection and applying evidence ablation to all samples hurts performance, showing that visually dependent example selection is necessary. Replacing query-critical masking with random masking or coarse perturbations produces weaker negatives and worse downstream gains, which supports the claim that negative quality matters more than perturbation quantity. The stability ablations are also important: without plausibility filtering or bounded negative advantages, training becomes less stable, indicating that the method is not simply “add negative rewards and hope for the best.”
