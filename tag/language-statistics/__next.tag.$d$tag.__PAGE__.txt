5:T765,The main idea is to disentangle context-length effects from order effects. For each target position, the authors construct two reveal conditions. In the intact condition, the preceding context is revealed in its original order. In the scrambled baseline, the same revealed span is permuted, preserving the word inventory and much of the topical signal while destroying sequential arrangement. A pretrained autoregressive transformer then provides conditional probabilities, or equivalently token-level surprisal, for the target span. By extending the revealed boundary farther back, they measure the incremental predictive gain contributed by each distance range. The difference between intact and scrambled gains defines the order-specific marginal benefit, and its dependence on distance d is the CPF, P(d).

Several design choices matter. They measure incremental gain rather than only comparing full versus truncated context, which localizes contribution by distance. The baseline uses permutations of the true words rather than random replacements, so lexical content and topic recurrence are controlled as much as possible while order is removed. The LLM is treated as a probe of structure in natural language, not as the object of analysis. The paper also includes controls with random-token sequences, synthetic corpora, partial order corruption, and analyses of whether the effect is concentrated in a few anchor tokens or broadly distributed.

Relative to Shannon-style context-length studies or Hilberg-type arguments, the novelty is the counterfactual baseline. Earlier work measured the total value of longer context but could not separate lexical availability from arrangement. Mutual information studies pairwise dependence, not dependence on ordered configurations. The contribution here is therefore a measurement design for sequence arrangement as its own statistical object.6:T523,The study covers ten written and spoken corpora spanning six language families. The headline result is that CPF follows an approximate power law, P(d) ∝ d^-α, over nearly two orders of magnitude in distance, with mean α = 1.04, standard deviation 0.15, and median r^2 = 0.96. The concentration of the exponent near 1 is central to the claim that this is a reproducible law rather than a loose curve fit.

The control experiments are important. The effect largely disappears on scrambled or random-token controls and on synthetic corpora, which argues against the pattern being a trivial artifact of the probe. When sequential integrity is progressively degraded, CPF weakens accordingly, supporting the interpretation that it is measuring order-specific signal rather than simple lexical recurrence. The paper also reports that the contribution is broadly distributed across prior sentences rather than being dominated by a few distant anchor tokens.

The experimental strength is not a model-size sweep or benchmark gains. It comes from replication across corpora and from careful controls that rule out obvious confounds. For a pretraining reader, the most informative parts are the ablations around the scrambled baseline, synthetic controls, partial corruption, and sentence-level contribution
