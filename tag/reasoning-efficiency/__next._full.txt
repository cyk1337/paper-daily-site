19:T453,The authors define a unified reward space that separates brevity incentives for correct rollouts from length penalties for incorrect rollouts. Correctness is denoted by c, CoT length by ℓ, α controls the brevity bonus reserved for correct answers, β controls the length penalty on incorrect answers, and a discrete format reward checks required tags such as <think> and boxed answers. By varying α, β, boundedness, adaptivity, and the shape of the length signal, they evaluate 11 reward configurations. The mechanism analysis focuses on GRPO group normalization: rewards from multiple rollouts for the same prompt are converted into relative advantages. With continuous length penalties on wrong answers, a short incorrect response can receive a better relative advantage than a longer incorrect response, and this can gradually reinforce under-thinking. Acoer addresses this by applying efficiency pressure only to correct rollouts, normalizing the budget adaptively as the model becomes more concise, and using a feedback control schedule that reduces pressure when accuracy preservation is at risk.1a:T78c,ThoughtFold combines standard RLVR with introspective preference learning. The starting point is still an outcome-correct chain of thought, but the paper no longer treats the whole successful trajectory as a uniformly positive sample. Instead, it analyzes the deletability of reasoning segments.

The chain of thought is first segmented into discrete reasoning steps using predefined rules. Then, starting from a correct trajectory, the method iteratively removes selected segments and checks whether the model can still arrive at the correct answer. This produces a spectrum of variants: some remain correct after deletion, suggesting that the removed segment was redundant; others fail, indicating that the deleted part carried essential logic. The process is called introspective redundancy identification because it does not rely on external step-level annotation. It uses the model’s own ability to recover the correct answer after deletion as evidence about which parts matter.

Once such trajectory variants are collected, ThoughtFold performs fine-grained preference optimization. The key is not a whole-sequence preference of A over B, but a mask-based local preference signal: identified redundant spans are penalized, while the model is encouraged to bridge directly between the surrounding essential reasoning segments. This is the “folding” operation. Compared with simple length penalties, the method tries to preserve the reasoning skeleton while compressing unproductive exploration in the middle.

From the paper’s description and figure, two supervision signals are meant to work together. Outcome-based learning keeps answer accuracy high, while fine-grained preference learning changes the internal structure of the trajectory, reducing repetition, hesitation, and off-target exploration. In that sense, ThoughtFold is not a replacement for RLVR, but an additional structural signal layered on top of it.1b:T628,The experiments cover reasoning models from the Qwen3 and DeepSeek families, with benchmarks ranging from GSM8K to AIME and GPQA. The main evaluation target is the joint trade-off between accuracy and token efficiency. The clearest number stated in the abstract is that on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, average token consumption is reduced by about 56% while maintaining strong accuracy, outperforming recent efficient-reasoning baselines. That is large enough to suggest a real change in trajectory behavior rather than a minor trimming effect.

More important than the headline number is the paper’s claim about why the trajectories become shorter. The authors argue that ThoughtFold does not merely reshape the output-length distribution; it induces a more concentrated reasoning mode with less structural redundancy. A deeper read should concentrate on the ablations and failure cases to judge whether the method is likely to transfer to stronger reasoning models.1d:T7b6,DUET is a dual-model, two-stage inference framework. Given an input x, a capable large model M first produces an intermediate representation z, and a lightweight model m then generates the final answer y conditioned on both x and z. In the main instantiation, z is a reasoning signal: the large model handles the hard cognitive work, while the small model performs cheaper answer generation.

The key point is that DUET is not just a sequential pipeline. The paper formulates training as a constrained optimization problem: maximize expected reward while keeping the expected length of z below a budget B. This directly ties collaboration quality to inference cost. Training uses a length-penalized joint objective with alternating optimization over the two models.

Two ingredients appear central. First, the marginal-utility reward gives positive incentive to the large model only when its intermediate output is likely to improve outcomes beyond what the small model could do alone. This discourages the large model from emitting long generic chains of thought and pushes z toward concise, decision-relevant information. In other words, the objective rewards useful compression, not verbosity.

Second, the adaptive length-penalty schedule stabilizes training under a token budget. A fixed penalty can be too weak, leading to long z, or too strong, collapsing the communication channel before the models learn to cooperate. The adaptive schedule gradually tightens the budget while preserving learnability.

Relative to standard CoT distillation, DUET is different in an important way: the small model is not trained to imitate the full reasoning trace of the large model. Instead, the large model learns to emit a shorter communication signal that is sufficient for the small model to finish the task. It is also different from speculative decoding or verifier-proposer setups, because the two models are not playing a draft-check role; they are dividing semantic labor.1e:T67f,The evaluation focuses on difficult reasoning benchmarks such as AIME and GPQA. The main reported result is that DUET matches the large-model baseline performance while reducing expensive large-model token usage by up to about 60%. The gain comes from replacing long large-model reasoning traces with a shorter large-model signal plus cheaper small-model completion.

The motivation is empirically grounded: reasoning models often generate much longer outputs than similarly sized instruct models, with examples in the paper citing 5–10x longer responses on AIME-like tasks. DUET is designed to exploit that redundancy. The most relevant experiments are likely comparisons against the standalone large model, the standalone small model, and simpler compression or short-CoT baselines, along with cost-performance tradeoff curves under different length penalties and ablations on the reward design.

Still, the experimental message is clear: the method is not trying to raise the capability ceiling, but to reallocate reasoning compute more efficiently on hard tasks.

A practical caveat remains important. Real deployment gains depend not only on token savings but also on the overhead of running two models serially and on how tightly the length of z is controlled. If z stays long or the orchestration cost is high, wall-clock savings may fall short of token-level savings.
