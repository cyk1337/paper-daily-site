9:T772,The paper has two main technical moves. First, it cleanly separates the quantities being measured. It defines harmful intent H, wrapper presence W, and realized harmful output Ym under a fixed judge, then evaluates ranking, calibration, and threshold transfer separately. This matters because many prior discussions collapse these notions into a single “harmfulness score”.

Second, it introduces Active Attention Probing (AAP) to remove a confound in attention-based detectors. Passive detectors often read from prompt-dependent locations. A jailbreak wrapper changes not only the content but also the coordinate from which the signal is extracted, making score changes hard to interpret. AAP inserts a short learned probe embedding into the system region after chat templating, creating a fixed, content-independent reference point. The model’s attention response at that probe is collected across selected heads and layers, concatenated into a feature vector, and mapped to a scalar score with XGBoost.

The key idea is not that the probe token is intrinsically better, but that it stabilizes the readout coordinate so plain and wrapped prompts can be compared at a common location. The primary probe uses K=3 rows, is trained only on the BeaverTails training split, and then frozen for all audits. The authors explicitly avoid selecting probes, heads, or readouts on jailbreak benchmarks such as JailbreakHub, WildJailbreak, or JailbreakBench. They also test multiple alternative channels—rare token, common token, norm-matched token, system-span, passive attention, and refusal-logit channels—to show that the main conclusion is not an artifact of one specific readout family. An outcome-supervised positive control further shows that the model does contain predictive signal for success labels; the failure comes from label mismatch, not from a total absence of useful internal information.a:T4a2,The central empirical result is the divergence between harmfulness and realized success. On Llama targets, adding a wrapper increases the harmful generation rate from 0.05 to 0.27, so the wrapper makes attacks more successful. At the same time, harmful-intent AUROC drops from 0.936 to 0.803. More strikingly, within wrapped harmful prompts, the score ranks successful attacks below failed ones, yielding an outcome AUROC of 0.220. This is not merely weak prediction; it is active anti-ranking.

The paper also reports that this pattern persists across measurement channels, target models, attack families, and independent judges. That makes the result harder to dismiss as a probe-specific artifact. The deployment analysis is also important: under a fixed benign false-positive budget, a threshold tuned on harmfulness spends blocking capacity on attacks that would have failed anyway, while letting more successful wrapped attacks pass.
