18:T688,The paper operationalizes “conceptual change” as four executable transformations (L1–L4) and, for each, distinguishes:
- a descriptive test: checks the defining property (closure, structural agreement, enrichment, etc.), and
- a consequence test: checks an independently specified implication that gives the claim meaning.

L1 (redundancy identification): separates gauge redundancies from genuine conserved symmetries using consequences on observables.
L2 (coarse-graining / reduction): shows closure alone is insufficient via the degenerate reduction Q(x)=0, which is perfectly closed but carries no dynamical variation; adds an observable-preservation consequence test.
L3 (structure transfer): validates transferred structures (e.g., conservation laws) by requiring they produce correct consequences in the target system; uses broken-symmetry controls.
L4 (missing-quantity inference from conservation imbalance): infers hidden quantities and requires the inferred imbalance to exceed noise scales.

It then ports the same logic to neural interpretability:
- CKA: because CKA is invariant to rotations, a random rotation can preserve CKA while breaking the claimed correspondence; the consequence test is functional-effect transfer under localized interventions.
- SAE features: concept enrichment can reject null features, but accepted features may lack causal, concept-specific support under ablations; matched controls and positive controls calibrate both enrichment and intervention sensitivity.

Three rates quantify failure modes: NAR (null acceptance), PAR (positive acceptance), and CAR (agreement between descriptive acceptance and consequence support).19:T454,Key quantitative findings emphasize how descriptive criteria can be gamed and how consequence tests repair the gap:
- Dynamical reduction on the dysts chaotic-systems benchmark: the constant reduction Q(x)=0 passes closure on 124/129 systems (~96%), despite preserving no variation; an observable-preservation test rejects it across the board.
- Constructed slow–fast systems: the combined criteria accept 24/24 known true reductions and reject 72 null candidates.
- L1: distinguishes four conserved symmetries from a gauge redundancy.
- L3: validates six genuine transfers while rejecting six broken-symmetry controls.
- L4: recovers a missing charge of 1.192 vs a withheld value of 1.200.
- Neural interpretability: demonstrations on Pythia LMs and ESM-2 protein LMs where CKA-preserving rotations break correspondence, and intervention-based consequence tests separate genuine from null mappings; for SAEs, enrichment rejects nulls but only a subset of accepted features receive concept-specific causal support under independent ablations, with matched/positive controls characterizing test sensitivity.1a:T42e,The method is a parallel agentic search loop. Human scientists provide a high-level prompt asking Claude to search DNA sequence databases for interesting reverse transcriptase examples. Claude reads the relevant literature, reproduces established results on public data to check its methods, searches protein families and genomic neighborhoods for systems that do not match known categories, and writes short reports for candidates with proposed functions and supporting evidence. Follow-up Claude sessions critique the evidence and eliminate many weak candidates. Human scientists then review the survivors and test them in the lab. The important design choice is not asking the model for a direct answer. The workflow decomposes discovery into literature calibration, database search, anomaly detection, candidate reporting, self-critique, expert filtering, and biochemical follow-up. The team also studies the model’s generated hypotheses as data, comparing candidates that experts keep against those they discard, then feeding those lessons back into instructions.1b:T496,All core experiments are built on LLM-SRBench-style equation discovery. Under matched budgets, parent-conditioned evolution achieves a median out-of-distribution NMSE of 0.045, statistically indistinguishable from fresh independent sampling at 0.049, with identical strict-solve counts. Explicit multi-parent crossover degrades OOD NMSE to 0.200 at 98.7% proposal validity, ruling out parsing failure as an explanation. Initial independent-sample quality predicts final run success with an AUC of 0.959, and useful components show only weak heritability. Three distinct iteration schemes fail to reliably add solved problems across four generations; the naive scheme drops strict solves from 50 to 38. On 717 cells with identical dictionaries and zero additional LLM calls, three joint set-level selectors solve 165–169 problems, while per-term reduction methods (marginal credit, stability frequency) solve only 78 and 74, with p < 10^-37. On the official 239-problem LLM-SRBench split, PTB-Search reaches 73.2% Acc@0.1 with Llama-3.1-8B and 77.0% with a single-seed DeepSeek-V4 anchor, versus 49.2% for the best prior baseline, at one tenth the standardized call budget.1c:T448,This is not a pretraining paper in the narrow sense, so its value for foundation-model researchers is indirect. The main contribution is a concrete design pattern for turning language models into more reliable open-ended discovery agents: persistent memory, executable hypotheses, external statistical validation, and reflection over accumulated claims. Three aspects matter. First, it separates propose, evaluate, and reflect as distinct functions rather than treating the whole loop as prompt chaining. Second, it insists that discoveries must be grounded in executable code and statistical tests, which is a useful reminder that in open-ended research settings the LLM should generate candidate analyses, not serve as the evidence source. Third, the new benchmark setup, especially iNatDisco, is relevant for evaluating whether a system is actually exploring and rediscovering latent structure rather than merely paraphrasing familiar patterns. For a pretraining-oriented reader, this is better viewed as an agentic discovery paper with implications for memory, tool use, and test-time search.1d:T73b,The central move is to formalize open-ended discovery as a stateful Propose-Evaluate-Reflect loop. A hypothesis is not a text statement but an executable program over the dataset X. The program returns a binary judgment, supported or rejected, together with statistical evidence. Only hypotheses that pass significance and effect-size thresholds are promoted into a persistent claim store C_t. Rejected hypotheses are also retained. This design constrains the LLM to hypothesis generation and analysis scripting, while outsourcing factual adjudication to statistical procedures.

The main novelty is the Reflect stage. Instead of simply appending prior findings into the next prompt, DiscoPER periodically analyzes the accepted and rejected claim sets to produce structured guidance G_t. The paper highlights two kinds of reflective signals: under-explored variables and recurring confounds across multiple discovered relationships. That guidance then conditions the next round of hypothesis generation. In scientific terms, the system tries to do two things humans often do: fill obvious gaps in coverage, and infer higher-order structure from repeated local findings.

The framework is also multimodal and code-based. Compared with classical structure-learning methods, it supports a much broader hypothesis space than pairwise relations or fixed graph templates. Compared with AI-scientist systems that require seed questions, it aims for question-free exploration.
