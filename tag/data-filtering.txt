19:T479,OpenVisTool defines an “instructive” trajectory as satisfying two constraints jointly:
- Outcome validity: the final answer passes the task evaluator.
- Causal utility: holding (image I, query q) fixed, compare a probe/base model’s success rate with the recorded tool calls+observations versus a no-tool baseline. Keep the trajectory only if tool conditioning improves reliability.
The pipeline operationalizes this via three stages:
1) Difficulty screening: remove items that a strong model can solve reliably without tools, reducing prompt-induced but unnecessary tool calls.
2) Domain-specific trajectory synthesis: use structured invocation strategies per domain (charts, tables, GUI grounding, visual search, web-to-HTML) to avoid arbitrary traces.
3) Supervision verification: enforce both validity and utility; the latter is implemented as an explicit counterfactual performance delta rather than a subjective “reasoning-consistency” judge.
Key design choice: use a reproducible, model-based counterfactual test instead of relying on human/judge plausibility, aligning filtering with whether tool evidence changes answerability.1a:T710,The benchmark has two tracks. In the Data Construction Track, a method starts from raw domain sources and outputs supervised fine-tuning data. The resulting dataset is then used to fine-tune a shared base model jointly with Dolly-15k, and the adapted model is evaluated on held-out domain benchmarks. This design serves two purposes: it removes confounds from changing base models or training recipes, and it reflects a realistic setup where generic instruction-following data is supplemented with domain-specific supervision. The released baseline, Data-Construction-Skill, is a skill-guided agent rather than a one-shot prompt. It decomposes construction into reusable components such as output schemas, filtering rules, coverage constraints, and validation utilities. The point is not the agent wrapper itself, but the explicit structuring of the data-generation pipeline to reduce uncontrolled drift in format and quality.

In the Data Quality Evaluation Track, the input is a pool of candidate training datasets. A scoring function assigns each dataset a scalar score, and the benchmark measures how well those scores linearly predict the actual downstream performance of models fine-tuned on the same datasets. The authors release both the candidate pools and the ground-truth performance records, which makes offline comparison possible. Their baseline metric is Distributional Alignment Score (DAS), a distribution-based evaluator built from Maximum Mean Discrepancy between a candidate dataset and a domain proxy. The underlying stance is clear: a data-quality metric is only useful if it predicts post-training utility reliably. A key strength of the benchmark is that it places construction and evaluation inside the same downstream-grounded protocol instead of treating them as separate problems.1b:T5fa,Three findings stand out. First, the negative result: across multiple domains, base models, and construction families including DataFlow-based, direct-LLM, and agent-based generators, adding synthetic domain data on top of Dolly-15k often underperforms the Dolly-only baseline. Since the training setup is otherwise shared, the regressions can be attributed directly to the added synthetic data rather than to recipe differences. Second, there is no universal winner among construction methods. DataFlow-Skill performs best in more structured domains such as Finance and Law; agent-based methods do better in reasoning-heavy domains such as Math and Medical; the authors’ Data-Construction-Skill is strongest in knowledge-extraction-heavy settings and improves the Dolly-only baseline by nearly 20 absolute points on Llama-3.1-8B Finance while matching the strongest competing methods. Science and parts of Law remain open. Third, DAS is the most reliable quality-evaluation metric overall. It achieves the strongest average correlation with downstream performance in four of six domains and is the only metric that clears r > 0.70 simultaneously in Math, Science, and Medical. By contrast, many quality- or diversity-based metrics are either narrow specialists or even sign-inconsistent across domain-model cells. Finance and Law remain difficult for all tested metrics. When reading the results, pay special attention to the Dolly-only versus Dolly-plus-synthetic comparisons and to the correlation tables for quality metrics.1c:T470,The experimental design scales down Common Crawl and its filtered variants while preserving their relative sizes, then varies model size and training steps over these pools. Increasing steps induces data repetition; increasing model size raises compute per step. This setup directly probes the tradeoff introduced by filtering: it improves average document quality but reduces the available token pool. The authors compare the best performance achievable under different compute budgets rather than relying only on fixed-token or fixed-step comparisons. They then fit empirical relationships among pool size, number of training steps, and model size to predict when no filtering should become optimal. A second set of experiments injects deliberately degraded data, including random strings and documents with shuffled word order, to test whether large models merely tolerate noise or can still extract weak signals from it. A post-hoc theoretical model explains the observed pattern: cleaning helps imperfect, capacity-limited learners, while sufficiently capable learners can benefit from modeling the noisy generation process itself.1d:T51d,The key scale numbers are informative. A heavily filtered dataset such as DCLM-Baseline keeps roughly 1% of the original Common Crawl, yielding about 3.8T tokens, while the DCLM-Pool scale is around 240T tokens. The study spans about two orders of magnitude in compute, and the same qualitative trend persists as the pool scale increases: once the model is large enough and trained long enough, the full pool overtakes filtered pools. The fitted scaling law suggests that the 240T-token Common Crawl pool may become optimal around 1e30 FLOPs. Filtering still wins in lower-compute regimes, which explains why earlier filtering studies saw strong benefits. The noisy-data experiments add a useful boundary case. Random strings hurt at low compute, but sufficiently trained large models narrow the gap. Shuffled-word documents are more revealing: they destroy syntax and local semantics while preserving unigram statistics, yet large models can still benefit from them. This indicates that useful signal is not limited to human-defined high-quality prose; token frequencies, entity coverage, topical diversity, and long-tail lexical statistics also matter. The authors also state an important limitation: adversarial, non-factual, or safety-critical data that resembles high-quality text could still be harmful.1e:T419,The proposed method, SQSD, assigns a continuous safety-degradation risk score to each training example. The method first defines two reference directions in parameter space: a safety direction and a danger direction. Following the task-vector idea, both are constructed as displacements from the same base model. The safety direction is obtained from a DPO-aligned model trained on PKU-SafeRLHF-10K, while the danger direction is obtained from a model fine-tuned toward harmful behavior using datasets such as Aegis and BeaverTails. For each training example, the induced parameter update is projected onto these two directions. A sample is considered riskier if its update aligns more with the danger direction and less with the safety direction. This differs from perturbation-magnitude analysis: two updates with the same norm can have different behavioral consequences if their directions differ. The paper also provides a first-order Taylor approximation that links the SQSD score to preference differences between safe and unsafe model states.1f:T492,The paper reports from-scratch training across several model sizes and token budgets. The central finding is that multi-epoch training on high-quality filtered data outperforms a single pass over a larger and more diverse lightly filtered corpus at the same pretraining budget. Stricter filtering increases average document length; the Dense Core subset has documents nearly 50% longer than the random baseline, indicating a shift toward longer-form substantive content. Tokenizer analysis shows that training the tokenizer on higher-quality filtered data improves fertility both on the corresponding training split and on shared held-out FW2-DE data, as well as benchmark prompts. The authors also report positive downstream effects after instruction tuning, with higher correctness in assistant-style tasks. They additionally clean noisy German translations of ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, LAMBADA, and OpenBookQA, and release German small language models up to 1B parameters trained with far fewer tokens than comparable baselines. Exact score tables and scaling curves should be checked in the experimental section, since the provided text does not include all numbers.20:T62d,The method consists of three translation stages and a diagnosis-repair loop. T1 maps the natural-language input x to a formal expression y_orig. T2 back-translates y_orig into natural language x'. T3 re-formalizes x' into y_rt. The system then checks whether y_orig and y_rt are equivalent under the target formalism Σ. In the instantiated setup, Σ is many-sorted first-order logic and equivalence is checked with an SMT solver.

The key idea is to treat roundtrip consistency as a semantic preservation test. If all three stages preserve meaning, the original and roundtrip formalizations should be equivalent. The authors are careful about the limitation: equivalence is necessary but not sufficient. The pipeline may converge to a wrong but self-consistent fixed point where both formalizations agree while still misrepresenting the original text. To address that, they add an independent NLI-based analysis to test whether equivalence-passing cases indeed show lower semantic drift.

When equivalence fails, an LLM judge inspects the full tuple (x, y_orig, x', y_rt) and predicts which stage is responsible. Repair is then scoped to that stage only.
