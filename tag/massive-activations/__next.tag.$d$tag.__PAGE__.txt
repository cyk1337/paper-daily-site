5:T5fd,The main contribution is Ledger Residuals. A standard pre-norm transformer has a single width-d residual stream that every sublayer reads from and writes to. Ledger Residuals factor this into two width-d streams. The Deliberation stream D is a scratchpad: sublayers can write to it freely and can also erase stale content. The Commitment stream C is an append-only ledger: sublayers may commit information into it through a gate, but cannot overwrite what is already there, and the unembedding reads only from C. Initialization is D0 = embed(x) and C0 = 0. Each sublayer produces both an update to D and a gated contribution to C. The design is intentionally asymmetric: D is where computation happens, C is where the model stores the representation it will be judged on. Importantly, the architecture reduces to a standard pre-norm transformer at a known gate setting, so it serves as a controlled instrument rather than a completely unrelated model family. The causal test is straightforward. If massive activations are just a workaround for the residual stream’s dual role, then a protected decode-only channel should make them unnecessary. The authors also add a commit-sparsity penalty to encourage selective use of C, hoping to keep the commitment channel clean. Instead, stronger sparsity pressure makes the rebuilt outlier more persistent and more concentrated. That is the key technical result: the model actively recreates the feature in the readout channel rather than passively inheriting it from a flawed architecture.6:T523,The experiments are modest in scale but well controlled. The authors train language models at 160M and 290M parameters and compare Ledger Residuals against standard transformers at matched loss, so the mechanistic conclusions are not confounded by large performance gaps. Three findings matter most. First, a protected decode-only commitment channel does not remove massive activations. The model rebuilds the canonical fixed-dimension outlier in C, and it remains strongly concentrated on the sequence-start token. Second, the rebuilt outlier is somewhat smaller in magnitude than in a standard transformer, but it is more sharply localized in both dimension and position. So the phenomenon is not gone; it is reorganized under the new architectural constraints. Third, the obvious remedy backfires. With stronger commit-sparsity regularization, dominant-dimension persistence rises from 0.58 to 0.96, and start-token concentration rises from 3.4 to 4.7. In other words, the model does not reduce reliance on the outlier channel; it makes that channel more stable and more focused. The paper emphasizes structural measurements rather than benchmark scores: activation magnitude distributions, persistence of the dominant dimension across layers, and concentration on the start token under matched-loss comparisons.7:T45d,For a skim, start with the Introduction to understand the two competing hypotheses: artifact versus function. Then go straight to Section 3 and Figure 1, which explain the Deliberation/Commitment split and why it is a meaningful controlled intervention. After that, read Section 5 carefully. The most informative plots are the ones showing activation magnitude, dominant-dimension persistence, and start-token concentration. The ablation with commit-sparsity penalty is especially important because it shows that making the commitment channel cleaner does not remove the outlier; it strengthens it. If you only have about 25 minutes, read the Introduction, Section 3, Section 5, and the conclusion. If you want more depth, compare this paper mentally with prior approaches such as key/value biasing, QK normalization, and gated softmax variants. Those works show that outliers can sometimes be suppressed without immediate loss collapse. This paper tests a stronger claim: when the architecture is changed to provide a dedicated readout channel, does the model still choose to recreate the outlier? The answer is yes.8:T826,The analysis proceeds in three stages. The first is channel disruption. In MMDiT architectures, the authors examine both the image stream and the encoder stream across layers and denoising steps, identify persistent top-k massive channels by activation magnitude, and then perform controlled interventions. They zero out the massive channels and compare the effect on generation quality against zeroing an equally sized set of low-statistic channels. This moves the analysis from correlation to functional necessity.

The second stage studies spatial structure. Because image-stream tokens preserve the spatial layout of the latent image, the authors restrict the representation to the top-k massive channels and inspect the resulting spatial response patterns. Even with only a few channels retained, the activations align with semantically meaningful regions such as foreground objects, background, and object boundaries. Clustering and segmentation-style evaluations are then used to show that these channels induce a representation in which semantic regions are more separable.

The third stage is activation transport, an intervention-based test. The authors extract top-k massive activations from one prompt-conditioned denoising trajectory and transplant them, under masking constraints, into another trajectory. They test both text-conditioned semantic transport and image-conditioned transport. In the first case, semantic content from one prompt is injected into another generation. In the second, subject-specific activations from a reference image are re-injected into a new generation. No additional training is required. The mechanism relies on the observation that these channels provide a relatively stable carrier of semantic information across denoising steps.

Compared with prior work that focuses only on attention maps or passive probing, this paper emphasizes a stronger triad: the channels can be disrupted, localized, and transplanted. On that basis, the authors interpret massive activations as a sparse semantic transport subspace rather than simple outliers.9:T645,The experiments cover five modern DiT or MMDiT families, including FLUX, FLUX-2, SANA, and Qwen-Image, with the goal of showing that the phenomenon is not model-specific. The first result category is the disruption probe: zeroing the top-k massive channels causes a sharp degradation in generation quality, whereas zeroing the same number of low-statistic channels has much smaller effect. This supports the claim that massive channels are functionally important.

The second category is spatial organization. Using only the massive channels, the authors recover image-stream activation patterns that align with semantic regions. They further validate this on generated images from GenAI-Bench with segmentation-oriented evaluation, suggesting that these channels are not limited to local texture synthesis but are tied to object-level semantic organization.

The third category is intervention. Through masked activation transport, the paper achieves localized semantic composition without training a dedicated editing model, and reports performance on DreamBench++ that is competitive with specialized editing backbones. The strongest part of the empirical case is that three forms of evidence point in the same direction: removing massive channels hurts, isolating them preserves semantic structure, and transplanting them induces controlled semantic change.
