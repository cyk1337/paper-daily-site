1a:T4be,The approach adapts neuroscience-style functional localizers and network recruitment analyses to LLM circuits:
- Task suite: 46 tasks spanning four domains, used to elicit domain-consistent activation patterns rather than to optimize a single benchmark.
- Module identification: contrastive analyses over internal representations (e.g., residual stream components, MLP/attention outputs, or discovered directions) to find units/subnetworks selectively engaged by a domain.
- Segregation tests: quantify overlap vs separation between domain-selective sets, checking whether domain modules are approximately distinct and consistent across tasks/prompts.
- Causal tests (if included in the full paper): ablation/patching/activation interventions to show domain-specific performance dependence, moving from correlation to causation.
- Brain analogy: map discovered LLM modules onto coarse human networks (language, Multiple Demand, Theory of Mind, intuitive physics) to argue convergent functional decomposition.
Key difference from prior ‘feature neuron’ work is the attempt to characterize domain-level functional organization with a unified task-contrast methodology and explicit overlap/segregation evaluation.1b:T41a,The study builds a routing-mass atlas and pre-registers six expert families before interventions: math, code, general, Arabic, Chinese, and Spanish. Each family consists of the top 16 experts by lift, excluding always-on shared experts. The intervention is router masking with gate renormalization: masked experts have their router logits set to −∞ before the top-k softmax, and a smoke test verifies zero routing leak. A family is counted as modular only if its on-target degradation exceeds a size-matched random-expert null, estimated as mean plus two standard deviations, and its worst off-target effect is at most one third of the on-target effect. The paper reports both the pre-registered point rule and a conservative bootstrap 95% confidence-interval rule. The same interventions are evaluated with task accuracy, problem-text likelihood, solution likelihood, and per-language likelihood on both FLoRes/Belebele passages and an independent Wikipedia corpus. Accuracy slices are small, with MATH-500 n=25, HumanEval n=60, and MMLU-Pro n=80, giving binomial standard errors around 0.03–0.10; this is why the likelihood-based and bootstrap analyses carry much of the evidential weight.1d:T981,方法核心是把 document 作为路由共享单位，而不是让每个 token 在全体 expert 上自由选择。具体做法是：模型总共有很多 expert，例如 128 个，总激活数 top-k 为 8；但对同一篇 document，router 先为它选出一个共享 expert pool，例如 32 个 expert，文档内所有 token 只能在这 32 个里再做 top-k 选择。这样做的目的不是硬编码“这篇文档属于某个领域”，而是利用一个较弱但普遍成立的假设：同一文档中的 token 往往在语义上更一致，因此应该依赖相近的 expert 子集。

这个设计和标准 MoE 的关键区别有两点。第一，标准 MoE 的路由粒度是 token 级、候选空间是全局 expert 集合，容易让不同 token 因局部词法特征走向不同 expert，最终一个任务会覆盖大量 expert；Emo 先加了文档级共享池约束，把路由自由度从“全局任意选”收缩到“文档内共享候选集”，从而鼓励 recurring expert subsets 在训练语料中反复出现。第二，和 FlexOlmo、BTX 这类显式按领域切分 expert 的方法不同，Emo 不需要预定义 math/code/biology 之类标签，也不把 expert 绑定到固定人工类别，而是让模块结构从数据里自发形成。

从机制上看，这个约束改变了 expert specialization 的压力来源。标准 MoE 中，expert 更容易因为高频局部模式而分工，例如标点、功能词、位置相关模式，因为这些模式在 token 级 routing 下最稳定、最容易被 router 捕捉。Emo 把文档内 token 的候选 expert 集合绑定在一起后，单个 token 的局部差异不再足以驱动完全不同的路由，router 更有动力按文档级统计规律组织 expert，于是 specialization 更可能落在 topic/domain 这类跨 token 持续存在的高层因素上。论文强调，这种更高语义粒度的 specialization 正是 modularity 的来源。

从训练目标上，Emo 仍然保持 MoE 语言模型的主干训练方式，重点不是改 loss 形式，而是改 routing constraint。也就是说，它试图在不牺牲 full-model 通用性能的前提下，给 MoE 增加一个结构归纳偏置：同域 token 共享更相近的 expert 子空间。这个取舍很重要，因为如果模块化是靠强监督硬切出来的，往往会损失组合后的整体能力；Emo 的目标是 full model 仍然强，同时子集也可用。1e:T8f9,实验规模是这篇工作的一个重要支点：作者训练了一个 14B total、1B active 的 Emo，并在 1 trillion tokens 上预训练，同时给出同数据、同规模的标准 MoE baseline。主结果有两部分。

第一，作为完整模型使用时，Emo 的整体性能与标准 MoE 基本匹配。这一点很关键，因为它说明文档级共享 expert pool 并没有明显破坏 MoE 作为通用基座模型的能力。若 full-model 性能明显掉点，这个方法就更像部署特化模型，而不是通用预训练方案。

第二，也是论文最核心的结果：在只保留部分 expert 的受限部署设置下，Emo 的性能下降显著更小。作者在 MMLU 和 MMLU-Pro 的领域子集上做 expert subset identification 与部署，例如数学、物理、生物、社会科学等。结果显示，只保留 25% 的 expert 时，Emo 仅约 1 个百分点绝对性能下降；只保留 12.5% expert 时，下降约 3 个百分点。对照的标准 MoE 在相同约束下分别下降约 10 和 15 个百分点。这个差距已经不是轻微改进，而是说明两类模型的 expert 组织方式完全不同：标准 MoE 的 expert 子集不可独立用，Emo 的子集则在相当程度上可独立部署。

论文还做了路由分析，结论是 Emo 的 expert subset 更倾向于按高层语义主题聚类，例如 math、code 等，而标准 MoE 更容易出现低层 syntactic/lexical specialization，例如 prepositions、punctuation。这个分析不是装饰性的，它直接解释了为什么标准 MoE 在裁掉大部分 expert 后会崩：因为它的能力分散在很多细碎、跨任务共享的低层 expert 上；而 Emo 把同域能力更集中地组织进可复用的子集。

In a standard MoE, each token selects its top-k experts from the full expert set. In Emo, each document first gets a shared expert pool, and every token in that document can only choose its active experts from that pool. For example, with 128 total experts and top-8 activation, a document may be assigned a pool of 32 experts, and all tokens in that document must select their 8 active experts from those 32. Different documents can use different pools.

This changes the inductive bias of routing. Standard token-level routing has enough freedom that experts often specialize in frequent local patterns such as punctuation, function words, or other low-level lexical cues. Those are easy for the router to detect and reward, but they do not produce deployable modules. Emo constrains that freedom by tying the candidate expert set across all tokens in a document. Since documents tend to be semantically coherent, the router is pushed to organize experts around document-level regularities rather than token-local quirks. Recurrent expert subsets can then emerge across the corpus without any explicit domain labels.

This is different from prior domain-partitioned MoEs such as approaches that assign experts to predefined categories like code or biology. Those methods rely on human priors and fixed taxonomies. Emo instead uses document boundaries as weak supervision and lets the modular structure emerge from data. The training objective remains that of an MoE language model; the main intervention is the routing constraint, not a fundamentally new LM loss. That design choice is important: the method tries to preserve full-model generality while inducing subset-level modularity, rather than trading one for the other through hard specialization.20:T82e,The experimental setup is large enough to make the claims meaningful: the authors train a 14B-total, 1B-active Emo model on 1 trillion tokens, alongside a matched standard MoE baseline trained on the same data. The first key result is that as a full model, Emo matches the baseline MoE in overall performance. This is essential, because it shows the document-level shared-pool constraint does not significantly damage the model’s utility as a general-purpose pretrained LM.

The second and central result is subset deployment. On domain-specific subsets of MMLU and MMLU-Pro, including areas such as math, physics, biology, and social sciences, the authors identify relevant expert subsets and evaluate the model while retaining only a fraction of experts. Emo loses only about 1 absolute point when keeping 25% of experts, and about 3 points when keeping 12.5%. The matched standard MoE drops by roughly 10 and 15 points under the same constraints. That gap is large enough to indicate a qualitative difference in how expertise is organized, not just a mild robustness improvement.

The routing analysis is also important. Emo’s experts appear to specialize at higher semantic granularity, such as topic or domain, whereas standard MoE experts are more associated with low-level syntactic or lexical patterns like punctuation and prepositions. This analysis directly supports the modularity claim: if expertise is concentrated into semantically coherent subsets, partial deployment becomes viable; if it is scattered across many low-level experts, it does not.
