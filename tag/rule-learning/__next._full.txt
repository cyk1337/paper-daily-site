17:T570,The authors propose the lifted representation hypothesis: instead of memorizing each x→y mapping independently, an LLM often maps multiple observations into a shared latent representation H, and predicts outcomes through H. This is different from standard generalization. The focus is not only whether a rule transfers, but how examples are assigned to a common internal type in the first place.

When later evidence shows that H was too coarse, the model must perform shattering: refine the original shared type into more specific subtypes without destroying valid prior rules. To test this, the paper builds controlled exception-learning tasks with two exposure orders. In general-to-specific settings, the model first sees a broad rule and later receives exceptions; success requires shattering. In specific-to-general settings, the model first sees fine-grained cases and later benefits from forming a broader abstraction; success reflects lifting.

The study evaluates this across in-context learning, LoRA, and full fine-tuning. The paper also gives a gradient-alignment argument for why lifting is attractive during learning: if examples share positive cross-gradient interactions, a shared update direction is efficient, making a common latent structure more likely to emerge.
