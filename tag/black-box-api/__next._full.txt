18:T46d,LEASH formulates weak-to-strong escalation as a budget-constrained sequential stopping problem, where the weak model’s estimated failure risk acts as a proxy for the benefit of escalation. It builds a representation bank from a small set of held-out tasks by recording hidden states on prompt and action tokens and labeling them with task-level and subgoal-level outcomes. At test time, the current prompt or execution trace is scored by a Mahalanobis-distance criterion: the hidden state is uncertain if it lies closer to past failures than to past successes. This score drives two mechanisms. First, an initial gate decides whether to escalate at the start, while a runtime gate monitors the weak agent during execution. Second, after handoff, the weak model becomes a shadow observer for the strong model, checking the trajectory every three steps. If uncertainty is high, the pending action is rolled back and re-decided once with higher reasoning effort. The main difference from prior escalation schemes is that no additional monitor training, external knowledge base, reference model, or per-step multi-sampling is required.19:T5fa,The key methodological move is preregistration plus a full audit trail. The authors froze the protocol in advance, including acceptance thresholds: same-window repeat rankings had to reach Spearman 0.90, and byte-identical next-day replays had to reach 0.99 agreement. They then refused to repair the protocol after seeing the data. This is what gives the negative result force.

Several design choices matter. First, they built an append-only audit chain: request bodies, hashes, exclusions, valid task groups, replay pairs, and recorded metadata are all pinned and traceable. That rules out the usual explanation that the requests were not actually identical. Second, they decompose instability into distinct observable modes: within-window fluctuation, cross-day drift, and amplification caused by structured outputs such as rankings, where a tiny token-level change can invalidate or reorder an entire record. Third, they add prospective attribution experiments rather than stopping at a failed gate: same-day versus cross-day comparisons, four-provider comparisons, self-hosted controls, and constructed-error calibration. These help separate endpoint instability from task difficulty or judge incapacity. Fourth, the paper connects systems-level nondeterminism to measurement failure. Batch-dependent kernels, serving-time batching, and silent deployment changes were already known in systems work; the paper shows how these low-level effects become fatal once the output is used as a structured ranking or thresholded gate.1a:T56e,The headline numbers are strong and easy to interpret. Across two campaigns, the audit covered 52,988 request attempts. The authors are careful not to treat this as the effective sample size; the actual analyses rest on 31 valid task groups, 100 replay pairs, and 10 constructed-error conditions. The first preregistered gate failed badly: same-window repeat rankings achieved only Spearman 0.400 against a required 0.90. The second gate also failed: byte-identical next-day replays agreed at 0.78 against a required 0.99.

Equally important is what did not fail. Requests were delivered successfully, schemas were valid, request-body hashes matched the frozen plan, and all recorded metadata fields remained constant. In other words, the engineering record looked clean while the measurement record was unstable. That is a major empirical point: standard API logs are not enough to establish reproducibility. The supplementary comparisons appear designed to answer whether the issue is specific to one provider or endemic to black-box shared serving, and whether self-hosting reduces the instability floor. The constructed-error calibration is also important because it asks a sharper question than average accuracy: even if the observer can detect obvious errors on average, is its variance low enough for threshold-based scientific use? Under the tested configurations, the answer is no.1b:T4c4,Start with the introduction and the preregistered thresholds. The paper is strongest when read as an instrument audit, not as another judge-bias study. Then go to the protocol and audit sections: frozen requests, hash-based identity, exclusion rules, valid-unit definitions, and the exact procedures for same-window repeats and next-day replays. Those sections determine whether the negative result is methodologically clean.

After that, focus on the mechanism decomposition and the attribution supplements. Three questions are worth tracking while reading: why ranking outputs amplify tiny generation differences; how shared-endpoint batching, kernels, and deployment changes surface as black-box drift; and why provider metadata is too weak to support reproducibility claims. If time is limited, prioritize the main failure tables, the same-day versus cross-day breakdown, the self-hosted control, the constructed-error calibration, and the appendix material on the audit chain and disclosed exclusions.
