9:T44b,Its relevance to foundation-model research is through supervision design. Many post-training pipelines, reward models, and LLM judges implicitly assume that pairwise preference is the natural signal, while rubric scoring is often favored for interpretability and auditability. This work provides relatively clean evidence that, in domains with substantial tacit expertise and poorly enumerable quality criteria, comparative judgment can preserve expert signal better and do so more efficiently. That matters for at least two reasons. First, it informs how to build reward signals for reasoning, writing, and professional knowledge work, where experts can often tell which answer is better without being able to fully enumerate why in advance. Second, it says something about the ceiling of automated evaluation: if human experts are more reliable in relative judgments than in absolute scoring, LLM autograders may also be easier to train or prompt in the preference format. The contribution is not a new training algorithm, but it is useful infrastructure and evidence for reward-data construction.a:T5c5,There are three important design choices. First, the tasks are intended to reflect real professional work rather than exam-style legal QA. The benchmark uses 30 tasks drawn from Harvey’s BigLaw Bench, covering realistic litigation and transactional workflows, with prompts, supporting documents, and task-specific rubrics. Second, the outputs are organized into constructed quality levels, so the benchmark has a known target ordering that an evaluation method should recover. Instead of only sampling naturally occurring model outputs, the authors generate LLM responses at three quality tiers and then ask experts to assess them. This turns the comparison into a measurable ranking-recovery problem. Third, the annotation protocol is paired: the same underlying items receive both rubric-based and pairwise-preference annotations from practicing lawyers. Rubric scoring uses atomistic criteria with both positive and negative components; comparative judgment asks which of two outputs is better overall. The paper also evaluates both human judgments and LLM autograders under the two supervision formats. The central argument is not that rubrics are useless, but that in tasks dominated by tacit, holistic expertise, rubrics compress expert judgment into an incomplete explicit checklist, while pairwise comparison better matches how experts naturally evaluate quality. The tradeoff is that preference signals are harder to decompose, audit, and reuse as explicit criteria.b:T542,The main empirical result is straightforward: comparative judgment more reliably recovers the constructed quality ordering than rubric scoring, while taking less than half the annotation time. The released dataset contains 30 real-world legal tasks, 1,539 rubric scores, and 1,530 pairwise preference judgments, all provided by practicing attorneys. The paper estimates the annotation effort at roughly 242 hours of attorney time, or about $242,000 at large-firm billing rates. The advantage of comparative judgment appears both for human evaluation and for LLM autograders, which is important: the result is not only about human convenience, but also about the learnability or automability of the signal. The time-cost analysis is also meaningful. Rubrics require expensive upfront specification and slower item-by-item checking, whereas pairwise judgments ask for a direct relative comparison and therefore extract more usable signal per unit expert time. One important caveat is that the benchmark measures the ability to recover a designed quality ordering, not open-world absolute calibration. For reward modeling, this supports preference signals for ranking and relative quality discrimination, but it does not imply that preferences dominate rubrics in settings where decomposition, error attribution, or compliance auditing is essential.c:T439,Start with the introduction and dataset construction to understand that the paper is comparing supervision formats, not legal capability.
