The paper reframes late-layer under-utilization as a property of residual signal routing rather than a generic optimization failure. That matters because it changes what kinds of interventions are plausible: if the issue is missing gradient diversity, rescaling gradients is not enough. This has implications for how to think about depth scaling, deep supervision, early-exit designs such as LayerSkip, and parameter allocation across depth. The second contribution is more practical: it suggests that uniform-width stacking may be a poor match to how training signal is distributed, and that tapering width with depth can improve deployment efficiency at fixed training FLOPs. The pruning result is also relevant because it uses accumulated training gradients as an online structural importance signal, avoiding expensive post-hoc analysis. This is not a recipe-changing result on the scale of a new optimizer or tokenizer, but it is a serious attempt to connect training dynamics, architecture shape, and pruning through one structural hypothesis.18:T6f2,The technical story has three parts. First, the paper defines GFA in Pre-LN residual Transformers. Writing the network as x_{l+1} = x_l + F_l(x_l), the input gradient at layer l can be expanded as a sum over identity and downstream functional paths. The key quantity is not the full combinatorial path count, but a tractable proxy: how many distinct downstream signal channels are aggregated into a layer’s gradient. This fan-in decreases with depth. With deep supervision, the imbalance becomes even stronger because shallow layers receive additional gradient hierarchies from auxiliary heads. Second, the paper tests the hypothesis with both correlational and interventional evidence. It first shows that average per-layer gradient norm correlates with eventual functional importance, but then argues norm is only a proxy. In the ablative intervention, the authors artificially amplify late-layer gradient magnitudes; this does not restore late-layer importance. In the constructive intervention, they increase structural path counts through layer repetition; this does restore importance. That contrast is the central argument of the paper. Third, they derive two methods from the GFA account. CascadeFormer uses a depth-tapered width schedule: earlier layers are wider, later layers narrower, under a fixed training-FLOP budget. The idea is to allocate capacity where gradient composition is richer. CascadeFlow Pruning accumulates training gradients over time and uses them as a proxy for structural importance to rank and remove layers. Unlike post-hoc pruning methods based on activation similarity, one-shot sensitivity tests, or simple depth heuristics, CFP uses information gathered throughout training and does not require an extra analysis phase after pretraining.19:T579,The experiments include models trained from scratch up to 1.2B parameters. The paper first presents observational evidence: in pretrained LLaMA, deeper layers show higher representational similarity, and in LayerSkip-style architectures the marginal utility of later layers drops sharply. The main method results are on the authors’ own training runs. For CascadeFormer, under matched training budget, the model reaches perplexity comparable to a uniform-width baseline while reducing inference latency by 8.6% and increasing throughput by 9.4%. That is the clearest practical result: better deployment shape without paying extra training cost. For CascadeFlow Pruning, accumulated training gradients outperform standard pruning heuristics on perplexity and rank stability, while remaining competitive on downstream accuracy. The abstract does not expose all numbers, but the full text emphasizes that the ranking induced by accumulated gradients is more stable across checkpoints and evaluation settings than simpler heuristics. The most important ablations are the two interventions supporting the structural claim: equalizing late-layer gradient magnitude does not recover importance, whereas increasing path multiplicity does. Within a layer or a bounded local scope of neighboring MoE layers, the routed experts are treated as the compressible object. Given a reduction ratio rho, the method selects a smaller prototype pool P from the original pretrained experts, then assigns each original expert e to a prototype m(e). The router is left unchanged: when it selects an original expert slot, execution is redirected to the assigned prototype. This preserves the original router interface and avoids relearning gating.

Formally, the setup resembles a weighted representative selection problem. Each original expert has an importance weight w_e, and each expert-prototype pair has a reassignment cost d(e,p). Under a budget on the number of retained prototypes, the goal is to minimize total weighted reassignment cost. In the default setting, prototypes must be existing pretrained experts rather than synthesized experts, and there is no post-compression finetuning. Post-hoc weight fusion is only used as a diagnostic sensitivity study, which is an important design choice because it isolates the effect of remapping itself.

Two design boundaries matter. First, the method allows local cross-layer sharing inside a bounded scope, but avoids model-wide sharing because distant layers are more likely to have incompatible expert functions. Second, the compression budget can be allocated non-uniformly across layers within a scope. The main novelty is not algorithmic complexity but the representational split: the logical set of router-visible expert slots stays intact, while the physically stored expert pool shrinks. Actual memory savings therefore depend on checkpoint or runtime support for deduplicated shared prototypes.1c:T4ab,From the provided full-text excerpt, the main empirical claims are qualitative rather than fully numeric: across multiple pretrained MoE language models, ConMoE is a viable alternative to pruning and merging under matched logical routed-expert budgets; deterministic reassignment is the most stable component; broader cross-layer sharing and post-hoc weight fusion are more model-dependent.
