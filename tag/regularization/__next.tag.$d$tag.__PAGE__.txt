9:T4b6,The proposed mechanism is trait-direction drift:
- Define a teacher-side preference gap for each sample (p,r) as the log-likelihood difference between a biased system prompt and a set of neutral prompts, with a length-normalized variant to compare variable-length completions.
- Use an empirically motivated low-rank/logit-linear factorization of the prompt-by-sample log-probability matrix (via SVD-like geometry): prompt embeddings ψ(s) and sample embeddings φ(p,r) explain why certain “innocent” samples still carry trait signal.
- Argue (and test) that student SFT updates contain a component aligned with a recognizable trait direction; across steps this component accumulates beyond noise, producing measurable drift and eventually behavioral transfer (preference shifts or higher misalignment rates).
- Introduce probe-space corridor regularization: monitor the student’s coordinate along a probed trait/steering subspace and penalize deviations outside a corridor, limiting motion along the trait direction while keeping general task learning largely intact.

Two regimes are identified: (a) dead-gradient regime, formalized in Proposition 1, where caption-invariance of image-derived signals nulls first-order discriminative gradients; (b) negative-utility regime, where the auxiliary signal actively harms the loss and gradients consistently drive the gate toward zero. 2. Prefix injection stability: prefix tokens projected from hyperbolic space exhibit norm mismatch with text embeddings; RMS normalization of prefixes to the text embedding distribution resolves generation collapse, which is attributed to residual-stream dominance rather than attention-softmax saturation.b:T5e0,The paper formalizes two student views: q_x = g_theta(y|x) and q_c = g_theta(y|x,c). Standard on-policy distillation with privileged context minimizes a KL from the teacher f(y|x,c) to the no-context student q_x. This enforces what the paper calls privileged fidelity, but it leaves q_c unconstrained.

To address this, the authors propose No-Context Anchoring (NCA). The no-context student output is treated as a stop-gradient anchor, and an additional consistency KL penalizes deviation of the context-conditioned student view q_c from q_x. The intuition is simple: if the privileged information has already been absorbed into the parameters, then reintroducing the context should not push the model into a different behavioral mode.

A few design choices matter. The anchor is the no-context student, not the teacher, because the deployment target is the no-context pathway. Stop-gradient is also important; otherwise the two views could co-adapt and weaken the primary objective. The method only requires one extra forward pass per step, so the overhead is modest.

Conceptually, the paper separates two goals that are often conflated. Privileged fidelity asks whether the no-context student can match the teacher with context. Context removability asks whether the context has become genuinely redundant. The paper argues, and empirically shows, that the first does not imply the second. Representation-level analysis further suggests that NCA changes internal states, not just final-token behavior.c:T498,The experiments span 12 configurations across different domains and model families. Three findings stand out. First, context-induced degradation is common. In most settings, adding the original context back hurts accuracy and inflates response length. The authors identify three regimes, with Regime A covering 7 of 12 settings: the no-context student already performs well, but the context acts as a perturbation and causes both accuracy loss and high harm.

Second, NCA reduces this harm in 11 of 12 settings and improves context-conditioned accuracy in the majority of cases. Third, it largely removes response-length inflation and, in many settings, even improves no-context performance, which is the main training objective.

The paper also introduces a useful metric, Harm = P(q_c wrong | q_x correct), which is more diagnostic than raw accuracy drop because it directly measures how often context flips a previously correct prediction into an incorrect one.
