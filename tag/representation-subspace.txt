18:T472,The proposed method, DDSN, has two main components. The first is Iterative Orthogonal Subspace Decomposition. Instead of learning one linear probe, the method learns multiple orthogonal directions sequentially. After extracting one direction, it removes the already explained deceptive signal and searches for the next direction in the residual space. The goal is to capture complementary components of deception rather than redundant variants of the same feature. The second component is a Domain-Adaptive Gating Mechanism. Static combinations of these directions are not sufficient because a direction that is informative in one domain may be irrelevant or noisy in another. DDSN therefore uses a small amount of test-time few-shot adaptation to assign context-dependent weights to the extracted directions. In effect, the detector asks which deception components are active in the current domain, rather than whether one universal deception vector is present. Relative to standard single-direction probing, the key difference is not just moving from one dimension to k dimensions, but making the composition itself adaptive to context.19:T499,The main empirical claim is straightforward: single-direction probes can work within a dataset but generalize poorly across heterogeneous deception settings, supporting the paper’s argument that deception feature composition depends on context. DDSN achieves stronger and more stable performance across datasets. The headline numbers in the abstract are an average AUROC of 0.97 and 95% recall at 5% FPR with only 10 adaptation examples. The paper also claims that transfer across related domains can work without re-adaptation, and that the 10-example adaptation is only needed when there is a substantial context shift, which can be flagged by a lightweight classifier. The most important evidence, if supported in the full text, would be ablations on the number of subspace directions, comparisons across layers, tests against simpler multi-probe baselines such as PCA-style decompositions or static ensembles, and adaptation curves showing how much the gating mechanism buys over fixed combinations.
