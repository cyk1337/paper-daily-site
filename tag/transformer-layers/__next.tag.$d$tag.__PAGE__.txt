b:T9b0,The methodology is simple but well controlled. The authors define a metric called layer contribution: train only layer l with RL while freezing all other layers, then measure what fraction of the improvement from full-parameter RL is recovered relative to the same base model. This normalized recovery ratio makes comparisons possible across models and tasks.

The paper then builds around three experiment families.

First, single-layer isolation. Each transformer layer is trained independently under RL. The best single layer can recover up to 114% of the gain from full-parameter RL, while weak layers recover less than 30%. So the answer to “is one layer enough?” is not uniformly yes; it depends strongly on which layer is chosen.

Second, stability analysis. The authors repeat the measurement across seven models from two families (Qwen3 and Qwen2.5), three RL algorithms (GRPO, GiGPO, Dr. GRPO), and three domains: mathematical reasoning, code generation, and agentic decision-making. High-contribution layers consistently cluster in the middle of the stack. For a fixed model, per-layer rankings are strongly correlated across datasets and even across tasks: Spearman rho is 0.76 between NuminaMath-CoT and DeepScaleR, and 0.59 between NuminaMath-CoT and DeepCoder. This suggests the pattern is not a dataset artifact but a stable property of how RL exploits pretrained depth structure.

Third, algorithmic exploitation. Guided by the measured contributions, the authors propose layer-aware RL that prioritizes high-contribution layers instead of updating all layers uniformly. On Qwen3-8B, training only the ten highest-contribution layers reaches 69.1% average accuracy on math benchmarks, compared with 66.4% for full-parameter RL. They also show that models trained on different layers exhibit complementary behaviors, and majority voting among them yields further gains.

Mechanistically, the paper points toward a plausible interpretation: lower layers are tied more to lexical and local representation building, upper layers are closer to output formatting and surface realization, while middle layers are where RL can most effectively reshape reasoning transformations and decision boundaries. The paper does not fully prove this mechanism, but the empirical case is coherent. If layer contribution were just optimization variance, rankings would not remain stable across algorithms, datasets, and tasks. Another notable result is complementarity: models trained on different layers make different mistakes, and ensembling them through majority voting improves beyond the full-parameter baseline. That hints that layer-wise RL may explore distinct strategy subspaces that joint full-model training partially collapses.
