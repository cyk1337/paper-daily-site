Concepts are still treated as linearly represented, but their contributions are allowed to overlap and need not be orthogonal. Under this view, the representation difference associated with a target concept decomposes into three parts: an axis capturing the main transition between concept absence and presence, a normal plane orthogonal to that axis that carries sample-specific residual variation, and sensitive sectors within that plane that determine whether a steering vector projection facilitates, suppresses, or delays concept activation. This explains an empirical puzzle: two steering vectors can have similar global angular alignment with the target concept yet produce very different outcomes because they land in different sectors of the sample-specific normal plane. Methodologically, the work is not a new steering procedure but a new geometric model plus an empirical validation protocol. It first argues, via finite-dimensional counterexamples, that lossless orthogonal disentanglement is too strong an assumption.
