18:T899,The central idea is competing rewards over different segments of the same trajectory. Instead of assigning a single scalar reward to the full rollout, the method splits generation into a reasoning segment and a final answer segment, and scores them separately. The reasoning segment is rewarded for exploring increasingly harmful or unsafe interpretations. The answer segment is rewarded for producing a safe final response conditioned on that preceding unsafe exploration. The intended behavior is not globally “safe-looking” reasoning, but controlled exploration followed by safe recovery.

Several design choices matter. First, the paper argues that optimization mismatch between the competing objectives leads to unstable training. If the exploration reward and the safe-answer reward are not balanced in how they are sampled or optimized, the model can collapse to one side: either unsafe exploration without recovery, or conservative refusal without meaningful exploration. Second, the authors show that dense outcome-level rewards are still too coarse for this setting. They do not provide enough credit assignment to tell the model which part of the trajectory should be exploratory and which part should be controlled. This motivates process-based rewards at the segment level on sampled rollouts. Third, the method keeps exploration and recovery within one continuous chain of thought, rather than outsourcing safety to a separate classifier or a detached control module. That is an important distinction: the goal is to train safety as a reasoning dynamic, not as a front-end filter.

Relative to prior safety-RL methods such as TARS, the difference is not merely a modified reward function. The underlying training assumption changes. Earlier work largely treats reasoning as a tool for deciding whether to refuse. This paper treats reasoning as a space where the model must be allowed to inspect unsafe possibilities under control; otherwise it cannot resolve ambiguous prompts well. The robustness analysis around pre-filling attacks reinforces this point: if a model has never learned to recover from unsafe intermediate reasoning, steering its chain of thought becomes much easier.19:T62d,The main model is SEAR-1.5B. The headline result is about the safety–refusal trade-off rather than a single benchmark score. The paper reports that SEAR achieves the best trade-off among open models in its size class, outperforming the closest baseline, TARS. It also claims that despite having only 1.5B parameters, SEAR performs comparably to the much larger TARS-7B on this trade-off, which suggests that the gain is not a minor tuning effect but a meaningful change in how the model uses reasoning.

The most important experiments fall into four groups. First, the paper analyzes existing safety-trained reasoning models and shows that their CoT often behaves like a rubber stamp rather than a mechanism for ambiguity resolution. That diagnostic section is important because it motivates the method. Second, there are reward-design ablations comparing single outcome rewards, dense but non-process rewards, and true segment-level process rewards. The conclusion is that only the last option reliably learns the desired combination of unsafe exploration and safe answering. Third, the paper studies optimization stability and shows that mismatched optimization across competing rewards leads to unstable behavior. Fourth, it evaluates robustness to adversarial attacks, especially attacks that target the reasoning process such as pre-filling. SEAR is reported to be more robust under such attacks.
