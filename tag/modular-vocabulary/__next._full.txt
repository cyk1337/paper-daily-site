18:T9e1,技术方案分成两层：模块化 tokenizer 构造，以及与之配套的预训练策略。

先看 tokenizer。作者分别给出 modular BPE 和 modular Unigram 的构造方法，目标是训练一个“大而可拆”的 multilingual tokenizer，然后从中抽取任意语言子集对应的 subtokenizer。这里难点不只是把不同语言的词表拼起来，因为简单拼接会破坏 token id、merge 规则或概率结构的一致性，也不保证抽出来的子词表还能接近单语 tokenizer 的压缩率。论文的做法是从训练阶段就约束 tokenizer 的结构，使每种语言或语言组对应的子词单元可以被稳定抽取，同时保留 byte-level fallback 和 lossless detokenization。对 BPE 和 Unigram 都提供了可抽取方案，说明作者不是只在某一种分词算法上做特例。

再看模型训练。核心思想是让模型在预训练时反复看到不同的 subtokenizer 组合，从而学会在不同 token inventory 下读写文本。论文把这些 subtokenizer 采样后组成 mini-batches，训练同一个模型共享主干参数，但输入 embedding 和输出预测都只在当前子词表上工作。这样做的直接结果是：部署到某个语言子集时，不需要加载完整 multilingual output matrix，只需保留目标语言相关 token 的 embedding/output rows。由于输出层 softmax 的维度显著缩小，推理速度和显存占用都会改善。

这套设计和传统 multilingual tokenizer 的区别有三点。第一，传统方法把所有语言塞进一个固定词表，压缩率和输出层成本一起被全局绑定；这里则把“共享训练”和“专用部署”拆开。第二，传统做法若想针对某语言优化，通常只能单独训练 monolingual tokenizer 和模型，跨语言共享会被破坏；这里保留了一个统一模型，只在 tokenizer/output head 层面做模块化。第三，作者强调目标不是追求极端压缩，而是在接近 monolingual compression 的前提下，保持多语言共享能力和部署灵活性。

从全文表述看，论文还引入了 balanced multilingual training 的思路：通过对子词表进行采样，模型不会总是暴露在同一个大词表下，从而减轻高资源语言在词表分配上的天然优势。这也是它改善 cross-lingual fairness 的机制之一。文中使用 Normalized Sequence Length, NSL 来量化各语言相对单语参考 tokenizer 的压缩损失，这个指标贯穿 tokenizer 评估和后续效率分析。19:Ta6b,实验部分有几条结果值得记住。

第一，现有开源 multilingual tokenizer 的压缩不公平问题很明显。作者在 FLORES-200 devtest 上，用 24k 单语 tokenizer 作为参考，比较 TinyAya、NLLB-200、Gemma-3、Llama-3、Qwen-3 等开放 tokenizer 的 NSL。结论是这些 tokenizer 对英语的压缩通常接近单语参考，但对大多数其他语言明显更差。作者还特意把评测限制在 21 个欧洲语言上，这些语言本来就属于中高资源、且通常被现有 tokenizer 覆盖得较好；即便如此，不公平仍然明显，说明问题不是只出在极低资源语言上。到了低资源语言，差距会更大。

第二，覆盖范围和压缩率之间存在清晰 trade-off。论文训练了只覆盖 21 种语言的标准 BPE tokenizer，记作 Multi21；当词表大小到 256k 时，它与 24k 单语 tokenizer 的压缩差距可以缩小到 4% 以内。这个结果说明，只要减少覆盖语言数，每种语言能分到更多词表容量，压缩自然改善。模块化 tokenizer 的意义就在于：不必为部署某几个语言时继续承担全量覆盖的代价。

第三，模块化 subtokenizer 的压缩率可以做到接近独立训练的 monolingual tokenizer。摘要已经明确给出这一点，全文则把它作为方法成立的关键证据：如果抽取后的 subtokenizer 明显劣于单语 tokenizer，那么后续训练和部署都失去意义。作者对 BPE 和 Unigram 都做了验证，说明不是某个特定 tokenizer 家族的偶然现象。

第四，推理效率确实受益，而且收益在小模型上更明显。论文在引言里举例，Gemma 2 2.6B 即便共享输入输出 embedding，256k 词表仍占到总参数的 22%。这给了一个很直观的量级：在小模型里，词表不是边角成本，而是核心成本。模块化方案在推理时只保留目标语言相关输出矩阵，因此能比标准 multilingual tokenizer 更快，同时在 multiple-choice、summarization、translation 上保持有竞争力。摘要没有列出全部具体分数，但全文强调速度提升来自两部分：更好的目标语言压缩减少序列长度，裁剪后的输出层减少每步 softmax 计算。

第五，作者对“压缩提升是否转化为质量提升”做了审慎处理。文中回顾了相互矛盾的既有结论，因此实验重点不是证明压缩越高模型越强，而是证明在不明显损害任务表现的前提下，可以把效率和跨语言公平性做得更好。这一点从研究设计上是合理的，因为 tokenizer 改动最直接的收益确实先体现在 token budget 和 output-layer cost 上。1a:T5a6,This is a meaningful contribution at the tokenizer–pretraining interface. First, it turns the tokenizer from a fixed global artifact into a modular object that can be specialized after training. That is important because multilingual tokenization has usually been treated as a one-shot design choice. Second, it connects tokenizer design directly to model memory footprint and output-layer compute, rather than discussing compression in isolation. For 1B–3B scale models, vocabulary-related parameters are not a side issue; they can take a large fraction of the total budget. Third, the paper does not stop at tokenizer construction. It provides a pretraining recipe that teaches the model to operate under different subtokenizer subsets, which is what makes deployment-time vocabulary pruning actually work. Finally, the paper is careful about claims: it frames better compression mainly as an efficiency and fairness gain, not as a guaranteed quality gain. That restraint is appropriate and makes the contribution more credible.

For pretraining researchers, two ideas stand out. One is that vocabulary size, language coverage, and output-layer cost do not have to remain a single static trade-off; they can be shared during training and specialized at deployment. The other is that tokenizer design is not just preprocessing. If the model is not trained to handle changing token inventories, modular tokenization is not usable in practice.1b:Ta8d,The method has two layers: modular tokenizer construction and a matching pretraining strategy.

On the tokenizer side, the authors propose both modular BPE and modular Unigram schemes. The objective is to train a large multilingual tokenizer from which one can extract subtokenizers for any language or language subset. The challenge is not simply concatenating language-specific vocabularies. A naive union would break consistency in token IDs, merge rules, or token probabilities, and the extracted subsets would not necessarily preserve monolingual-like compression. The paper therefore designs the tokenizer training process so that language-specific components remain extractable while preserving byte-level fallback and lossless detokenization. The fact that the framework works for both BPE and Unigram is important; it suggests the idea is not tied to one tokenizer family.

On the model side, the key idea is to expose the model during pretraining to many different subtokenizer combinations. These subtokenizers are sampled and used to form batches, so the same backbone learns to read and predict text under varying token inventories. In each case, the input embedding and output prediction operate only on the active sub-vocabulary. At deployment time, this allows the system to load only the rows of the embedding/output matrices relevant to the target language subset, instead of the full multilingual vocabulary. The efficiency gain comes from two places: shorter sequences for the target languages and a smaller softmax/output layer.

Compared with standard multilingual tokenization, the difference is threefold. First, conventional approaches bind language coverage and output-layer cost into one fixed vocabulary; this framework decouples shared training from specialized deployment. Second, the usual way to optimize for a language is to train a separate monolingual tokenizer and often a separate model, which weakens multilingual sharing; here the model remains unified and only the tokenizer/output interface becomes modular. Third, the paper is not chasing maximal compression at any cost. The target is monolingual-like compression together with multilingual parameter sharing and deployment flexibility.

A further mechanism in the paper is balanced multilingual exposure through subtokenizer sampling. Because the model does not always train under the same giant vocabulary, high-resource languages have less structural advantage in vocabulary allocation. The paper uses Normalized Sequence Length (NSL) throughout to quantify how far each language is from a monolingual reference tokenizer, and this metric anchors both the tokenizer analysis and the efficiency discussion.1c:Ta0d,Several empirical results matter.

First, existing open multilingual tokenizers show clear compression unfairness. On FLORES-200 devtest, using 24k monolingual tokenizers as references, the paper compares tokenizers from TinyAya, NLLB-200, Gemma-3, Llama-3, and Qwen-3. The pattern is consistent: compression is often close to monolingual quality for English, but substantially worse for most other languages. Importantly, the evaluation is intentionally conservative: only 21 European languages, mostly medium- to high-resource and already relatively well covered by open multilingual tokenizers. If unfairness is visible there, it is not a niche low-resource artifact.

Second, the coverage–compression trade-off is explicit. The paper trains a standard BPE tokenizer on only those 21 languages, called Multi21. With a 256k vocabulary, its compression comes within 4% of 24k monolingual tokenizers. This is a useful calibration point: if you reduce the number of covered languages, each language gets more vocabulary capacity and compression improves. The modular framework is valuable because it lets deployment benefit from that specialization without giving up a shared multilingual model.

Third, extracted modular subtokenizers achieve compression close to independently trained monolingual tokenizers. This is one of the central validity checks of the method. If the extracted subtokenizers were much worse than monolingual ones, the whole framework would collapse. The paper demonstrates this for both BPE and Unigram, which strengthens the claim.

Fourth, the efficiency gains are real and especially relevant for small models. The introduction gives a concrete scale example: Gemma 2 2.6B spends 22% of its parameters on a 256k vocabulary even with tied embeddings. That makes the cost of the vocabulary impossible to ignore. By pruning the output matrix to the target language subset at inference, the modular approach speeds up decoding while remaining competitive on multiple-choice evaluation, summarization, and translation. The full paper emphasizes that the speedup comes from both better target-language compression and a smaller output layer.

Fifth, the paper is careful about the relationship between compression and quality. It reviews prior work showing mixed evidence on whether better compression improves downstream performance. Accordingly, the experiments are designed to show that efficiency and cross-lingual fairness can improve without materially hurting task quality, rather than claiming that compression alone guarantees better models.1d:T6af,This one is worth a careful read. A good order is: problem setup → tokenizer construction → pretraining recipe → efficiency and quality results.

Start with the introduction and the section on multilingual tokenizer inefficiencies. They clearly explain why a single multilingual vocabulary creates both compression unfairness and output-layer waste. Figure 1 and the NSL definition are foundational. Then read the modular BPE and modular Unigram sections closely to understand what “extractable subtokenizers” really means and how it differs from ordinary jointly trained tokenizers. After that, focus on the pretraining strategy: how subtokenizers are sampled at the batch level, how embeddings and output rows are shared or restricted, and how the model remains stable while the token inventory changes. Finally, read the experiments with attention to three groups of results: per-language NSL comparisons, speed/compression trade-offs as vocabulary size changes, and downstream performance when deploying to language subsets.

There are four questions worth keeping in mind. First, does switching among subtokenizers create optimization instability or sparse embedding learning? Second, after pruning the output layer, is cross-lingual transfer weakened because the token inventory has changed? Third, do the gains remain meaningful at larger model scales, or are they concentrated in the regime where embeddings are a large parameter fraction? Fourth, could the same modular idea extend beyond multilingual text to unified discrete token spaces for text, speech, or vision?
