a:T44b,The architecture repeatedly applies one Transformer block Fθ to a hidden state, h^{t+1}=Fθ(h^t,e), with optional input injection through a learned adapter. The main grid uses NoPE, with positional-encoding controls in the appendix. The key methodological move is to replace tail instruments with a head instrument. Tail instruments such as skipping a late loop or comparing Jacobians across late loops are unreliable because converged trajectories sit near fixed points; near such states, skipping is cheap and local circuits look similar by construction. The proposed diagnostic is convergence-time scaling τ(n,i): the first loop at which the decoded output at position i reaches its final value and stays there. Its scaling class acts as a mechanism signature. Constant τ indicates shortcut or refinement, τ proportional to log n indicates a parallel scan, and τ proportional to n indicates serial step-indexed computation. A streaming positive control validates the instrument: when token i is revealed only at loop i, τ recovers a linear slope of 1.0, while shortcut controls remain flat.b:T577,The experiments are controlled population studies over many seeds, recipes, and model sizes. The reported scales include a small model with a 2-layer block and d=128, a medium model with a 2-layer block and d=256, and a larger model with a 4-layer block and d=512. Tasks include prefix parity, LSB-first increment-with-carry, and group word problems over Z60, S4, S5, and A5. These tasks have known algorithmic structure: some admit constant-depth shortcuts, while S5 and A5 connect to NC1-complete word problems, and associative scan gives a logarithmic-depth solution in principle. The main empirical result is a linear computation frontier learned by free training. The frontier solves v positions per loop, with v approximately n_tr/T_tr; under T=n training, the speed is about 1 position per loop. Giving more loops at test time rescues late positions at fixed input length. Standard tail diagnostics fail: skip retention is essentially total on sufficiently solved examples, cross-step Jacobian alignment is uniformly high, and these metrics do not predict length generalization. The study also separates optimization walls from circuit complexity: A5 hardness is not the main barrier, while S5 operator scale is; an operator-first curriculum removes that wall. After unrolling, h_N=h_0+ε∑r_n. The squared norm contains a quadratic term, ε²/d · ||∑r_n||². In an ordinary non-shared residual stack, different residual branches are approximately independent, so this term grows like Θ(ε²N), which motivates ε=1/√N. In a looped network, the same W generates all updates. The activations are produced by the same recurrence, the residual directions remain positively correlated, and the quadratic term grows like Θ(ε²N²). Keeping the residual stream bounded therefore requires εN=O(1), i.e. ε=1/N. The same accumulation appears in the optimization analysis: the output change after one optimizer step scales as ηεN, so ε=1/N makes the stable learning rate roughly independent of N. For a practical block with L unique layers repeated N times, the paper separates two effects: a linear N factor from within-layer reuse and a √L factor from across-layer random-walk accumulation. In tied embedding/unembedding decoder-only transformers, tokens that are unseen or rarely used during training develop nearly collapsed (un)embeddings. Even if the model learns the reasoning algorithm structurally, it cannot reliably distinguish U1 from U2 if their output representations are almost the same. Second, it provides theory. The authors show that under SGD with weight decay and LayerNorm, this collapse is not an accident but a natural consequence of the training dynamics. Intuitively, unseen tokens receive little task-specific gradient to keep them separated, while regularization and normalization push them toward a low-variance shared region. Third, it studies interventions. A copy-enabled architecture alone is insufficient, because copying does not solve the problem once multiple unseen tokens are already indistinguishable at the output layer. Reliable symbolic reasoning requires a combination of: a copy-capable architecture, sufficiently diverse symbolic training data, and a mechanism that prevents embedding collapse. The latter can be achieved by freezing the (un)embeddings or periodically resetting them. The paper also reinterprets active forgetting from earlier work: its benefit is not merely “preventing knowledge storage in embeddings,” but more concretely interrupting collapse.
