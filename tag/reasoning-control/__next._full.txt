17:T500,The paper proceeds in two stages. First, it performs a descriptive analysis with anchor-conditioned routing. Repeated anchor token ids are aligned across rollouts, and the corresponding sparse routing vectors are compared to test whether routing still separates problem identity, trajectory context, and effort mode. The authors define three anchor families—trajectory, boundary, and delimiter—and vary the routing readout window around the anchor. Routing representations are built from selected expert ids and router weights across layers, and then analyzed with nearest-neighbor leakage and KNN basin-purity style metrics.

Second, the paper proposes RAD, Routing Agreement Decoding. Given a pool of completed rollouts for one problem, RAD locates a fixed anchor by token id, represents each rollout using the routing window around that anchor, and selects the rollout that lies in the densest Weighted-Jaccard KNN routing neighborhood. During selection, it never parses, normalizes, compares, or executes answer strings. The answer text is only read after the routing-based choice is made. In that sense, RAD is analogous to majority voting in objective, but not in interface: majority aggregates agreement in answer space, while RAD aggregates agreement in routing space.18:T52a,The evaluation covers 10 sparse-MoE configurations and 6 datasets, including gpt-oss and Qwen3-MoE variants, with math, GPQA, LiveCodeBench, and SWE-bench Verified. The results have three main parts. First, identical token ids do not imply identical routing states: routing still separates problem and effort conditions, with reported cross-problem and cross-effort nearest-neighbor leakage below 5%, suggesting token identity is not a sufficient statistic for router state. Second, routing neighborhoods align with final-answer basins, especially near answer-opening delimiters; delimiter@16 gives the strongest basin purity, while even marker-only delimiter@marker is already informative. Third, RAD is roughly on par with textual majority in math and GPQA, slightly ahead in the authors’ rollout pools, while using no answer-string comparison. Its more distinctive use case is code and agentic settings: on code generation, where lexical variation makes exact string voting awkward, RAD can directly select rollouts and report pass@1; on the decidable subset of SWE-bench Verified, boundary-based re-anchoring improves over random selection by about 4–5 points.

The evidence is best read as a feasibility result rather than a universal selector. It depends on access to router traces and on sensible anchor design.19:T5d3,The key construct is Minimal Sufficient CoT (MSC). Given input x, gold answer y*, and a generated CoT trajectory z, the paper defines a sufficiency score Sθ(z|x,y*) based on how well z supports producing y* under the model. Instead of raw joint likelihood (which decays exponentially with answer length), it uses a geometric mean (per-token average) for stability. With a threshold δ, a trajectory prefix is δ-sufficient if Sθ exceeds δ; MSC is the shortest sentence-level prefix that is δ-sufficient, avoiding token-level truncation that can break step structure.

SuCo is a two-stage training recipe:
1) MSC-Aligned Fine-Tuning (MFT): extract MSC prefixes from full-CoT trajectories to build (x, MSC, y*) supervision, then SFT to internalize concise-but-sufficient reasoning patterns.
2) Sufficiency-Aware Policy Optimization (SAPO): RL to learn continuous, difficulty-adaptive reasoning allocation. The paper emphasizes (i) sufficiency-aware rewards that penalize both insufficient reasoning and excessive tokens, and (ii) a dynamic complexity pool that tracks the evolving distribution of reasoning behaviors during training to stabilize sampling and mitigate policy-induced distribution drift.

Compared to prior adaptive reasoning models, SuCo avoids external classifiers/domain labels and avoids discrete budget modes; it operationalizes “when to stop” via sufficiency thresholds and shortest-prefix extraction, then trains a policy to control reasoning length continuously.1a:T411,AutoTTS shifts the design target from individual TTS heuristics to discovery environments. The concrete instantiation uses a width-depth space: width controls the number of reasoning branches, and depth controls how far each branch is extended. For each problem, the system pre-collects reasoning trajectories and intermediate probe outputs. A controller then operates in an offline replay environment, choosing actions such as creating branches, continuing generation, probing intermediate answers, pruning branches, and stopping. The state tracks the question, instantiated branches, active branches, branch depths, revealed probe feedback, and remaining budget. Two engineering choices are central. Beta parameterization exposes only one scalar trade-off parameter, β, while deriving internal thresholds deterministically, reducing the risk of overfitting and making search easier.
