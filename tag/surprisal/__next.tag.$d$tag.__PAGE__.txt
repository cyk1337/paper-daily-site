9:T5be,The method extends prior layer-wise surprisal analysis in two important ways. First, it uses the MECO L2 eye-tracking corpus and splits readers by LexTALE vocabulary proficiency into high- and low-proficiency groups, instead of averaging across readers. Second, it introduces Predictive Depth, a summary statistic for how predictive power is distributed across layers. The point is not only to identify the single best layer, but to characterize whether the predictive mass is concentrated in shallower or deeper layers.

Operationally, the authors take several base LLMs, apply Logit Lens to hidden states from each layer, and compute token-level surprisal layer by layer. They then use those surprisal signals to predict two word-level gaze measures: first-pass gaze duration (FPGD), which reflects earlier processing, and total gaze duration (TGD), which includes rereading and later integration. The central claim is not simply that deeper layers are better, but that the preferred depth profile depends on both reader proficiency and the temporal scope of the gaze measure.

The model choice is sensible for the question. They use base rather than instruction-tuned models because the goal is to probe pretraining-induced linguistic representations, not alignment-modified output behavior. The model set spans Gemma 3, Qwen 2.5, OLMo 2, and Llama 3.1 across multiple sizes, which helps test whether the pattern is family-robust rather than tied to one architecture.a:T5e0,The main findings visible from the full text are fourfold. First, across 12 tested LLMs, lower-vocabulary readers tend to show deeper Predictive Depth for FPGD, meaning that later-layer surprisal better explains their early reading-time behavior. This is consistent with compensatory processing accounts in reading theory, where less proficient readers rely more on broader context. Second, the proficiency gap is much smaller for TGD. Once the measure includes later fixations and rereading, deeper contextual integration appears important for both groups, reducing the between-group contrast. Third, TGD is generally associated with deeper Predictive Depth than FPGD across proficiency groups, which fits the interpretation that the two gaze measures reflect different stages of processing. Fourth, a leave-one-out analysis over texts shows that selecting an internal layer usually gives small but consistent gains over the final layer on unseen texts.

The experimental design is clean in the sense that the same texts, readers, and models are reused while only proficiency grouping and gaze measure vary. The limitations are also clear: the corpus contains only 12 texts of roughly 100–200 words, so external validity is limited; the predictive gains are weak to modest rather than large; and LexTALE captures vocabulary proficiency, not the full space of language ability.
