9:T7c2,The paper starts by revisiting AlphaEdit. In standard locate-then-edit formulations, one updates an MLP down-projection matrix W with an increment Δ so that new edit keys map to target values, while minimizing interference with historical edit keys and preserved knowledge keys. AlphaEdit constructs a projector P_tau from the eigenspectrum of K_0 K_0^T and restricts updates to a supposed null space. The authors show that this story is incomplete. Under default settings, P_tau is very close to the identity matrix, so only a small fraction of directions is actually removed. In other words, the default projection barely projects anything out. When the projection is made stronger, preservation improves, but edit efficacy and locality degrade, suggesting that hard subspace constraints are too coarse.

Based on this analysis, the paper reframes preservation as a regularization problem rather than a projection problem. The key idea is that parameters differ greatly in sensitivity: perturbing some weights changes general behavior a lot, while perturbing others has limited effect. FisherEdit estimates this sensitivity with a diagonal Fisher matrix and uses it to impose element-wise weighted penalties on the update. Parameters with larger Fisher values receive stronger penalties; less sensitive parameters remain more available for encoding new facts.

Optimization-wise, FisherEdit replaces activation-space projection with a parameter-space objective that includes Fisher-weighted quadratic regularization. The paper also derives an efficient row-wise closed-form solver, keeping the method practical in the same spirit as direct weight editing methods such as ROME, MEMIT, and AlphaEdit. The advantage is finer control: instead of removing entire directions, it regularizes each parameter according to estimated importance. The obvious limitation is the diagonal Fisher approximation, which ignores parameter correlations and depends on the quality of the Fisher estimate.a:T67c,Experiments cover multiple backbones, including Llama-3, Qwen2.5, and Olmo-3, and multiple sequential editing benchmarks. The abstract claims that FisherEdit achieves the best overall editing performance while better preserving downstream general abilities and reducing both representation drift and parameter drift relative to prior methods. The key thing to inspect is not a single edit-success number, but how performance degrades as the number of sequential edits grows.

The most informative part of the paper is the AlphaEdit diagnosis. The authors inspect the default projector and show that its diagonal entries are close to one and off-diagonal entries are negligible, meaning it is nearly an identity map. They then examine the spectrum of K_0 K_0^T and show that only a small number of singular directions exceed the default threshold. Controlled ablations separate the effects of projection strength, regularization strength λ, and threshold τ. The conclusion is that preservation is driven mainly by regularization, while stronger projection trades off efficacy and locality.

For FisherEdit itself, the important results should include edit success, sequential robustness, preservation of unrelated knowledge, downstream capability retention, and drift metrics. The excerpt does not include the full numeric tables, so the exact gains are not visible here, but the main reading target is whether FisherEdit consistently improves the preservation–efficacy trade-off across backbones and over long edit sequences.
