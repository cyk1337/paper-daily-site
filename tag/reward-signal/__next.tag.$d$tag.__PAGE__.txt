5:T938,The paper models LLM-as-judge as a low-rank projection operator on a latent quality manifold. A response representation h is decomposed into approximately orthogonal components such as style, factual content, and cultural/contextual content. Human scoring is modeled as a rubric-dependent weighted combination over these components: objective rubrics put most weight on factual dimensions, while subjective rubrics spread weight across cultural and contextual dimensions as well. An LLM judge, by contrast, is modeled as an orthogonal projector P_J with relatively small rank k, and the score is derived from the energy of the projected representation.

The intuition is that next-token pretraining and common post-training objectives make judges especially sensitive to fluency, common factual regularities, and model-familiar directions, but relatively insensitive to culturally grounded or community-specific dimensions. If multiple judges are trained on similar corpora and aligned with similar objectives, they can project onto similar low-rank subspaces. That yields high judge-judge agreement even when all of them remain far from the human scoring subspace.

From this framework, the paper derives four diagnostics. First, score-spread compression compares judge variance to human variance; strong compression indicates that judges flatten distinctions humans care about. Second, effective rank measures how many principal components are needed to explain most score variance; lower rank for judges indicates fewer active evaluation dimensions. Third, principal angle to the human subspace directly measures whether judges and humans are looking in similar directions. Fourth, stacked correlations and null-space fraction quantify whether judge-judge agreement systematically exceeds judge-human agreement and how much human variation lies in the judges’ blind space.

A strong design choice is that the paper does not stop at raw judges. It evaluates in-context prompting, supervised fine-tuning, DPO, and post-hoc demonstration-anchored calibration, asking whether training can actually rotate judges toward the human subspace rather than merely rescale outputs. Another strong choice is the dataset regime: Indic multilingual, culturally situated, community-grounded tasks, where the subjective/objective distinction is real rather than synthetic.6:T7f3,The study covers four community-built Indic datasets, eight Indic languages, and 41 LLM judges. The data include cultural QA and community-health settings, with three independent human raters per item and rubrics spanning the objective-to-subjective spectrum. On the model side, the paper evaluates 14 base judges and 27 trained, preference-optimized, or calibrated variants from three model families ranging from 3B to 405B parameters. Uncertainty is reported with paired item bootstrap intervals rather than point estimates alone.

The main findings are consistent across datasets. On subjective rubrics, LLM judges use much less of the human score range, with sigma_J / sigma_H around 0.3 to 0.5. Geometrically, judge subspaces are nearly orthogonal to the human subspace: principal angles are around 87 to 89 degrees, whereas human-human subspace angles are around 78 to 81 degrees. So judges are farther from humans than humans are from each other. Correlation patterns show the same asymmetry: judge-judge Pearson correlations are roughly 0.35, while judge-human correlations are around 0.1 or lower. The gap closes only on the rubric with a verifiable factual answer, which supports the paper’s boundary-condition story: objective rubrics have lower intrinsic dimensionality, while subjective rubrics require more dimensions than the judges effectively use.

The intervention results are especially informative. SFT and DPO often restore some score spread, so judges stop compressing scores as severely, but they barely improve the principal angle to the human subspace. In some cases they even hurt certain rubrics. That means training can make judges look more expressive without making them more human-aligned. Post-hoc demonstration-anchored calibration is the only intervention that improves all four community-health rubrics together, but it still falls short of human reliability. This is an important negative result: better scaling or more preference optimization does not automatically solve the structural mismatch.7:T646,This one deserves a full read. Start with the geometric formulation before looking at the result tables. The paper becomes much clearer once each diagnostic is mapped to a distinct question: score compression asks whether judges flatten distinctions; effective rank asks how many dimensions they actually use; principal angle asks whether they evaluate along human-like directions; stacked correlations ask whether inter-judge agreement should be trusted at all.

The most important figures are the subjective-versus-objective spread comparisons, the effective-rank plots, the principal-angle comparisons between judge-human and human-human subspaces, and the intervention plots across prompting, SFT, DPO, and calibration. The intervention section is especially worth close attention because it answers a practical question many teams implicitly assume away: can standard training repair judge misalignment? The paper’s answer is mostly no; it can widen the score range, but it rarely rotates the evaluation geometry enough.

From a foundation-model perspective, three follow-up questions are worth tracking while reading. First, how exactly does next-token pretraining induce a fluency- and familiarity-biased judge geometry? Second, why does demonstration-anchored calibration help more consistently than additional training—does it mainly rescale outputs, or does it locally re-anchor the evaluation basis? Third, could these geometric diagnostics be inserted upstream into reward-modeling or synthetic-preference pipelines as a gating criterion?
