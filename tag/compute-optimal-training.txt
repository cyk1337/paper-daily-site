18:T47f,The main experimental tool is Byte Latent Transformer, because it can vary compression rate without changing vocabulary size. BLT first aggregates byte-level inputs into latent tokens with a local encoder, applies a global Transformer over those latent tokens, and then uses a decoder to predict bytes. This separates compression rate from vocabulary size and avoids the confounding present in subword tokenizers, where token boundaries, vocabulary, parameter count, and compression are coupled. The authors modify the original BLT setup by removing byte n-gram hash embeddings, since such n-grams can span more bytes than a latent token and would interfere with controlled compression. They also adjust the scaling of local modules, favoring width over depth. The scaling analysis then fits laws that include compression rate, comparing data measured in tokens with data measured in bytes. Additional subword-tokenized Transformer experiments test whether the trends are specific to BLT. Multilingual experiments use parity, the byte-length ratio of parallel sentences across languages, to explain shifts in optimal data ratios and compression rates.19:T4a3,The experimental sweep is substantial: 988 BLT models are trained, ranging from 50M to 7B parameters, across multiple target compression rates. The paper explicitly compares this range with common subword regimes, including a popular BPE tokenizer at about 4.57 bytes per token on English DCLM-like text and SuperBPE at about 6.16 bytes per token. The key empirical finding is that, along the compute-optimal frontier, parameter count scales with training data measured in bytes rather than tokens. Equivalently, increasing compression should reduce the optimal tokens-per-parameter ratio. The experiments also show that compression is not free. Higher compression shortens the global sequence and reduces Transformer FLOPs, but each latent token must represent more bytes, increasing local modeling burden and prediction difficulty. This leads to an optimal compression region under a fixed compute budget rather than a monotonic preference for maximum compression.
