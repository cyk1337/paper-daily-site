9:T8d6,The work has two layers. The first is ResearchMath-14k, a corpus of research-level mathematical problems. The authors collect 1,233 source documents from three streams: arXiv papers explicitly about open problems, public web pages containing open-problem lists, and workshop/problem-session/proceedings collections. They use a two-stage agent pipeline. The Extractor reads a source end to end, verifies that it truly contains open problems, extracts candidate problem statements, and produces a first rewrite. Importantly, it is instructed to recover definitions, notation, and assumptions from the broader document context rather than just clipping local text spans. The Refiner then revisits the source and rewrites each item into a standalone problem statement. It also searches later papers that cite or extend the source, using them to recover background that the original text may have left implicit. The design choice is notable: the target is not archival fidelity, but trainable, self-contained problem statements.

The second layer is ResearchMath-Reasoning. The authors generate about 220K teacher trajectories from two open models and then study their failure modes in detail. The paper highlights several recurring pathologies: non-attempts, silent substitution of the original problem with an easier or narrower one, fabricated arXiv links or PDF URLs, and references that sound plausible but are unrelated or nonexistent. Based on these observations, they apply behavioral and factuality filters to produce a 5,000-trace subset, ResearchMath-Reasoning-Filtered, intended for training. This is a fairly different philosophy from generic reward-model scoring. The authors explicitly define the failure modes that are especially damaging in research-level math and filter for them directly. That makes sense in a setting where there is often no canonical answer, and where models can easily learn the style of research discourse without learning the discipline of truthful reasoning.

Relative to prior work, the paper does not rely on expert-written solutions and does not center on synthetic hard-problem generation. Its main contribution is to build a realistic data substrate and a cleaning pipeline for weak supervision at the research frontier.a:T687,On the data side, ResearchMath-14k contains 14,056 research-level problems. The initial agent pipeline extracts 20,835 candidate problems from 1,233 source documents, which are then deduplicated, filtered, and normalized. The source breakdown reported in the paper is: 524 arXiv open-problem documents yielding 8,182 problems; 161 open-problem web pages yielding 5,331 problems; and 548 problem-session sheets or curated lists yielding 7,322 problems. The Extractor produces 16.9 questions per source on average, with median 10 and maximum 358, showing a highly skewed source distribution.

On the trajectory side, ResearchMath-Reasoning contains roughly 220K teacher traces. A manual review of 100 sampled traces finds that around 30% are visibly problematic, including non-attempts, problem substitution, and fabricated references. A broader trace-level analysis spans eight open-weight models and surfaces a striking pattern: newer models cite much more, but are not more factual. One headline number is that 54.0% of 720 analyzed ResearchMath-14k traces contain at least one fake reference. The abstract also reports that newer generations produce 5.6x more references, reinforcing the gap between research-like style and actual factual grounding.

On the training side, the filtered 5,000-trace subset is used to fine-tune three Qwen3 base models: 4B, 8B, and 30B-A3B. The reported average gain is 9.2 percentage points. The key takeaway is not just the absolute score, but the fact that weak supervision on unsolved problems can still be useful once the most damaging behaviors are removed.
