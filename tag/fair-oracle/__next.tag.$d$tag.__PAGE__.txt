b:T606,There are three central design choices. First, the task is a 26-week factored POMDP with six hidden factor processes governing demand, supply, logistics, quality, and related risks. Each week the agent observes a dashboard of symptoms—inventory, in-transit orders, realized demand, freight quotes, berth waiting times, canal transit counts, supplier scorecards, and quality reports—then chooses replenishment actions. Second, the hidden dynamics are intentionally factorized and the event tape is fixed, which makes a fair reference policy tractable: an exact Bayes filter is run per factor, and a rollout policy acts on the same observation stream seen by the LLM. The key point is not that the oracle is strong, but that it is non-privileged. Third, the benchmark defines decomposed metrics instead of relying on total cost alone. A skill score places the agent between a symptom-blind base-stock floor and the fair Bayesian oracle. Detection lag measures how quickly the agent names the active hidden factor in its rationale. Knowing-doing rate measures how often the agent still incurs stockout-like action failures during stress weeks it has already diagnosed correctly. The benchmark also introduces isolated, persistent, and compound stress profiles, keeping the world model fixed while varying overlap among hidden failures. One useful detail is that models are tested with a rules-only prompt and no strategy playbook, which makes the benchmark more about converting diagnosis into action than following a hand-crafted procedure.c:T5ba,The evaluation covers 50 seeds and 200 episodes, grouped by stress profile, with Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-5.4, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, and Grok 4.5. The main empirical result is a strong mismatch between diagnosis and control. On the perception side, all four models detect hidden-factor episodes at broadly similar rates, around 84% to 88%, typically within one week of onset. In some cases, the lower-skill models even diagnose faster. On the cost side, however, performance diverges sharply: two of the four models finish below the symptom-blind floor overall, meaning that on roughly half of their seeds, a policy that ignores symptoms entirely and follows a fixed inventory rule would have been cheaper. This is the clearest evidence that action quality, not just inference quality, is the main failure source. On persistent seeds, even during stress weeks that were correctly diagnosed, every model still suffers stockout in about 34% to 43% of those weeks. The paper further notes a severity effect: the weeks severe enough to be easy to detect are also the weeks most likely to break the shelf. Even after accounting for that, the main separation between models remains the quality of mapping diagnosis into order quantity, route, supplier choice, and protective actions.
