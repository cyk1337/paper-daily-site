It turns a common empirical concern into a formal statement: non-Euclidean geometry can buy useful inductive bias, but it may also degrade robustness to the starting checkpoint. Many RLHF-style and KL-regularized updates can be viewed as mirror-descent-like procedures. If the initialization is the output of upstream pretraining, then downstream instability is not merely noise; it can be an intrinsic property of the algorithm. The contribution has three layers. First, a clean exponential separation between MD and GD in initialization stability. Second, a mechanism-level decomposition: instability can come from non-quadratic geometry itself, even without poor conditioning, and it can also come from the severe local conditioning of entropy near the simplex boundary. First, the lower-bound construction in a well-conditioned setting. The authors build a three-dimensional example with a convex smooth objective and a non-quadratic regularizer that is strongly convex, smooth, and well-conditioned in Euclidean norm. The point is to rule out the easy explanation that instability is caused only by bad conditioning. The mechanism is the nonlinearity of the mirror map: a small perturbation in primal space can evolve into a multiplicatively growing discrepancy through repeated dual-space updates and nonlinear primal projection. This cannot happen for quadratic regularizers, where the dual-primal map is linear and the dynamics behave much more like GD.

Second, they analyze entropic mirror descent on the simplex, with negative entropy as the regularizer. Here the mechanism is more transparent. Near low-mass coordinates, negative entropy is highly ill-conditioned in ℓ1 geometry, so even linear objectives can produce exponential amplification of initialization differences. The paper gives a lower bound that is uniform over all simplex initializations in high dimension and also captures the low-dimensional near-boundary regime. It also proves a matching exponential upper bound, which is important because it shows the exponential rate is not a proof artifact but the correct stability scale for entropy MD. The analysis is then extended to broader Legendre regularizers used in online learning and RL.

Third, the stabilization methods. Initialization-Anchored MD adds an extra Bregman penalty to the starting point x0 at every step, effectively tethering the trajectory to the initialization. In well-conditioned settings, this yields initialization stability O(ε + 1/√(T log T)) together with optimization error O(log T / T). But this guarantee depends on local smoothness of the regularizer around the initialization, so it can become vacuous for ill-conditioned geometries such as entropy near the boundary. To address that, the authors introduce Fixed-Anchor MD, where the extra Bregman term is anchored at a fixed reference point independent of the initialization. This removes the dependence on local curvature at the start and gives O(1/T) initialization stability even in ill-conditioned settings, while keeping optimization error at O(log T / T). In the three-dimensional well-conditioned non-quadratic example, the output discrepancy grows roughly like exp(c·ηT) before saturating at a constant scale, while GD or quadratic regularization does not show this behavior. On the simplex with entropic MD, even linear objectives exhibit the predicted exponential sensitivity, especially near the boundary and in high-dimensional settings.
