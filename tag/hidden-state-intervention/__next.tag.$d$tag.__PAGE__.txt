9:T5eb,HRS shifts distribution matching from output calibration to hidden-state editing.
1) Formulation: for each item x, predict a categorical rating distribution p(r|x) that matches the empirical human judgment distribution, covering both ordinal and nominal label spaces.
2) Oracle residual study: assuming access to human ratings, solve for an additive hidden-state correction Δh at a chosen transformer layer such that the downstream frozen layers produce a distribution closer to humans. Empirically, (i) intervening at a single layer captures most of the attainable gain, and (ii) the set of oracle corrections is largely low-rank—most improvement is preserved in a small subspace.
3) Human-Residual Steering: learn an input-conditioned intervention without human labels at test time. A small predictor g takes the layer hidden state and the original rating logits, outputs low-dimensional coefficients a, and a learned basis B maps them to Δh = B a. Apply h' = h + Δh at that layer; keep the backbone frozen while backpropagating through the frozen downstream stack into g and B.
4) Training objective: optimize proper scoring rules on individual annotations (multiple ratings per item), with objectives tailored to ordinal vs nominal judgments to encourage calibrated distribution prediction.
5) Positioning: compared to output-level calibration, HRS can express richer, input-dependent distribution reshaping; compared to distributional fine-tuning, it is far cheaper to deploy but may have a lower ceiling.a:T70d,The method, TILR (Trajectory-Invariant Latent Refinement), follows a simple principle: identify a stable low-rank subspace first, then constrain refinement to that subspace. The authors collect hidden-state differences between stronger and weaker reasoning trajectories and study their structure across inputs, paraphrases, perturbations, and reference checkpoint choices. Empirically, these contrastive differences are highly concentrated spectrally: a small number of directions explain most of the variation between strong and weak trajectories. TILR uses this observation to estimate an invariant subspace that captures reasoning-associated directions that persist across semantically equivalent inputs and across reference settings. Once this subspace is learned, the usual contrastive latent update is projected into it, filtering out unstable components that are specific to a particular phrasing or trajectory realization. A second component, the adaptive alignment gate, modulates intervention strength based on how well the current trajectory aligns with the learned invariant structure. When alignment is high, the intervention is trusted more; when alignment is weak, the method suppresses the update to avoid injecting noisy or mismatched steering signals. This is the main conceptual difference from prior latent refinement methods: the paper rejects the assumption that the full strong-minus-weak hidden-state difference is uniformly meaningful. It treats only the low-rank, cross-condition stable component as reliable. The paper also introduces trajectory-level analyses beyond final accuracy, including paraphrase consistency, latent trajectory variance, and checkpoint-pair sensitivity, to test whether the identified directions are functionally relevant rather than merely descriptive.b:T485,Several experimental findings are clear from the full text excerpt.
