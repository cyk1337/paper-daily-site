Key findings include: 1. During training, the chordal distance between new and old subspaces at each refresh reaches 96.7%–99.6% of the geometric maximum, nearly orthogonal. 2. The difference between two same-step subspaces (0.725√(2r)) is nearly equivalent to the difference across T steps (0.742√(2r)), indicating subspace variation is dominated by estimation noise. 3. The gradient spectrum contains only a small number of reproducible dominant directions (~39 at r=128), followed by a gapless smooth power-law tail. The top-r cutoff lies inside the bulk spectrum, with an adjacent singular value ratio of only ~1.005; per the Davis-Kahan theorem, subspace estimation has inherent randomness. 4. Averaging gradients cannot open a spectral gap, as the tail is power-law signal rather than pure noise, shrinking only as N^(-1/4), so subspace stability cannot be achieved by increasing averaging budget.
