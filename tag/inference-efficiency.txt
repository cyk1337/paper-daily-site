18:T53c,The framework has two components. The first is Anchor-Label Relabelling, or ALR. Instead of using the original corpus token as the label for every drafted slot, ALR relabels slots using distributions derived from greedy rollouts of the target model. This restores valid supervision after divergence because the labels now correspond to what the target would generate under the actual context reached during rollout. In effect, ALR replaces invalid corpus continuations with target-consistent labels.

The second component is In-Rollout Anchors, or IRA. Relabelling alone does not fully solve the mismatch if the drafter is still mostly trained on corpus contexts rather than target-generated contexts. IRA inserts draft blocks directly into target rollouts, exposing the drafter to contexts that the target model actually visits while also reusing prefix computation. This reduces train-test mismatch and improves efficiency.

An important detail is that the paper treats erase-style methods as serious baselines, not strawmen. The comparison includes soft survival-weighted erase from prior work, and the appendix notes that soft gating is stronger than matched ungated distillation. Relative to these baselines, ALR recovers labels, while IRA recovers context. The combined method addresses both invalid supervision and exposure mismatch.19:T646,The experiments cover both text and vision-language targets. On text benchmarks with Qwen3-4B, the average speedup over seven tasks at T=0 improves from 2.35x for DFlash, 2.67x for Erase, and 2.70x for ALR to 2.80x for ALR+IRA. The corresponding accepted-length metric tau improves from 3.32, 3.75, and 3.87 to 3.93. At T=1, the average speedups are 2.23x, 2.45x, 2.48x, and 2.55x, with tau values 3.10, 3.46, 3.56, and 3.60. Gains are especially visible on reasoning and coding tasks. For example at T=0, HumanEval improves from 3.46x with Erase to 3.63x with ALR+IRA, AIME25 from 3.01x to 3.14x, and GSM8K from 3.16x to 3.55x. MT-Bench and Alpaca also improve, though by smaller margins.

On vision-language settings, Table 1 and Figure 2 show that ALR+IRA achieves the best accepted length across multiple 8B and 4B configurations. The paper states that on ALLaVA, adding IRA on top of ALR yields up to an 8.54% margin over Erase. Compared with DFlash trained on the same data, ALR+IRA improves accepted length by up to 36.5% at T=0 and 33.1% at T=1 across the two 8B settings. The gains also transfer to another target model, Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, where ALR+IRA exceeds Erase by 7.13% in accepted length at T=0. Another useful result is that labels are derived from greedy target rollouts, yet the ranking remains consistent under stochastic sampling at T=1, which suggests the method is not narrowly overfit to greedy decoding. The paper also reports a 5.90% aggregate accepted-length gain over Erase under chain verification at T=0 on ALLaVA, indicating the benefit does not depend on tree branching.1a:T47c,For a quick read, start from the failure mode: why does one off-policy token invalidate supervision for all later slots in a drafted block? Once that is clear, ALR and IRA become straightforward. Then read the method definitions and jump to Table 1 and Figure 2, which already capture the main empirical message. The text benchmark table is also useful because it shows where the gains concentrate.

If you want to judge whether the method is worth reproducing, focus on three comparisons. First, ALR versus Erase tells you whether relabelling alone helps. Second, ALR+IRA versus ALR tells you whether target-generated context matters beyond relabelled labels. Third, compare T=0 and T=1 to see whether the method only helps greedy decoding or generalizes to sampling. The appendix is worth checking for soft-gating baselines, training-horizon sensitivity, and the exact accepted-length evaluation rule.

This is a good skim paper. Around 20 minutes is enough to extract the main idea and results. If you work on serving or drafter training, spend another 30 minutes on the appendices to understand the baseline definitions and evaluation protocol.1b:T857,The system, Galahad, has two separable components. Taliesin stores and restores the KV state produced by prefilling a block of text. Blaise stores the text itself in sectioned form and selects a relevant section on the CPU so the model reads only that section rather than the full corpus. Taliesin removes repeated computation; Blaise removes unnecessary reading.

Taliesin is built around byte-exact memory. After a runtime computes KV state for a prompt block, the system stores that state under a fingerprint defined by the exact input bytes, the model fingerprint, and the tenant identity. If a later request contains the same byte block, the runtime loads the saved KV state instead of recomputing it. The design goal is exactness: restored state must yield bit-identical logits to a fresh prefill. To enforce this, the system validates stored records on load and falls back to normal computation on any failure. The guiding rule is explicit: serve without memory rather than serve with wrong memory. This differs from approximate cache reuse or ordinary GPU prefix caching, where the emphasis is often hit rate rather than strict equivalence.

Blaise is retrieval-like, but the returned unit is byte-exact text rather than an approximate semantic chunk assembled from embeddings. The paper does not disclose the internal section-selection method. It reports only the first operating mode, where the CPU selects one section and passes its exact text to the model. A second mode is mentioned, where the model reads Blaise’s index and Taliesin amortizes that reading too, but that mode is not evaluated here.

Implementation-wise, Galahad is packaged as a shared library that plugs into vLLM, SGLang, and llama.cpp without modifying model weights or runtime source code. It requires tenant identity and model fingerprinting to prevent cross-model or cross-tenant misuse, supports integrity checks and encryption at rest, and explains why reuse must happen at block granularity with exact token-position alignment. That boundary follows from transformer positional structure and KV semantics: near matches are not safe substitutes.1c:T662,The evaluation has two main parts: correctness and system behavior. The most important result is not a benchmark score but exact restoration. The authors report bit-identical logits after save-and-restore and byte-equal prefill across four model architectures. They also test four safety mechanisms—confirmation hash, licence signature, tenant separation, and byte comparison on load—using explicit attack toggles: attacks succeed when the defense is disabled and fail when it is enabled. This makes correctness a first-class requirement rather than a best-effort property.

On workload analysis, the paper reports that across seven real-world datasets, 98.7% of prompt tokens were text the model had already read before. That number is the main justification for persistent memory: if nearly all prompt tokens are repeated, prefill reuse can dominate latency and energy savings.

In a controlled recall test, Blaise reduces the text passed per question from roughly 97,000 tokens to about 668 tokens by selecting one relevant section instead of sending the full corpus. The introduction also states that the paper measures accuracy, latency, and GPU energy, includes an external retrieval baseline, and covers 30 out of 30 tested models, but the provided full-text excerpt does not include the complete result tables. The most concrete confirmed findings are therefore exact restoration, very high repeated-token rates, and large context reduction from section-level text selection.

The second is Sparse Attention.
