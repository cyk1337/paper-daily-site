9:T4be,The paper separates two evaluation modes. In simulation, a scorer reads an explanation and predicts feature activations token by token; the score is pooled Pearson correlation. In fuzzing or detection, the scorer judges whether marked tokens match the explanation, and the metric is balanced accuracy. The authors audit four public pipelines and identify three shortcut families. Neuronpedia stored fuzzing examples crop activating windows around the activation peak, placing the peak on the center token; whether that token is marked almost determines the answer. Current delphi and newer Neuronpedia full-context runs contain fixed mark-position or mark-count cues in fuzzing negatives. OpenAI-style simulation sets contain sequence membership and ordering cues in top-and-random examples, allowing sequence-level activation priors to explain part of the score. For each cue, the paper derives either a closed-form shortcut rule or a sharp bound. It then constructs controls: random-offset natural windows to remove the center-position cue, matched marks to remove mark-count and mark-position cues, and a sequence-level decomposition to separate within-sequence explanation reading from between-sequence priors.a:T5b2,On 500 random GPT-2 small SAE features and 1,001 stored Neuronpedia fuzzing runs, 91.3% of activating examples are cropped with the peak at the center token. A rule that answers yes when the center token is marked reaches 0.979 balanced accuracy, compared with 0.838 for the stored LLM scorers after recomputation from stored per-item verdicts. A mark-count-only rule reaches 0.969. In the OpenAI simulation setting, a post-hoc rule that predicts activity on the first five sequences obtains 0.227 pooled correlation, and an oracle given each sequence’s mean activation obtains 0.288, both above the published GPT-4 simulator score of 0.160. The paper also asks whether these shortcuts change conclusions. A commercial typed-decision scorer, Jev, scores higher on stored snippets than on natural windows for 44 of 49 features, but the preregistered ranking test does not show clear feature reordering; Spearman correlation between the two rankings is 0.732. In the few fuzzing items where the center cue conflicts with the answer, LLM scorers rarely follow the cue. A small scorer trained on stored snippets loses 63% of its score on natural windows, while the same architecture trained without explanations reaches 83% of its held-out score. On natural windows, Jev scores 0.331 against 0.013 for shuffled explanations, clearing explanation-free position and sequence priors; an open 8B simulator also beats its shuffled control there, 0.194 versus 0.019.b:T6bf,The paper proceeds in three steps. First, it formalizes the issue. An explanation function E maps features to strings, and the collision cluster of a feature is the set of all features sharing the same explanation. The authors then define descriptive resolution as the mutual information I(F; A) between a uniformly sampled feature F and its assigned explanation A = E(F). This is a useful move because it turns a vague concern about label reuse into an information bottleneck statement: if the explanation space is much smaller than the feature space, ambiguity is structurally unavoidable. Second, the paper performs a reanalysis of a public human-annotated SAE feature corpus rather than generating new labels. This choice is deliberate. Human labels are a cleaner, higher-variance descriptive source than current LLM explainers, so showing severe collision there strengthens the claim that machine-generated labels should be at least as collision-prone. Third, the paper critiques existing evaluation. It proves that detection-style explanation scores are invariant under substitution among collision-equivalent features, meaning a high score cannot establish that the explanation identifies the intended feature rather than another feature in the same collision class. To address this, the authors propose two corrective directions: discrimination scoring, which asks whether an explanation distinguishes the target feature from colliding alternatives, and collision-adjusted detection, which modifies standard detection-style evaluation to account for ambiguity. The paper also provides pseudocode and discusses computational cost. The main conceptual shift is from explanation correctness to explanation identifiability.c:T494,The empirical section is a statistical reanalysis rather than a new large-scale training study. After deduplication, it contains 722 annotated features: 470 from Gemma 2 2B SAEs and 277 from Pythia 70M SAEs. The headline numbers are strong. Those 722 features correspond to only 235 distinct annotation strings. The mean annotation string is reused across 3.07 features. 82.1% of features share their annotation with at least one other feature. The most frequent label, “plural nouns,” is assigned to 101 distinct features spanning both model families, 18 layers, and four model components. This makes collision look structural rather than anecdotal. On the theoretical side, the paper proves a proposition showing that detection-style scoring cannot distinguish among features within the same collision cluster, so high explanation scores do not imply successful feature identification.
