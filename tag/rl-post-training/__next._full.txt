18:T675,PGB Curriculum has two main components. The first is gradient-based learning-unit discovery. Motivated by an NTK-style view, the paper treats sample-level gradients as approximate descriptors of local optimization effects, projects them into a lower-dimensional representation, and clusters samples based on those representations. The resulting curriculum units are not grouped by task type or difficulty, but by whether they induce similar parameter-update directions under the current policy. The rationale is simple: group-level scheduling is meaningful only if samples inside a group have reasonably consistent optimization effects.

The second component is a predictive multi-armed bandit. Instead of using recent average advantage or success rate as a direct estimate of utility, the method trains a lightweight temporal predictor that jointly models external learning signals and internal policy states to forecast each group’s future utility. Those predicted utilities are then converted into a Boltzmann sampling distribution, increasing the probability of high-utility groups. The key distinction is that the scheduler predicts future usefulness rather than smoothing past usefulness, and it conditions on policy state rather than only on visible reward-like signals.

The method is designed to avoid two common failure modes. Some samples are already mastered and provide little marginal gain if oversampled. Others are currently too hard, producing nearly indistinguishable rewards and weak gradients. PGB tries to keep sampling mass on the region that is most useful for the current policy, and to move that region as the policy improves.19:T48d,The empirical results are straightforward. Relative to uniform-sampling GRPO, PGB improves in-domain performance by 8.8% to 14.3% and out-of-domain performance by 14.7% to 16.3%. Relative to existing curriculum baselines, it gains another 4.9% to 9.2%. The paper also reports better training efficiency: PGB reaches the final performance level of the GRPO baseline earlier in training, so the benefit is not only higher final quality but also faster progress.

The OOD gains are notable. They suggest the method is not merely oversampling easy in-domain prompts, but may actually be improving the quality of the learning signal. The provided excerpt does not include the full list of model sizes, datasets, or all ablation tables, but two ablations are especially important to inspect in the full paper: replacing gradient-based grouping with difficulty/category-based grouping, and replacing the temporal predictor with a retrospective average-based bandit. If both hurt performance, that would support the claim that the gains come from the combination of optimization-aware grouping and predictive scheduling, rather than from adding a generic curriculum module.1a:T769,The core idea is replay-driven decoupling. Rather than forcing the trainer to wait for a newly collected on-policy batch, ReFlow maintains a buffer of recent trajectories and allows optimization to proceed continuously by sampling from that replay pool while rollout workers asynchronously add fresh data. The point is not to import generic off-policy RL wholesale, but to exploit short-horizon sample reuse without letting policy mismatch grow too large.

The design appears to have three layers. First, execution-level decoupling: rollout and training are disaggregated so the learner is no longer tightly gated by fresh generation. This directly reduces long-tail bubbles caused by variable decoding and environment latency. Second, algorithmic support for replay: recently generated samples are reused under staleness-aware controls and correction mechanisms so that replay improves efficiency without drifting too far from the current policy. The paper also claims a theoretical result that replay can reduce gradient variance, which is relevant because LLM RL suffers from noisy rewards and large sequence-level variance. Third, runtime adaptivity: instead of fixing rollout/training resource splits and generation concurrency, ReFlow adjusts them as workload characteristics shift over time.

Relative to standard asynchronous actor-learner systems, the differences are clear. Existing designs usually tolerate only mild stale on-policy data to hide some generation latency. ReFlow goes further and treats replay as the main lever for reducing rollout demand. Existing systems also tend to treat resource allocation as a static tuning problem; ReFlow makes adaptation part of the system design. At the same time, the paper seems careful not to push into fully off-policy RL with very old data. The emphasis is on recent replay, which matches the stability requirements of LLM post-training.1b:T569,From the available full-text portion, the experimental agenda is clear even though the exact tables are missing. First, the paper needs to show end-to-end efficiency gains: less trainer idle time, better GPU utilization, and shorter wall-clock training, especially on multi-turn agentic workloads with heavy latency tails. Second, it needs to establish the quality-efficiency trade-off: compared with synchronous RL, mild asynchronous baselines, and variants without replay or with restricted replay, ReFlow should achieve similar quality with fewer fresh rollouts or better quality under the same time budget. Third, it needs stability and ablation evidence: replay ratio, freshness window, staleness control, and adaptive scheduling should each be tested, and the claimed variance-reduction effect should have empirical support.

The provided text does not include the result tables, so I cannot reliably state model sizes, task scales, speedup factors, or reward deltas. What is clear is that multi-turn agentic RL is a primary target setting, because that is where rollout cost, trajectory length, and environment irregularity are largest. When reading the experiments, the key checks are: how old replayed samples are, whether importance weighting or clipping is used, how performance changes across staleness ranges, and whether dynamic scheduling adds value beyond replay alone.1c:T6a8,EliteReplay has two stages. Before RL training, the frozen base model is used to build an elite archive with MAP-Elites. This is a key design choice: simply replaying random correct samples from the base model would mostly recover frequent strategies and still underrepresent rare ones. MAP-Elites discretizes a behavior-descriptor space into cells and keeps at most one high-quality solution per cell, so the replay set is explicitly balanced over distinct strategy types rather than over raw sample frequency.

During post-training, the model is optimized with GRPO plus a small replay loss on the archived elite solutions. The replay term acts as an anchor, encouraging the policy to keep assigning probability to diverse correct strategies while still allowing RL to improve task reward. This differs from generic KL regularization or entropy bonuses. KL-to-reference constrains overall drift from the base model but does not specifically protect low-probability correct strategies. Entropy regularization encourages spread, but spread alone does not guarantee preservation of distinct correct solutions. EliteReplay is more targeted: it stores verified, behaviorally distinct solutions and explicitly rehearses them.

Another important technical point is the definition of diversity. The paper evaluates diversity at the level of solution strategies, using a deterministic structural description extracted from the solution itself.
