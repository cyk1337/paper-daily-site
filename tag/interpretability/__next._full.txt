18:T4d7,Two-layer contribution:
- Theory (MUtE*): define erasure as a map f: R^D→R^D producing Q=f(X) such that concept information is removed (perfect privacy / indistinguishability across class-conditionals in the erased space) while utility is maximized (minimal distortion / maximal retained information under the chosen criterion). The authors characterize optimal erasure functions and show that optimality inherently induces a dual deterministic counterfactual mapping between concept classes.
- Practice (MUtE): to avoid the heavy compute of general nonlinear erasure (adversarial training, density estimation, Bayesian optimization), they propose an implementation with a translational bias: many concepts in modern encoders behave approximately like linear directions / translations, so counterfactual trajectories can be parameterized as (near) translations. This yields an efficient procedure that supports both erasure and counterfactual representation generation.
Compared to projection-based linear methods (INLP/LEACE), MUtE expands the objective to a more general optimality framing and explicitly couples erasure with counterfactuals; computationally, it trades generality for scalability via a structured geometric assumption.19:T463,KPI (Key Path Identification) refines a standard SAE-steering pipeline. Starting from a set of ‘positive’ SAE features correlated with the target behavior (faithfulness), it builds an interaction graph among these features using a small development set. Nodes are candidate positive features; edges represent activation/causal dependencies across layers (and potentially within-layer interactions), motivated by the observation that a strongly steering feature often requires upstream features to activate it. KPI then defines key features via graph structure—high indegree features are treated as bottlenecks/aggregators—and constructs key paths by anchoring on a key layer with strong steering effect and tracing back through preceding layers’ key features. Steering is applied only on features along these key paths, aiming to remove redundant features that dilute the intervention. The paper further connects the intervention to attention dynamics: key-path steering increases attention to context-provided ‘golden answer’ tokens and shows a gradual formation of knowledge-selection behavior across depth.1a:T4b6,SharedSAE is a TopK sparse autoencoder with shared latent indices and model-specific encoders and decoders. Each model tokenizes the same text with its own tokenizer, and a window aligner constructs spans whose decoded text matches across models, producing aligned residual-stream activations. Each model’s encoder maps its activation into the common latent dictionary. For latent selection, the method normalizes selection scores across models, sums the normalized votes, and applies a joint TopK to choose one shared index set. The selected magnitudes are not normalized, so each model retains its activation scale information. Each model reconstructs its own activation with its own decoder, and a cross-stream rescaling term makes codes from one model reconstruct other models’ activations, tying the same latent index to similar features across models. During training, vote-source dropout selects the TopK indices from a randomly chosen single model with probability 0.5, which makes solo-model inference in-distribution. For adding a new model, frozen-dictionary adaptation trains only the new model’s encoder, decoder, and biases while keeping the shared latents and their descriptions fixed.1b:T45e,The method has two main parts. First, the paper localizes refusal. Instead of treating the residual stream as the universal readout site, it distinguishes the fresh write from the accumulated residual. In transformers this is the attention output; in SSMs it is the selective-scan block output; in hybrids the relevant write sites are examined separately. Linear harm probes are trained to separate harmful from benign prompts, and the strongest decodability appears at the architecture-specific write site rather than in the accumulated residual.

Second, the paper tests cross-architecture alignment and causal transfer. It uses a Procrustes rigid rotation to align one model’s representation space to another’s, allowing only rotation, not reshaping. A refusal direction learned in a transformer is mapped into the target architecture’s space, then removed or gated at the target write site. If this transported direction still controls refusal, the result is stronger than simple linear separability: it suggests the models share a genuinely similar representation rather than merely a shared label boundary.1c:T52d,The main target model is Falcon-Mamba-7B, with Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B as transformer references, plus validation on RWKV-6 and Zamba2-7B. Several results stand out. 1) On a surface-controlled subset, a harm probe at the write site reaches AUROC 0.99, while a surface-only probe scores 0.54, indicating the probe is not just detecting jailbreak wrapper text. 2) A single rigid rotation transfers the transformer refusal direction into SSM and hybrid spaces; removing that direction weakens refusal, most clearly for the transformer-SSM pair and at the Mamba write site in Zamba2. 3) A matched-magnitude control shows that directions estimated at the write site causally control refusal, whereas directions estimated in the accumulated residual degrade substantially. 4) The detector-triggered gate is used as a causal test rather than as a defense claim. On Falcon-Mamba, prefix-injection attack success drops from 98.0% to 5.0%. Under a suffix attack optimized through the gate, the detector still fires on all 100 attacked prompts and attack success remains at 1.0%, down from 15.0%. The paper is careful to note that this gate is not stronger than a simpler baseline that always emits a fixed refusal once the detector fires. What transfers across architectures is the refusal direction, not a superior defense recipe.1d:T479,The method has two layers. First, the paper defines and estimates step advantage. A CoT is segmented into steps. For each step, the authors take the prefix up to that point and run Monte Carlo rollouts to sample continuations and final answers, estimating expected reward from that prefix. Comparing expected reward with and without the step gives the step’s advantage. This avoids prompt rewriting and avoids relying on manually designed perturbations; it measures marginal contribution directly on the original trajectory. Second, they turn noisy step-level estimates into analyzable structure. A changepoint-based procedure identifies whether a trace contains steps whose advantage confidently exceeds an effect-size threshold, separating informative from uninformative traces. They then ask whether judges can predict these labels or scores from text alone, using both prompted judges and critics fine-tuned on advantage-derived supervision. Performance is compared not only to simple baselines but also to a noise ceiling induced by rollout uncertainty, which is important for interpreting how much decodable signal is actually available.1e:T504,The experiments span multiple generator models, datasets, and both thinking and non-thinking settings. Three findings stand out. First, important steps are sparse: many sentences that look like reasoning have near-zero advantage. The opening geometry example captures this well: two self-checking steps look similar in text, yet one changes success probability negligibly while another raises it from about 52% to about 94%. Second, judge performance is strongly asymmetric. Larger zero-shot judges beat a prevalence baseline, but remain far below the noise ceiling. Fine-tuned critics improve substantially and get close to the ceiling on incorrect traces, yet recover only a small share of the decodable signal on correct traces. This suggests that in successful reasoning, the state changes that matter are often not fully externalized in the text. Third, the paper uses advantage trajectories to study reasoning dynamics and finds that gains from scale and from thinking mode are driven mostly by stronger answer priors before the first reasoning step, rather than by the answer being discovered progressively during the CoT. The paper also reports that uncertainty-management steps are disproportionately important in correct responses, for both thinking and non-thinking models.1f:T59e,The method has three parts. First, the authors recover the prior direction. Given the unembedding matrix W and an empirical unigram distribution p_unigram, they solve a linear least-squares problem over the frequent-token subset to find a vector d_prior such that softmax(W d_prior) reconstructs the unigram distribution as closely as possible. This requires only the output weights and token counts from a corpus, with no model forward passes. They argue that the recovered direction is unique under the fitting procedure and show that it is absent at random initialization but emerges through training. Second, they derive a tempered Bayesian interpretation. For any final prediction state y_pred, they decompose it into λ d_prior plus an orthogonal component. Plugging this into the softmax yields a factorization of the predictive distribution into a unigram prior raised to the power λ times a context-dependent likelihood term. This gives λ a clean probabilistic meaning: λ=0 removes the prior, 0<λ<1 tempers it, λ>1 sharpens it, and λ<0 inverts it. Third, they validate the mechanism dynamically and causally. They vary contextual informativeness through coherent context, shuffled tokens, short contexts, and ambiguous prompts, and track λ. They also intervene directly on the prediction state along d_prior and compare against norm-matched random-direction perturbations to test whether this axis is functionally special.20:T59b,The experiments cover Llama, Qwen, Gemma, and Pythia, from 0.4B to 405B parameters. Four results matter most. First, existence and universality: a single direction reconstructs the unigram prior with low KL error across all tested families, suggesting this is not an artifact of one architecture or recipe. The paper also shows that the direction is not present at initialization and appears only after training. Second, context dynamics: λ decreases as coherent information accumulates, but not merely when token count grows. Shuffled or semantically weak contexts do not reduce λ in the same way, which supports the claim that λ tracks information rather than length or position. Third, causal intervention: increasing the projection along d_prior pushes predictions toward the unigram prior, while decreasing it makes predictions rely more on contextual evidence. Random directions with matched magnitude do not produce the same effect, so d_prior is not just a post hoc statistical fit. Fourth, scaling behavior: the paper reports that in the high-context regime, λ often decreases with hidden dimension across families, hinting that larger models suppress default priors more effectively when context is informative. To control entanglement, they repurpose Selective Gradient Masking during pretraining. For biology examples, gradients to the non-biology parameters are zeroed after backprop, forcing biology knowledge to flow mainly into the biology subnetwork. For a sampled subset of non-biology examples, the biology subnetwork is zeroed in the forward pass, training the model to handle retain and adjacent data without relying on that specialized pathway. The remaining examples are trained normally. By varying the fraction of training steps that use SGTM, they produce six models with graded levels of disentanglement. This is the central design choice: the dataset and the downstream unlearning algorithms stay fixed, while the internal routing of knowledge during pretraining changes.

They then apply three standard unlearning methods to every model: Weighted Gradient Ascent, Weight Divergence Regularization, and RMU. The logic of the paper has two stages. First, show that the six models indeed span a meaningful range of disentanglement, using three separate metrics rather than relying on naming or training settings alone. Second, compare retain cost at matched forgetting levels. This makes the experiment much cleaner than prior studies that varied data mixtures or unlearning procedures and then attributed the outcome to entanglement.24:T5e8,The main result is consistent and easy to interpret: more disentangled models achieve better retain-forget trade-offs. At a fixed level of forgetting, the most disentangled model incurs about 4x lower retain cost than the most entangled one under WGA and RMU, and about 1.3x lower under WDR. That is large enough to suggest that entanglement is not a minor nuisance variable but a substantial driver of collateral damage in unlearning.

The experimental scale is moderate but well controlled: roughly 3.7B training tokens, 1024-token blocks, a forget domain that is 3.7% of the corpus, and a fixed 20% subsample of each test split used for unlearning sweeps and reporting. All models have the same 254M parameter count, which removes model-size variation as a confound.

Two details are especially worth noting. First, the adjacent domain is explicitly separated from the retain domain, which makes the setup more realistic than a simple biology-versus-rest split. It allows the paper to probe whether forgetting one domain also harms nearby knowledge. Second, the gain from disentanglement depends on the unlearning method: WGA and RMU benefit much more than WDR. That suggests an interaction between representation structure and the mechanics of the editing algorithm, rather than a single universal effect size.
