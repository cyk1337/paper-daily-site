9:T91d,The method builds on on-policy distillation and paired-model contrastive supervision. Let the student be an autoregressive policy πθ. The model first samples its own response y from prompt x, so training happens on the state distribution the student actually visits. Standard OPD would query a teacher πT on those states and train the student toward the teacher distribution, often with reverse KL. WISD replaces the external teacher with a pair derived from the same model: the original model serves as the stronger branch π+, and a weakened copy serves as the weaker branch π−. The training signal comes from their log-probability contrast on the same on-policy states, indicating which tokens the original model prefers more strongly than its weakened counterpart.

Conceptually, this is close to policy-shift supervision in DOPD-style methods. The goal is not merely to imitate a teacher distribution, but to extract a direction of improvement from the relative preferences of a model pair. This has two practical advantages. First, the supervision is dense across every token on the student’s own trajectories, which is useful for reasoning where intermediate steps matter. Second, because the strong and weak models share the same origin, the distribution gap may be easier to transfer than in privileged-information self-distillation, where the teacher is conditioned on extra context unavailable to the student.

The paper studies two training-free ways to construct the weaker model. One is low-bit quantization, which introduces systematic precision loss in the weights. The other is magnitude pruning, which removes low-magnitude parameters and reduces effective capacity. Both are cheap, require no calibration data, and can be applied directly to the original model. That design choice is important: the whole pipeline only needs unlabeled prompts.

The method relies on a meaningful assumption: the difference between the original model and its weakened copy should encode a stable capability drop, so that moving against that drop becomes a useful learning direction. Quantization and pruning are sensible choices because they induce structured degradation rather than arbitrary noise. If the weakening were too distorted, the contrast could reflect random perturbation instead of a usable improvement signal.a:T655,The experiments cover multiple reasoning architectures and scales, with benchmarks in mathematical and coding reasoning. The headline numbers are strong and broad rather than isolated. Quantization-based WISD improves average accuracy by 4.0 points and yields positive gains in 21 of 25 model–benchmark settings. Pruning-based WISD improves average accuracy by 5.5 points and yields gains in 23 of 25 settings.

The paper highlights several concrete results. With pruning, Qwen3.5-4B improves from 57.7% to 70.5% on HMMT 2025 and from 39.1% to 56.7% on LiveCodeBench v6.
