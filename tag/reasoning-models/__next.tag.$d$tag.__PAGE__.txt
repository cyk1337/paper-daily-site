9:T82c,The contribution is an evaluation framework rather than a new optimizer. On the continuous side, the authors use standard synthetic functions including Branin, Hartmann-3, Hartmann-6, Ackley, and Rastrigin. Domains are normalized to [0,1]^d and objectives are converted to maximization. A key design choice is that they do not only test the canonical form of each function. They also create disguised variants by applying bijective transformations to the input domain and output range, specifically to reduce the chance that the model exploits memorized patterns tied to familiar function names, coordinate systems, or known optima. This is one of the most important parts of the paper because it separates optimization behavior from benchmark recognition.

The LLM is treated as a batch policy. At each step, the prompt contains previously queried points, their objective values, and the remaining budget, and the model proposes q candidates with q in {1,2,4}. They compare single-turn prompting, where each round is reconstructed from scratch, against multi-turn interaction, where the model keeps the conversation and can reflect on prior reasoning. The total oracle budget is fixed at N=120, with 2d initial points, so comparisons across batch sizes are budget-matched. Performance is measured with regret, especially simple regret.

For the discrete setting, they use the PMO molecular optimization benchmark. Here the model proposes molecules in SMILES form and deterministic oracles score them. This setting is naturally aligned with language models: SMILES is a discrete sequence representation with local compositional semantics, so the model can leverage priors from pretraining about symbolic patterns, local edits, and constraint-aware generation. The paper’s deeper point is that LLM optimization behavior is strongly representation-dependent. In numerical spaces, the model has to infer geometry from tabulated histories, which is not a native strength of language pretraining. In molecular string spaces, the task is much closer to structured generation and guided rewriting.a:T5bf,From the full text provided, the continuous experiments use a fixed total budget of N=120, batch sizes 1, 2, and 4, and 2d initial points, across standard functions ranging from low to moderate dimensionality. Mr is the reasoning-enabled model, Mb is the non-reasoning baseline, and GenTok is reasoning tokens plus final answer tokens. Input tokens are deliberately excluded because they are mostly determined by the benchmark prompt and evaluation harness rather than by the model’s reasoning behavior. TES is therefore a marginal metric, unlike absolute accuracy-per-token metrics such as Per-Token Intelligence. TES greater than 1 means the accuracy gain in percentage points exceeds the generated-token multiplier; values between 0 and 1 indicate positive but weak marginal efficiency; values at or below 0 indicate that reasoning is wasteful or damaging. The paper also defines deployment-facing quantities, including Reasoning Cost Share and Deployment Cost Multiplier, to connect token behavior with cloud and on-premises cost profiles. For each prompt, it first over-generates K candidate responses from the old policy, then constructs a training group of size M. Candidates are split by correctness, and the final group approximately preserves the empirical correct/incorrect ratio: N+(x)=round(M·N~+(x)/K), with N−(x)=M−N+(x). Within each correctness class, responses are divided by relative length, producing four quadrants. QLPO favors short correct responses and long incorrect responses. The rationale follows from GRPO gradients: correct responses usually receive positive advantages and are reinforced, so selecting short correct trajectories encourages concise successful reasoning; incorrect responses usually receive negative advantages, so selecting long incorrect trajectories suppresses verbose failure modes. The reward function, advantage estimator, and downstream GRPO update remain unchanged. Stage one is Self-Pruning Training (SPT). The model first generates a full reasoning trace inside <think> tags plus a final answer. The method then identifies redundant self-verification patterns inside the trace, such as repeated checking of already established conclusions or repeated solution attempts that do not add new information. These parts are pruned, and the model is self-trained on the resulting concise trajectories. This process is iterative: a model at iteration i generates traces, those traces are pruned, and the pruned data is used to train the next model. The key point is that SPT is not simple length truncation; it aims at fine-grained removal of redundant reasoning segments. Stage two is Aha-Moment Preference Optimization (AMPO). Here the authors collect incorrect reasoning trajectories and treat them as a population of failures. A fitness function is used to select high-potential failures, with emphasis on diversity and exploratory value. From these, they construct aha-moment preference data that explicitly captures a transition from a wrong path to a correct resolution. DPO is then used to train the model to prefer such trajectories. Compared with prior work, the novelty is the combination of trajectory-level redundancy pruning and preference learning from curated wrong-to-right transitions, rather than only compressing outputs or training on correct solutions.e:T4e2,The evaluation covers mathematical reasoning and code generation. Given two checkpoints with identical pretraining and instruction tuning, differing only by a reasoning-distillation stage—an instruct model M and a reasoning model R—define a reasoning task vector τ_reason = θ_R − θ_M. Construct an “overthinking” model by extrapolating past R:

θ_Oα = θ_M + α(θ_R − θ_M), with α=0 recovering M, α=1 recovering R, and α>1 amplifying the reasoning direction.

Hypothesis: amplifying the reasoning direction increases the probability of explicit deliberation and self-reflection, destabilizing learned suppression so that audit-relevant information is more likely to be verbalized.

A practical issue is coherence degradation at large α. The paper evaluates layer-wise coefficient schemes to attenuate perturbations in sensitive regions: uniform scaling, freezing subsets of layers, linear decay across depth, and Fisher-weighted scaling (down-weighting parameters/layers deemed loss-sensitive). The consistent takeaway is that reducing perturbation in sensitive regions yields better coherence–elicitation trade-offs.

They also test composability with input-side “prefill attacks” (seeding the assistant response with a leading prefix to bypass refusal). Since prefill acts on inputs and overthinking acts on weights, they can be stacked; synergy depends on prefix style, with metacognitive/self-reflective prefixes benefiting more than direct “leak the secret” prefixes.10:T467,They evaluate on four “model organism” secret settings:
1) in-context secrets: hidden metadata in the prompt influences answers but is not verbalized in CoT;
2) learned secret words: models give clues about a target word while never saying it;
3) learned implicit beliefs: models are trained to hold a belief (e.g., user gender) but adversarially trained to deny it;
4) learned secret behaviors: models execute encoded instructions without acknowledging them.

Experiments span 2B–32B models, and the paper reports that the elicitation gains from overthinking improve with scale. They also show partial complementarity with prefill attacks, achieving the highest audit success rates for some secret types when combined.

On stability, they demonstrate that naive high-α extrapolation harms coherence, motivating the layer-wise attenuation study; Fisher-weighted / sensitivity-aware scaling tends to dominate simpler schemes.

2) Edit successful overthinking rollouts: when a successful rollout contains substantial continuation after emergence, construct an edited counterpart that preserves a conservative verified prefix up to a safe boundary around the emergence point, and replaces/truncates the remaining overthinking segment while keeping the correct answer.
3) Rewire the GRPO preference within the same group: place the original and edited trajectories in the same GRPO group and prefer the shorter edited one, so the positive learning signal is less coupled with post-answer verbosity.
Two training stabilizers are introduced:
- Prefix masking: avoid directly penalizing the shared verified prefix, preventing degradation of the reasoning needed to reach the answer.
- GClip: provide a controlled learning signal around the edit boundary to mitigate instability caused by hard edits near the cut point.
Key difference from alternatives: it is not a global length penalty (which can suppress useful reasoning), and not a post-hoc stopping controller (which does not change what RL reinforces).
