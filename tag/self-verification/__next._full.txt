1b:T4d9,SVR makes the model emit, at every turn, a solution, a discrete verdict, and a confidence estimate. The verdict is one of Correct, Incorrect, or Unsure. At inference, the trajectory stops only when the verdict is Correct and the confidence exceeds a threshold; otherwise, the next prompt includes the original problem, the previous solution, and the model’s own self-verification. Correctness labels are used during training to build rewards, but they are never inserted into refinement prompts and are not available to the inference controller. Training uses fixed-horizon trajectories with a GRPO-style objective. Per-turn solve, verification, and format scores are averaged into a trajectory return, while optimization is applied to the final completion tokens rather than treating intermediate turns as separate samples. The confidence reward uses a Brier-style calibration term, penalizes confident mistakes, and encourages recognizing both error states and correct states that are eligible for stopping. Compared with fixed-budget refinement, the main change is not longer reasoning but learned answer retention. Compared with verifier-guided methods, the deployment-time information boundary is reduced to the model’s own outputs.1c:T401,The experiments use Qwen3.5-2B on seven mathematical reasoning benchmarks, including arithmetic search, grade-school word problems, competition math, and multi-step reasoning. SVR reaches an All-7 macro-average accuracy of 0.563 with 2.99 inference turns on average. It improves over the strongest non-oracle multi-turn baseline by 7.5 percentage points and is reported to exceed the evaluated fixed-budget oracle-guided reference by 4.4 points in the complete-system comparison. Fixed-budget sweeps with the same trained policies show that no single shared stopping turn matches adaptive SVR, which supports the claim that the gain comes from instance-level retention rather than simply generating more turns. SVR also matches the aggregate accuracy of 10-sample GRPO majority voting while using roughly half as many tokens. The scope is still bounded: the evidence is mainly mathematical reasoning with a 2B-scale model, so transfer to larger models, code execution tasks, or open-ended generation remains an open question.1d:T493,1. Core observation: Correct solutions are stable fixed points of the discrete flow denoising process — re-noising a correct solution and solving it returns the original answer, while incorrect solutions drift significantly. This stability signal acts as a high-AUROC self-verifier without requiring external labels or checking tools. 2. Test-time scaling paradigm: Generate multiple candidate solutions and filter correct ones via a re-noise, re-solve, consistency-check stability pipeline to directly improve solve rates. 3. Inference-time self-conditioned refinement: Feed the complete previous prediction as conditioning at each denoising step, so a single sampling run iteratively converges to a stable fixed point, adding cross-refinement-step memory to flow models without requiring multiple independent sampling runs. 4. FlowDPO training objective: Construct paired samples of (gold solution, model-mined high-confidence incorrect solution) and use direct preference optimization to push the model away from incorrect states and toward correct ones, more suited to structured constraint satisfaction than traditional cross-entropy maximum likelihood objectives.1e:T5a9,The method, FINEVERIFY, has a proposer-verifier structure. A proposer first runs agentic search and samples multiple trajectories, producing a set of candidate answers. The verifier does not assign one global score per candidate. Instead, it first decomposes the original question into a set of checkable sub-questions, each corresponding to a requirement, attribute, or constraint in the task. Then, for every candidate answer, it verifies each sub-question against retrieved evidence and labels the result as supported, not_found, or contradicted. These per-check judgments are aggregated into an evidence-grounded verification score, and the highest-scoring candidate is selected.

Relative to best-of-N or confidence-based reranking, the design changes the problem in three ways. First, it replaces one difficult global judgment with multiple simpler local judgments. Second, all candidates are evaluated against the same explicit checklist, which makes scores more comparable across candidates. Third, the aggregation rule is explicit rather than implicit, so the final decision is inspectable.

The paper also uses a practical early-stopping rule: if a candidate receives full support on all sub-questions, sampling can stop because further compute is unlikely to change the decision. Another useful byproduct is the verification trace itself, which can be used to audit benchmark quality and diagnose why a candidate was accepted or rejected.1f:T57f,The evaluation covers four agentic search benchmarks and two models. With only 4 sampled trajectories, FINEVERIFY improves average accuracy for GPT-5-mini from 59.2% Pass@1 to 67.4%, an absolute gain of 8.2 points. For Gemini-3-flash, it improves from 71.3% to 76.9%, a gain of 5.6 points. It consistently beats standard test-time scaling baselines such as Majority Voting, Weighted Voting, Best-of-N, Solution Aggregation, and Confidence Verify.

The more important result is that it keeps improving as the number of samples grows. On BrowseComp-Plus, Figure 1 shows that with 12 samples, FINEVERIFY allows GPT-5-mini to surpass GPT-5, with a 20.5-point gain over GPT-5-mini Pass@1. That matters because it indicates the method is not just a small reranking trick at low sample counts; it converts additional test-time compute into accuracy more effectively than the baselines.

The paper also includes a verification-accuracy analysis: when the correct answer is present in the candidate pool, FINEVERIFY is more likely than coarse candidate-level scoring methods to actually select it. That isolates the source of improvement to better selection rather than better search coverage.
