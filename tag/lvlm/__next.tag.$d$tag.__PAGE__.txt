9:T4ef,The method is deliberately lightweight and training-free. Instead of training a separate verifier, it reads how much each generated token attends back to visual tokens and uses that as a proxy for visual reference usage. The scoring has two levels. First, token-level calibration: each token is scored using both its generation likelihood and its visual lookback strength. This matters because plain likelihood over-rewards generic fluent tokens, while many visually decisive words are object, attribute, or relation tokens. Second, response-level aggregation: token scores are not simply averaged. They are aggregated under a visual relevance distribution that gives more weight to tokens with stronger visual reference usage. This separation is one of the paper’s better design choices. It distinguishes whether a token is visually supported from how much that token should matter for the final response score. Compared with prior approaches, LookBack avoids extra reward models, preference labels, and additional verification passes. Compared with pure confidence scoring, it explicitly injects image sensitivity. Compared with vision-only heuristics, it still preserves the model’s own uncertainty signal rather than replacing it with raw attention alone.a:T51b,The contribution is a broad empirical study plus mechanism analysis rather than a new TTS algorithm. The focus is on zero-shot or no-extra-training test-time scaling: prompting, sampling, aggregation, and iterative refinement methods are applied directly to existing instruction-tuned LVLMs, without additional SFT or RL. The study spans multiple model families and sizes, nine TTS methods, and six benchmarks. It also compares against methods designed specifically for VLM reasoning, such as Compositional CoT, to test whether generic LLM techniques are already sufficient.

The stronger part of the paper is the analysis. Instead of reporting only accuracy, the authors examine token budgets, reasoning-chain quality, and attention behavior. Their main observation is that LVLMs have a short early window in which generated tokens attend strongly to the image and absorb visual evidence into the hidden state. After that, attention to the image decays, and later tokens operate more on the internal representation than on direct visual grounding. This explains the mixed effect of test-time scaling: moderate extra reasoning helps on tasks that genuinely require multi-step inference, but excessive reasoning can detach the model from the image and amplify language-prior-driven errors and hallucinations.b:T451,The evaluation is fairly extensive: multiple LVLMs, multiple parameter scales, nine test-time scaling methods, and six benchmarks. Several findings stand out. First, conventional LLM-style TTS methods transfer well to LVLMs and often outperform some approaches designed specifically for VLMs. Second, small models are not inherently bad at TTS. On the contrary, small but competent models such as Qwen3-VL-4B can gain the most, with improvements reported up to about 40%, reaching or even surpassing the baseline performance of a larger model without TTS, such as Qwen3-VL-32B in some settings. Third, TTS can hurt perceptual tasks, not merely because of wasted compute, but because overthinking introduces wrong assumptions and accumulates hallucinations.

The paper also evaluates reasoning-chain quality using both LLM-as-a-judge and human assessment, at global and fine-grained levels. Combined with the attention analysis, this gives a coherent explanation of when and why TTS helps or hurts.
