18:T516,The authors first treat RIM as a conditional failure mode rather than a universal outcome. They extract two activation-space directions from the base model: a reasoning direction that separates correct from incorrect reasoning, and a safety direction that separates refusal from compliance. In Qwen2.5-Instruct 3B and 7B, where RIM appears, these directions have consistently negative cosine similarity in mid-to-deep layers, suggesting that reasoning-improving updates move representations away from the safety-refusal direction. The authors then use CKA distance ratios to compare representation changes on harmful versus harmless inputs after fine-tuning, locating a compact set of safety-decision layers. Linear probes provide an important check: after fine-tuning, the model can still recognize harmful requests, but its refusal behavior collapses. Based on this diagnosis, the paper proposes the Safety-Direction Penalty, which penalizes squared hidden-state displacement along the learned safety direction during reasoning SFT. The penalty is first applied to the localized safety-decision layers; if compensatory drift appears in unpenalized layers, the same diagnostics are used to expand the scope. The method uses no safety training data, no reference policy, and adds no inference overhead.19:T584,The work has two parts. First, a diagnostic analysis. On four ordinal benchmarks and four MLLM backbones, the authors probe frozen hidden states across layers and prompt protocols to test whether ordered labels are linearly recoverable. They then go beyond probe scores and analyze how the unembedding matrix filters the ordinal direction, and how much of that direction is retained by the digit-token row subspace W_S. This turns the gap between probe-readability and generation into a concrete output-subspace issue. Second, they propose Ordinal Lens Alignment (OLA), a frozen-backbone inference-time correction method. OLA does not modify the vision encoder, LM backbone, or original unembedding. It trains lightweight lenses on K mid-to-deep decoder layers, with the lenses anchored to the digit-token row space, produces layer-wise ordinal distributions, fuses them with learned softmax weights into an instance-specific P_OLA, and then applies a discrepancy-driven correction to logits only at the answer position and only over the target digit-token set. The design choices are deliberate: multi-layer lenses because ordinal structure emerges across depth and varies by instance; W_S anchoring because the goal is to reconnect latent evidence to the native output interface rather than to maximize free probe accuracy; and target-restricted correction to avoid disturbing the full vocabulary distribution.1a:T491,The strongest evidence is in the diagnostic section. Under the most informative setup, task-designed prompt plus last-token hidden state, all backbone-dataset combinations achieve probe SRCC of at least 0.756, with peaks including 0.938 on Adience with Qwen3-VL, 0.919 on Aesthetic with Gemma-4, and 0.843 on HCI with Qwen3-VL. In contrast, the digit-token row space retains less than 1.15% of the ordinal-direction energy across all 16 model-dataset pairs, which strongly supports the output-bottleneck claim. At the behavioral level, there is a 16 to 77 absolute-point accuracy gap between hidden-state linear probes and native digit-token outputs. That is large enough to argue that the main issue is not the absence of latent ordinal evidence. In method comparisons, OLA outperforms prompt-only, hidden steering, capacity-matched baselines, and offline-lens variants, and beats the LoRA-tuned OrderChain baseline in most settings while keeping the backbone frozen. For each layer and checkpoint, an activation decomposition is fitted on a training split, using methods such as SVD, ICA, NMF, SemiNMF, or sparse dictionaries. The decomposition provides codes, a dictionary, and a reconstruction baseline. A conservative candidate width Kmax is estimated from training activations using a two-nearest-neighbor intrinsic-dimension estimate with clipping. On a calibration split, the method learns a sparse soft mask with temperature-controlled gates, optimized with Adam under a preservation objective plus a sparsity penalty. On the test split, the masked reconstruction is inserted back into the original model graph. The paper compares four preservation targets: geometric reconstruction, fixed-head decodability through an affine translator, native downstream preservation through the remaining model layers measured by KL to the original predictions, and checkpoint transfer through a learned affine alignment from an earlier checkpoint to the final checkpoint.
