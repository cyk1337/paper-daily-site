18:T5b8,The method has three layers. First, formalization: the benchmark score is written as a composition of stage-specific functions rather than a static dataset-plus-metric object. This makes it possible to localize failure to a specific stage. Second, auditing: under a common framework, the authors inspect eight benchmark implementations and identify 15 recurring failure modes, including prompt formatting, stop sequences, token budgets, handling of reasoning spans, output parsing, label aliases, binary versus partial-credit scoring, invalid-response treatment, and aggregation. Importantly, they do not stop at anecdotal examples. They run controlled perturbations, changing one pipeline field at a time while holding the rest fixed, and measure the effect on scores and rankings. Third, standardization: they build a harness that exposes and standardizes nine configuration fields wherever task semantics allow, so that differences caused by benchmark-specific evaluator choices can be separated from differences caused by the task itself. Two analyses are especially useful. PCA on the 23-task by 10-model score matrix shows that the first principal component explains 95.25% of the variance, suggesting that many tasks mostly measure the same broad capability axis. Kendall’s tau-b on semantically similar task pairs then shows that rankings can still disagree sharply because scoring conventions differ, especially around partial credit and alias handling.19:T419,The audit covers 8 benchmarks, 23 tasks, 48,662 questions, and 10 models spanning proprietary, open-weight, and cybersecurity-specialized systems. The most striking case study is RedSage-Bench: using “\n” as a stop sequence causes Qwen3.6 to terminate inside its reasoning preamble before producing an answer. Switching to EOS termination, allocating enough generation budget, and stripping the reasoning span before extraction recovers 85.9 percentage points. More broadly, the paper reports that a single pipeline choice can shift a model’s score by over 80 points. Under the standardized harness, 9 of 10 models move by at least three ranks on at least one benchmark. At the task-structure level, PCA finds that the first component explains 95.25% of the variance in the 23×10 score matrix, yet two semantically similar task pairs from CTI-Bench and AthenaBench have weak rank agreement, with Kendall’s tau-b of 0.29 and 0.24. The most informative ablations are the stage-level perturbations rather than any single aggregate leaderboard.1a:T563,The method has three parts. First, the authors build a controlled coordination substrate, ET-MCP, an ephemeral trace store exposed through MCP. It stores typed negative-knowledge events, especially failed_path events, and uses a simple TF-IDF ranker for retrieval. This keeps the memory mechanism intentionally simple so the comparison focuses on protocol effects rather than retrieval sophistication. Second, they implement two reader variants on top of the same store. In pull, the agent explicitly calls trace.query when peer information may help. In intercept, the framework automatically checks the store on every tool call and prepends a [PEER-WARNING] block when the current (name, arguments) matches a peer failure event. Because both variants share the same backend state, the comparison isolates who reads and when. Third, they define a paired measurement protocol around configuration equivalence rather than identity replay. They audit system prompt, tool list, sampling parameters, and message-array construction by code inspection plus a SHA-256 byte audit, but do not claim byte-identical replay. They also introduce pass_active^k, a pass@k metric restricted to tasks where the coordination store is non-empty at trial start. This separates overall deployability from the narrower question of whether coordination helps when it actually has an opportunity to help.1b:T590,The main experiments use τ²-bench retail with Claude Haiku 4.5 at T=0, with two seeds of n=100 tasks per protocol. At the top-line level, pull, intercept, and no_coord all achieve trial-1 success of 0.54, and all paired sign tests fail to reject, with p=1.0. The key contrast is the clean trial-0 comparison between no_coord and intercept, which are configuration-equivalent. For seed 1, the paired outcomes are 21/11/68, giving a signed gap of +10 percentage points. For seed 2, they are 13/13/74, giving 0 points. Pooled across both seeds, the gap is +5 points with Wilson CI [-2, +12], Bonferroni-corrected p=0.711, so not significant. The largest single-seed contrast is pull vs intercept at seed 1: 27/9/64, or +18 points, corrected p=0.012. But it fails to reproduce at seed 2, where the contrast becomes 18/21/61, or -3 points. The pooled gap is only +7.5 points, with an upper Wilson bound around 15 points. The authors summarize the observed configuration-equivalent envelope for this benchmark/model/harness as [-3, +18] points, with pooled upper Wilson CI ≲15 points. They also run small transfer probes on Haiku-airline (n=30) and Sonnet-retail (n=30), showing that the floor is local and does not transfer cleanly. Another important empirical point is power: only 8 to 17 informative coordination-active pairs remain after ties, which explains why many coordination benchmarks are structurally underpowered.1c:T62f,The main contribution has two parts. First, the authors define an operator-controllable pairwise-disagreement rate, or rank-flip rate. Instead of reporting score variance, they ask a more decision-relevant question: within a pre-declared, commit-stamped envelope of plausible evaluation configurations, can model A switch from outperforming model B to underperforming it? That unit matches how benchmark results are actually consumed. Second, they instantiate this idea with a tiered configuration envelope. The core tier reflects common benchmark and harness defaults; the stress tier adds still-plausible but more aggressive settings such as T4 CoT and temperature 0.7 decoding. Empirically, they exhaustively evaluate three 7–9B open-weight instruction-tuned models on five alignment-related benchmarks over a 612-cell grid inside one harness, then compute exact rank-flip statistics. They also use LightGBM plus SHAP for axis-level attribution, to localize which evaluator choices drive instability. Separately, they run a cross-package case study with lm-evaluation-harness, HELM-lite, and Inspect AI under a nominally matched anchor configuration. The important point is not just that scores differ, but that the evaluated candidate object is often not the same across packages. For example, TruthfulQA may be scored as log-probability over the canonical MC1 answer set, as concat-then-rank multiple-choice joint scoring, or as log-probability over answer letters with a separate label map. That distinction is handled carefully and is one of the stronger parts of the paper.1d:T552,The empirical scope is intentionally narrow: consumer-grade 16GB-class NF4 deployment, three 7–9B open-weight instruction-tuned models, five alignment/safety benchmarks, and one fixed item subset per benchmark. The authors explicitly avoid claiming a population-level statement about all LLM evaluation. Within that envelope, the result is strong: on every benchmark tested, configuration choice alone can flip the pairwise verdict. XSTest is the clearest example, where all six possible orderings among Qwen, Mistral, and Yi are reachable within the tested envelope. In variance accounting, implementation/configuration main effects explain at least as much aggregate variance as model identity on BBQ, ToxiGen, and XSTest; TruthfulQA is the main exception, where model identity dominates more clearly.
