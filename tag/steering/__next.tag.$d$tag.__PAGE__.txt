在每一层，作者取最终 prompt token 的 residual activation，分别对 analogy 与 literal 两组求均值，做 difference-in-means，得到该层的 Analogy Vector。推理时在指定层把该向量按系数 α 加到残差流上，观察输出风格是否朝类比解释偏移。这里有两个技术点比较关键。第一，作者系统比较了七组 matched base/instruct 模型，覆盖 Gemma-2、Llama-3.1、Qwen-2.5，参数规模 2B 到 32B，用同一套提取与干预流程来比较“方向是否存在”和“方向是否可用”。第二，他们发现高系数干预会出现 mode collapse，原因不是语义方向本身失效，而是 lexical entanglement：向量里混入了明显的词汇触发成分，尤其是 “Imagine” token 的 unembedding 方向约占总范数的 10%，会把模型硬推向模板化开头。为此作者把向量投影到与该 token 方向正交的子空间，范数只下降 1%–5%，说明主体信号仍是语义性的，而不是单纯的词触发。

更有意思的是作者提出的“behavioral accessibility”框架。不是只看能否提取出方向，而是看该方向注入后，模型分布是否真的发生可传播、可利用的变化。这里他们用两类信号来刻画：一类是输出质量和 analogy score 的提升；另一类是干预前后 token 分布的 KL divergence。后者被作者解释为 wiring diagnostic：即使模型最终没生成高质量类比，只要 KL 明显上升，说明这条方向至少接入了生成路径；如果 KL 很低，则更像是几何上有痕迹，但没有被行为电路有效读取。a:T926,The method follows the representation-engineering line of work, but moves from near-binary behaviors such as refusal to a continuous generative style. The authors build a paired dataset of 1,000 concepts, each eliciting both a literal explanation and an analogical explanation. The source is ELI5-style community QA data, filtered in two stages with an external LLM: first to keep only mechanism-like concepts suitable for structural analogy, then to assign each item to one of eight domains for near-balanced sampling. The final split is 600/100/300 for train/validation/test.

For each layer, they collect the residual activation at the final prompt token and compute a difference-in-means vector between analogy and literal examples. This yields a layer-wise Analogy Vector. At inference time, they add the vector with coefficient alpha to the residual stream before the attention and MLP blocks, then measure whether the output shifts toward analogical explanation style.

Two technical details matter. First, they run the same extraction-and-steering pipeline on seven matched base/instruct pairs from Gemma-2, Llama-3.1, and Qwen-2.5, spanning 2B to 32B parameters. This lets them compare not just whether the direction exists, but whether it is behaviorally usable. Second, they identify a concrete failure mode at high steering strength: lexical entanglement. The vector partially aligns with the unembedding direction of the token “Imagine,” which accounts for about 10% of the vector norm and can force templated generations. They remove this by orthogonalizing the steering vector against that token direction. The norm drops by only 1%–5%, suggesting the main signal is semantic rather than lexical.

The broader contribution is the behavioral accessibility framework. The paper argues that extracting a direction is not enough; one must test whether the intervention actually enters the model’s generation circuitry. To measure this, they use both output-side quality metrics and KL divergence between steered and unsteered token distributions. They interpret KL as a wiring diagnostic: even if the final generations are not yet good analogies, a substantial KL shift indicates that the direction is connected to the decoding pathway. Low KL suggests a representation that is geometrically present but behaviorally inert.b:T5d8,The central empirical result is that the Analogy Vector can be extracted from every base model tested, but accessibility varies continuously with instruction exposure rather than following a clean base-versus-instruct split. Gemma-2 base models, where pretraining and instruction tuning are more cleanly separated, show flat steering landscapes and much lower KL divergence than their instruct counterparts. The direction is there, but it barely moves behavior. Qwen-2.5 base models, where instruction-like data is more entangled with pretraining, already show partial accessibility. Llama-3.1 sits between these two regimes.

On the held-out set of 300 prompts, the paper reports that steering Gemma-2-9B-IT with the extracted direction improves the harmonic mean of literal quality and analogy quality from 3.22 to 3.94, a gain of about 22%. Starting from a set of ‘positive’ SAE features correlated with the target behavior (faithfulness), it builds an interaction graph among these features using a small development set. Nodes are candidate positive features; edges represent activation/causal dependencies across layers (and potentially within-layer interactions), motivated by the observation that a strongly steering feature often requires upstream features to activate it. KPI then defines key features via graph structure—high indegree features are treated as bottlenecks/aggregators—and constructs key paths by anchoring on a key layer with strong steering effect and tracing back through preceding layers’ key features. Steering is applied only on features along these key paths, aiming to remove redundant features that dilute the intervention. SAEs are trained on ~250M tokens (Emilia) with dictionary size 16,384 and k=50 active features per token, across multiple layers.
A key contribution is modality-aware auto-interpretation for mixed sequences. For each SAE feature, they collect top-activating positions and route evidence based on whether activations occur in the text-prefix segment or the speech-token segment: text evidence is a marked token window; audio evidence is a 1-second waveform clip centered on the activating speech token; mixed features receive both. Labels are generated by an LLM (Gemini 3.0 Pro) and validated via a held-out detection-style protocol (shuffled positives/negatives; AUROC and balanced accuracy).
For causal tests, they steer generation through the SAE latent space: encode residual states at speech-token positions, shift selected feature activations by a signed, normalized amount with strength α, decode back into the residual stream, and continue generation (leaving text inputs and speech prompts unchanged).e:T41f,Layer sweeps show a strong depth-dependent modality shift: early/mid layers contain many mixed features; layers 16–20 are dominated by audio-modal features; the final hidden state reverts to a mostly text-modal subspace. First, the authors use the logit lens to inspect intermediate next-token distributions during transliteration. They find that when the model is producing a non-Latin output, middle-to-upper layers often transiently predict romanized subwords before committing to the final script. In other words, the model often does not move directly from source script to target script. It appears to pass through an intermediate representation aligned with Latinized forms. The paper turns this into a quantitative phenomenon, latent romanization, and studies it across scripts and models rather than relying on a single anecdotal example.

Second, the paper analyzes representations of the same language written in different scripts. Script identity becomes increasingly linearly separable in deeper layers. Based on this, the authors construct simple linear steering directions that flip the output script while largely preserving semantic content. Two points matter here. One is that script choice can be manipulated with a low-complexity linear intervention, suggesting that script identity is explicitly encoded in later layers. The other is a strong directional asymmetry: steering toward non-Latin scripts is more reliable than steering toward Latin. This supports the paper’s main hypothesis that Latin is not a compact target cluster but a more diffuse representational region.

Third, the authors localize a small set of late-layer attention heads that causally mediate script generation. With activation patching on only a few heads, they can redirect the output script on natural prompts, and the same heads transfer across unrelated languages and writing systems. This suggests that script choice is not uniformly distributed across the network. Instead, part of it is concentrated in a small number of routing components in later layers. The asymmetry appears again at the mechanistic level: non-Latin generation depends on compact, identifiable mechanisms, whereas Latin generation is more distributed and harder to control through a small intervention.

From a pretraining perspective, the paper is effectively arguing for a joint effect of data frequency, tokenization convenience, and representational sharing. Latin script is more common in pretraining corpora and often easier for tokenizers to encode cleanly, so it becomes a natural shared substrate. Non-Latin scripts then require additional late-stage routing to write the shared semantic content back into a specific surface form.10:T783,A representative qualitative example uses Llama-2-13B to visualize transliteration from Malayalam to Devanagari. The model choice is deliberate: its tokenizer makes Devanagari visually legible in logit-lens plots, whereas more byte-fragmented tokenizers such as Llama-3 obscure the transition. That detail is informative on its own, because it shows how tokenization affects the observability of internal mechanisms.

The main empirical findings come in several forms. First, quantitative logit-lens analysis supports latent romanization: romanized pieces of the target word appear in intermediate layers before the final target script emerges. Second, representations of the same language in different scripts become more separable in deeper layers, implying that script identity is not fully disentangled from the start but is progressively made explicit later in the network. Third, linear steering can reliably flip output script while preserving meaning to a large extent, and the steering directions transfer to unseen languages and writing systems. However, the transfer is asymmetric: moving toward non-Latin scripts is easier than moving back toward Latin. Fourth, mechanistic probing identifies a small number of late attention heads whose patching is sufficient to change output script on natural prompts, and these heads transfer across languages, suggesting a relatively general script-routing mechanism.

The paper is lighter on headline benchmark numbers and heavier on consistency of evidence across analyses. A useful way to read the experiments is to keep the paper’s central geometric picture in mind: non-Latin scripts form compact, well-separated clusters, while Latin occupies a wider and more diffuse region. Many of the observed asymmetries follow from that picture: why non-Latin steering is easier, why a few heads can write into non-Latin scripts, and why steering back to Latin is less stable.11:T556,A good skim is to read it in the order of phenomenon, representation, then mechanism.
