18:T546,BEST-RQ-2 makes two core changes to the original BEST-RQ design. First, it replaces the Conformer encoder with a Vision Transformer operating on patchified log-mel spectrograms. Unlike convolutional Conformer layers that rely on dense, contiguous input layout, ViT uses position-aware self-attention that natively supports arbitrary subsets of input patches, making encoder-predictor decomposition feasible. Second, drawing design inspiration from Audio-JEPA, masked prediction is split into two separate stages: a context ViT encoder processes only unmasked patches to produce contextualized representations, and a lightweight ViT predictor combines these context representations with position-aware mask embeddings to predict discrete codes for masked positions. The predictor is only used during pretraining and discarded entirely at inference, so downstream compute and model size remain identical to the original BEST-RQ. The core design choice from BEST-RQ is retained: discrete targets are generated via a frozen random projection quantizer with no learned codebook, enabling stable cross-entropy training. A controlled baseline, BEST-RQ (ViT), uses the same ViT encoder but keeps the original single-stage in-place masked prediction pipeline, to isolate performance contributions from the architecture swap versus the two-stage decomposition.19:T626,The method starts from log-mel spectrograms, split into patches. Instead of using generic augmentations or MAE-style random masking, it creates two positive views by independently masking along the time and frequency axes. The resulting views are complementary rather than near-duplicates. This is the central design choice. It reduces shared local redundancy between the two views while preserving broader temporal-spectral patterns that still identify the same utterance.

Only visible patches are fed into the encoder, which lowers memory and compute, similar in spirit to masked autoencoding but with a different objective. The training loss is utterance-level contrastive learning in a shared embedding space. The paper positions itself against three families of prior work: waveform contrastive methods such as CPC and wav2vec that focus on short-range temporal views; masked spectrogram modeling methods that reconstruct missing content from local context; and direct reuse of SpecAugment, whose zero-masking works well as supervised regularization but is not necessarily a good way to generate informative positive pairs for SSL.

A useful part of the paper is the representation-level analysis. The authors measure effective rank of learned embeddings and argue that when positive pairs preserve too much local structure, the model tends toward dimension collapse. Structured time-frequency masking increases effective rank, suggesting that the model is forced to distribute information across a richer feature subspace rather than relying on a few easy local cues.1a:T5c6,The evaluation covers AudioSet, ESC-50, Speech Commands, and EnvSDD, targeting general audio understanding, transfer across domains, and robustness under distribution shift such as environmental sound deepfake detection. The most informative results are in the ablations. First, masking structure matters: unstructured random masking is better aligned with reconstruction objectives, while independent time and frequency masking works better for contrastive learning. Second, mask ratio has a clear trade-off: too little masking makes the two views overly similar, too much masking destroys semantics, and an intermediate regime works best. Third, effective rank tracks downstream quality reasonably well, supporting the paper’s collapse argument. Fourth, when the pretrained encoder is aligned with an LLM for audio-language tasks, performance improves further, suggesting that the learned utterance-level representation is compatible with multimodal interfaces.
