1a:T472,The analysis starts from a simplified residual recurrence, h_{n+1}=h_n+ε W φ(h_n), where the same matrix W is reused at every step. After unrolling, h_N=h_0+ε∑r_n. The squared norm contains a quadratic term, ε²/d · ||∑r_n||². In an ordinary non-shared residual stack, different residual branches are approximately independent, so this term grows like Θ(ε²N), which motivates ε=1/√N. In a looped network, the same W generates all updates. The activations are produced by the same recurrence, the residual directions remain positively correlated, and the quadratic term grows like Θ(ε²N²). Keeping the residual stream bounded therefore requires εN=O(1), i.e. ε=1/N. The same accumulation appears in the optimization analysis: the output change after one optimizer step scales as ηεN, so ε=1/N makes the stable learning rate roughly independent of N. For a practical block with L unique layers repeated N times, the paper separates two effects: a linear N factor from within-layer reuse and a √L factor from across-layer random-walk accumulation. This yields ε=λ/(N√L) and a learning-rate law η≲1/(λ√L).1b:T4f4,The framework defines three measurements. The first is scaling-law fit error, which evaluates how well a functional form predicts the optimal hyperparameter across scale. The second is a transfer robustness exponent κ, which measures whether errors made during small-to-large extrapolation become more or less harmful at larger scale. The third is asymptotic loss degradation R(∞), which checks whether a parameterization obtains cleaner transfer only by sacrificing final loss. The authors then express standard parameterization and μP in a common GPT-pretraining form and isolate four differences: the embedding learning rate changes from Θ(1/n) to Θ(1), the final-layer initialization variance changes from Θ(1/n) to Θ(1/n²), the LayerNorm learning rate changes from Θ(1/n) to Θ(1), and attention scaling changes from 1/√d to 1/d. Too small an embedding learning rate throttles representation learning and can trigger instability.1c:T40f,The paper addresses a precise but important failure mode in μP/Complete-P: standard μP checks can appear to pass for grouped-query attention (GQA), yet learning-rate transfer across widths still breaks in practice. The goal is not to patch this empirically, but to explain why the existing theory becomes insufficient for GQA and to derive a parameterization that restores transfer. The issue has two layers. First, prior spectral-μP arguments treated spectral-norm conditions as a sufficient route to feature learning, while the operational checks used in implementations were weaker; this leaves room for architectures that pass coordinate checks without satisfying the stronger operator-scale conditions needed for transfer. First, the paper reformulates the spectral-μP perspective around operator norms more directly. Instead of starting from a weaker feature-learning definition and then showing that spectral conditions imply it, the authors elevate operator-norm scaling conditions to the center of the framework. This lets them derive Complete-P-style prescriptions for depth and weight decay without leaning on a lazy-training fallback.

Second, they introduce the expected operator norm to replace the ordinary spectral norm when matrices are rank-degenerate. The standard spectral norm captures the largest possible deformation over all unit vectors. That is appropriate for full-rank random matrices, where the extremal scale is asymptotically representative. But for GQA-like low-rank matrices, the extremal directions occupy measure-zero sets relative to the i.i.d. input distribution encountered during training. The expected operator norm instead measures the average deformation under a chosen input distribution, taken in the paper to be Gaussian i.i.d. This preserves the usual scaling for full-rank random matrices while correcting the mismatch for low-rank operators.

Third, using this revised norm and operator-centric framework, the paper derives μP scaling rules for GQA. Because GQA shares key/value heads across multiple query groups, the effective rank, output scale, and update scale of the relevant projections differ from standard multi-head attention. The authors work through these differences and derive the corresponding initialization, learning-rate, depth, and weight-decay scalings.
