19:T553,Core method is Expert Tying: share gate/up/down projection weights of all MoE expert FFNs across a group of g consecutive Transformer layers via Python-level pointer aliasing. Only optimizer logic needs adjustment to correctly accumulate gradients from tied parameters across multiple use sites; no other training or inference infrastructure changes are required. Per-layer routers, attention projections, and RMSNorm parameters remain independent, so hidden states still pass through g distinct layer operators. Controlled component ablations confirm the feasibility of this design: per-layer attention (not routing) is the primary source of layer distinctiveness, so sharing the large expert FFN parameter pool incurs minimal quality cost, while sharing attention causes order-of-magnitude larger degradation. Two key design rules are validated: 1) leave the first 2 and last 2 layers untied (2+2 prelude/coda), which delivers the single largest architectural performance gain; 2) heterogeneous width expansion: reinvest parameters saved by tying into more experts in middle tied layers, which consistently outperforms untied baselines at iso-parameter count. Unlike prior MoE cross-layer sharing work that either ties full layer blocks or a single fixed component, this work provides controlled, cross-architecture, multi-granularity analysis of sharing costs.1a:T639,The paper has two technical layers. First is the experimental design. The authors train four prelude-recur-coda variants with recurrence counts r in {1, 2, 4, 8}, where r=1 is the non-looped baseline. All models have 20 effective layers. Prelude and coda are fixed at 2 layers each, while the recurrent block has 16/r unique layers and is executed r times. This keeps effective depth and, at matched width, per-token training and inference FLOPs nearly constant across architectures. As a result, the main changing factor is parameter sharing, not depth or token-wise compute. That is a cleaner setup than fixed-parameter recurrence sweeps, which entangle sharing, depth, and inference cost. Second is the joint scaling law: L = E + A (N_once + r^φ N_rec)^(-α) + B D^(-β). Here N_once denotes parameters used once in the prelude and coda, N_rec denotes the unique parameters in the shared recurrent block, and φ measures how much recurrence contributes in parameter-equivalent terms. φ=1 would mean each recurrence is fully equivalent to adding the same amount of unique parameters; φ=0 would mean recurrence adds no effective capacity at all. This formulation lets the authors fit all 116 runs jointly instead of fitting separate Chinchilla laws and comparing them indirectly. They also fit per-architecture Chinchilla laws to study how compute-optimal width and token allocation shift with recurrence. Higher-r models prefer wider widths and fewer tokens per parameter, but because wider models are more expensive per token, they end up training on fewer total tokens at fixed compute.1b:T4c4,The study includes 116 pretraining runs over six compute budgets from 4.64×10^17 to 2.15×10^19 FLOPs, about a 50× span, with r in {1, 2, 4, 8}. The central result is a joint fit with φ=0.46 and R²=0.997. That number is easy to interpret: recurrence helps, but much less than linearly. One concrete example from the paper is that at r=4 and matched training FLOPs, the looped model reaches only the validation loss of a non-looped model with 42% fewer parameters. A 410M r=4 model performs roughly like a 580M non-looped model, while costing about as much to train as a 1B non-looped model under matched token-wise FLOPs. So looped models save parameter memory, but not enough effective capacity to match the compute they consume. The downstream suite spans five axes. Parametric-knowledge tasks track the validation-loss gap most clearly. Open-book tasks narrow as validation loss improves. Reasoning-related axes remain near measurement noise at these development-scale budgets, so the paper does not find strong evidence that looping yields a measurable reasoning advantage in this regime. That negative result is useful: it cautions against attributing too much to recurrence before the basic loss gap is closed.1c:T42e,For a quick but useful read, focus on three parts. First, the methodology section, especially why the paper uses iso-depth and matched per-token FLOPs rather than a fixed-parameter recurrence sweep. That is what makes φ interpretable. Second, read the joint scaling-law definition and the main fit results, paying attention to the meaning of φ and the contrast between the free-φ fit and the φ=1 hypothesis in the main figure. Third, read the per-architecture Chinchilla fits and compute-optimal allocation trends; that part is the most actionable if you actually want to train looped LMs. In 25–35 minutes, the introduction, methodology, main scaling-law figures, and downstream conclusions are enough to capture the paper. The deeper questions are whether φ changes with training recipe, optimizer, positional scheme, or input injection; whether the exponent is architecture-specific beyond prelude-recur-coda; and whether stronger state constraints, adaptive compute, or latent-reasoning style recurrence can push φ upward rather than accepting 0.46 as fixed.1d:T423,LoopLM reuses a stack of L transformer layers for t recurrent steps (shared weights), effectively deepening the computation graph without increasing parameter count. A prediction head is attached at each step, enabling multi-step supervision.

Adaptive compute is implemented via a learned early-exit (or equivalently, a learned weighting over step-wise losses). The key training issue is collapse: without constraints, the model either exits too early (cheap but weak) or always runs the maximum steps (strong but no adaptivity). The proposed fix is an entropy-regularized objective with a uniform prior over exit steps to force unbiased exploration of depths early in training, followed by a focused stage that tunes the compute–quality trade-off and lets the model allocate steps based on input difficulty.

For interpretation, the paper positions recurrence as iterative refinement in latent space, and backs it with controlled studies that aim to disentangle “knowledge storage” from “knowledge manipulation” (composition, multi-hop reasoning).1e:T52e,Scale: pretraining up to 7.7T tokens; open-sourced Ouro models at 1.4B and 2.6B parameters.

Efficiency claim: 1.4B/2.6B LoopLMs reportedly match roughly 4B/8B standard (non-looped) transformers on many benchmarks, implying ~2–3× parameter efficiency under their evaluation protocol.

Ablations: varying loop steps shows generally increasing capability with more steps, but training can be unstable due to exit-policy collapse; entropy regularization plus the two-stage schedule stabilizes depth usage and preserves peak performance while enabling early exit on easier inputs.

Mechanistic results: the paper reports similar “knowledge storage density” between looped and non-looped models (on the order of ~2 bits/parameter), while showing larger gains on tasks requiring fact composition and multi-hop reasoning—supporting the “manipulation over storage” explanation.

Safety/faithfulness: lower harmfulness on HEx-PHI, improving with more recurrent steps (including extrapolated steps). It also argues latent iterative traces align better with final answers than explicit CoT, suggesting less post-hoc rationalization.
