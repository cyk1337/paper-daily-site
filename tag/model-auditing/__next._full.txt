Once the atlas is trained and curated, a new target model is attached by fitting only a linear target decoder into the fixed atlas coordinates. This produces two audit views. The atlas channel reads the target through already-interpreted panel features and enables feature-by-feature comparison against the reference models. The residual channel trains a separate sparse dictionary on what the atlas fails to reconstruct, namely the residual h* - D* z_A. This differs from sparse crosscoders, which typically place shared and target-specific features in one dictionary and separate them later using decoder-norm asymmetries. Here, residual membership is architectural: the feature lives in a dictionary trained only on panel-uncovered residuals. Attachment FVU/KL gives a scalar measure of how far the target sits outside the panel, while the residual SAE provides the inventory of responsible features.1b:T609,The experiments use five 7–9B instruction-tuned open models: Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Gemma-2-9B, and OLMo-2-7B. The authors train leave-one-out atlases and audit held-out Mistral and Qwen targets. In the controlled setting, they inject three LoRA hidden objectives into the held-out targets: a fictional dessert brand, Ptolemaic epicycles, and Flying Spaghetti Monster advocacy. Across all six objective-target pairs, a runtime intervention reduces the planted-objective rate to zero while matched benign controls remain at zero. The residual dictionary recovers the injected mechanism as the top-ranked latent in every paired run, with mean rank 1.0 and rank standard deviation 0 across the reported seeds and target families. On the Mistral side, per-target SAE and pairwise crosscoder baselines do not recover the mechanism in the same head-to-head comparison. In the panel-relative Qwen audit, Qwen-2.5 attaches to Atlas B at roughly 7 times the in-panel KL and more than 15 standard deviations above the in-panel value across three atlas-training seeds. The residual channel localizes the gap to continuation clusters involving emergency-power restrictions, media-suspension framing during unrest, and law-and-order justifications that down-weight individual rights. Intervening along the same cluster monotonically changes political-keyword refusal and law-and-order framing rates, while 100 matched out-of-domain controls are unchanged. The authors frame this as a panel-relative finding, not a model-intrinsic claim.1c:T5c8,The method is task-vector extrapolation. Given two checkpoints with identical pretraining and instruction tuning, differing only by a reasoning-distillation stage—an instruct model M and a reasoning model R—define a reasoning task vector τ_reason = θ_R − θ_M. Construct an “overthinking” model by extrapolating past R:

θ_Oα = θ_M + α(θ_R − θ_M), with α=0 recovering M, α=1 recovering R, and α>1 amplifying the reasoning direction.

Hypothesis: amplifying the reasoning direction increases the probability of explicit deliberation and self-reflection, destabilizing learned suppression so that audit-relevant information is more likely to be verbalized.

A practical issue is coherence degradation at large α. The paper evaluates layer-wise coefficient schemes to attenuate perturbations in sensitive regions: uniform scaling, freezing subsets of layers, linear decay across depth, and Fisher-weighted scaling (down-weighting parameters/layers deemed loss-sensitive). The consistent takeaway is that reducing perturbation in sensitive regions yields better coherence–elicitation trade-offs.

They also test composability with input-side “prefill attacks” (seeding the assistant response with a leading prefix to bypass refusal). Since prefill acts on inputs and overthinking acts on weights, they can be stacked; synergy depends on prefix style, with metacognitive/self-reflective prefixes benefiting more than direct “leak the secret” prefixes.1d:T467,They evaluate on four “model organism” secret settings:
1) in-context secrets: hidden metadata in the prompt influences answers but is not verbalized in CoT;
2) learned secret words: models give clues about a target word while never saying it;
3) learned implicit beliefs: models are trained to hold a belief (e.g., user gender) but adversarially trained to deny it;
4) learned secret behaviors: models execute encoded instructions without acknowledging them.

Experiments span 2B–32B models, and the paper reports that the elicitation gains from overthinking improve with scale. They also show partial complementarity with prefill attacks, achieving the highest audit success rates for some secret types when combined.

On stability, they demonstrate that naive high-α extrapolation harms coherence, motivating the layer-wise attenuation study; Fisher-weighted / sensitivity-aware scaling tends to dominate simpler schemes.
