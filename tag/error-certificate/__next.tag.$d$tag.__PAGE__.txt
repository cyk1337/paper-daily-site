First, an impossibility result. In a single-head, single-step attention setup, the authors formalize the online sigma-field available to the serving system: retained KV pairs, query history, scores, and any statistics computed from them. They prove that no estimator measurable with respect to this information can consistently estimate the true eviction-induced attention-output error under deterministic top-k-style selection. The proof is constructive: keep the retained state fixed, alter the evicted values, and the true error can change arbitrarily while every observable quantity remains unchanged.

Second, they restore identifiability via randomized design. The proposed mechanism is certainty-plus-Poisson eviction: high-score items are kept deterministically, while the tail is sampled with known inclusion probabilities π_i. Once the sampling design is known, design-based survey inference becomes available. The key implementation trick is elegant: Hájek correction is realized inside attention by adding log(1/π_i) to the logits of retained tail tokens, which performs inverse-probability reweighting through the softmax normalization. They then derive a retained-set variance estimator of Sen–Yates–Grundy type for the linearized error, and wrap it with an empirical-Bernstein radius to obtain a per-step error certificate. An e-process extension is also discussed to move toward time-uniform validity over the autoregressive trajectory.

Two aspects stand out. It is training-free and lightweight: no new parameters, no retraining, and only scalar work over the sampled tail. And its target is attribution rather than generic failure prediction: it aims to tell whether cache compression caused the damage, not whether the answer will be wrong for any reason.b:T84e,The empirical study is unusually disciplined: the authors pre-register seven falsifiable claims and report the failures openly.

At the attention level, the certificate achieves about 0.97 empirical coverage, certificate-error correlation exceeds 0.94, and there is no accuracy penalty relative to deterministic top-k baselines. They also directly measure the silent-failure phenomenon: retained-attention entropy for H2O- and SnapKV-style deterministic eviction gives AUC around 0.50 for predicting eviction-induced failures, essentially chance. Other online self-signals such as margin behave similarly; evicted score mass reaches at most 0.73, while the randomized certificate reaches roughly 0.77–0.86 on the same attribution-oriented yardstick.

On synthetic long-context tasks, the signal transfers to task level: all 16 model-task cells show positive certificate-failure AUC, with mean 0.836. This is the regime where compression damage is a dominant source of failure, so the certificate remains informative.

The real-workload results are more nuanced and more useful. Three of the seven pre-registered claims fail. On LongBench-style question-aware eviction at 25%–50% budgets and 6k/16k contexts, eviction is nearly free, so overall task failure is dominated by intrinsic task difficulty rather than cache damage. In that regime, mean output log-probability, a free baseline, beats the certificate at predicting whether the final answer is wrong. Certificate-gated escalation from 25% to 50% budget also does not help. What survives is attribution: among failed examples, the certificate separates eviction-induced from inherent failures with AUC about 0.75 and 0.73 at the two scales, whereas output confidence is at or below chance because it conflates task difficulty with cache damage. In recomputation scheduling, certificate-gated reruns outperform random and confidence-based gating at matched compute; confidence gating can even underperform random because it spends budget on examples the model would fail anyway.
