18:T655,FleetSieve treats profiling as downstream decision identification. For each workload-class/configuration cell, it maintains intervals for sustainable capacity and tail latency rather than a single estimate. It then solves two fleet allocations under the same resource constraints and lexicographic objective: a conservative allocation using lower capacity and upper tail bounds, and an optimistic allocation using upper capacity and lower tail bounds. The objective first enforces minimum fulfillment floors for critical classes when feasible, then maximizes the minimum satisfaction ratio, then total served goodput, and finally reduces fragmentation.

The key idea is the acquisition rule. FleetSieve does not measure the cell with the largest uncertainty, nor does it try to cover the grid uniformly. It measures the configuration expected to reduce disagreement between the conservative and optimistic downstream allocations. In other words, a cell with high uncertainty is not important if no plausible outcome would change the fleet decision. Conversely, a cell becomes important if resolving it could flip SLO feasibility or alter resource allocation for a critical workload.

The paper also introduces a conditional decision certificate with three states: Certified-Feasible, Undecided, and Certified-Infeasible. The wording is careful. The certificate is not distribution-free or unconditional; it is valid only under the empirical uncertainty model used to bound the unknown performance table. That restraint is a good sign: the method is presented as an engineering decision tool, not as a universal guarantee.19:T491,The evaluation is intentionally narrow and concrete: one 31B open-weight decoder model, one H100 node, FP8, a vLLM-based serving stack, speculative decoding disabled, and candidate TP degrees {2, 4, 8}. Each profiling cell is measured for 300 seconds. The main metric is how many profiling GPU-seconds are needed to recover the oracle aggregate fleet decision.

The headline result is modest but real: on the fixed H100 measurement grid, FleetSieve reaches the oracle aggregate decision with 22,200 GPU-seconds, a 6.9% reduction relative to uniform profiling. The paper is refreshingly explicit that gains are selective rather than universal: the reduction is larger for Chat and absent for Code. That matters because it clarifies when the method helps. If unresolved cells can still change the global allocation, decision-aware profiling is useful. If the allocation is effectively determined early, the extra machinery may not buy much. The experiments also show why joint modeling of capacity and tail latency is necessary: throughput-only profiling can certify configurations that look efficient but violate the actual SLO, which then corrupts fleet-level planning.1a:T42e,They define a single-axis TSP group (size N_TSP) where each rank simultaneously owns a shard of the model weights and a shard of the token sequence.

Two main runtime schedules are described:
1) Attention: an iterative TSP loop that (a) broadcasts one rank’s packed attention projection weight shards (WQ/WK/WV/WO) to peers, (b) computes local projections on each rank’s sequence shard, and (c) performs sequence-wise K/V exchange (all-gather-like) to reconstruct the needed context for correct causal attention. A zigzag sequence partition is used to balance causal-attention workload across ranks.
2) Gated MLP: a ring-style point-to-point schedule motivated by the structural analogy between σ(X·W1^T)·W2 and softmax(Q·K^T)·V. Weight shards circulate via send/recv while each rank accumulates partial outputs locally, aiming to avoid the all-reduce commonly used in standard TP.

A topology argument is also central: since N_TSP ≤ N_TP·N_SP, the combined group is more likely to fit within intra-node bandwidth, whereas a 2D layout can spill across nodes.1b:T82a,FlashOverlap changes the overlap strategy at two levels: it rewrites the collective execution structure and it reorders computation according to communication dependencies.

First, it decomposes reduce-scatter and all-gather into asynchronous point-to-point communication primitives, referred to as Flash-RS and Flash-AG. Standard collective implementations often impose stage-wise synchronization, so even if some chunks are already available, dependent computation may still wait on the collective schedule. By moving to a P2P decomposition, the dependency becomes more local: a rank can proceed once the required fragments arrive, without waiting for a broader barrier. The paper presents this as an exact algorithm rather than an approximation or a redundancy-based trick.

Second, it introduces rank-adaptive partial output scheduling. This appears to be the most important idea in the paper. Not all output fragments have the same communication dependency profile. Some can be computed immediately after a subset of messages arrives; others are communication-free or less urgent. Existing chunk-based overlap tends to process outputs in a fixed order, which leaves a structural tail at the end. FlashOverlap instead prioritizes the outputs whose computation can best absorb incoming communication, and defers less critical or communication-independent work. The goal is to shrink the exposed tail rather than merely spread communication more evenly.

Third, the framework is presented as unified across multiple parallel modes: DP, TP with sequence parallelism, and Ulysses-style communication patterns, as well as both training and inference, including prefill and decode. If that generality holds in practice, it is more useful than a one-off kernel optimization for a single collective.

Compared with mainstream chunk-splitting overlap in systems such as Megatron or MindSpeed, the difference is that FlashOverlap does not only tune chunk granularity. It attacks the structural source of the tail by changing both the communication schedule and the order of dependent computation.1c:T5c6,From the provided text, the evaluation spans multiple accelerators, model families, and parallel strategies, with MFU, latency, and time-per-token as the main metrics. The paper claims consistent MFU improvements and latency reductions across Transformers, Mamba, and hybrid architectures, and argues that TP/TPSP can scale beyond the usual single-node comfort zone more effectively under this scheme.

4. Sensitivity to chunk/message granularity, which matters because a strong method should be less dependent on a narrow chunk-size sweet spot than conventional overlap.
