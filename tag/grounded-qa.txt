18:T772,The framework has two stages: offline rubric generation and online RL. In the offline stage, the system generates an instance-specific rubric for each query using three inputs: the user query, retrieved passages, and a reference answer. The reference answer is not treated as the only correct target; instead, it helps identify what a strong answer should cover. Rubrics are organized into fixed buckets: accuracy, composition, safety, and general. Each bucket contains atomic, testable criteria tailored to the current query. The paper emphasizes that rubric items should be query-specific, evidence-grounded, discriminative, and easy to judge independently. Each item is also labeled as Essential or Optional and assigned a weight from 1 to 5.

In the online stage, the policy generates multiple candidate answers conditioned on the query and retrieved passages. An external LLM judge evaluates each candidate against the rubric items and returns item-level judgments. These judgments are then aggregated into a scalar reward, and the policy is optimized with KL-regularized GRPO. The key design choice is the combination of retrieval-conditioned rubric generation and rubric decomposition. Retrieval conditioning ensures that the reward criteria are tied to available evidence rather than generic answer style. Decomposition ensures that answer quality is represented as a structured set of principles rather than a single holistic score. The paper also compares against flat rubric variants, which helps isolate the value of structured decomposition beyond merely using a checklist format.

Compared with standard preference modeling or pairwise ranking, this approach makes the reward semantics explicit before optimization. Compared with fixed task-level rubrics, it is instance-specific and therefore better suited to open-domain QA, where the content requirements vary substantially across queries.19:T4cf,The experiments are conducted in a grounded knowledge QA / RAG setting. The main comparisons include an instruction-tuned baseline, flat rubric variants, and the full rubric-based RL framework. The paper also isolates the contributions of its two main ingredients. Retrieval-conditioned rubric generation improves factual support and groundedness, suggesting that conditioning the reward criteria on retrieved evidence materially changes what the policy learns. Rubric decomposition yields broader quality gains than a flatter reward specification, indicating that the structure of the reward matters, not just the presence of extra criteria.
