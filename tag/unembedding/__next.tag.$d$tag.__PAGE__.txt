b:T43c,Pipeline: empirical symptom → interpretability tools → subspace identification → linear filtering.
1) Symptom via Logit Lens: projecting raw text embeddings through the unembedding matrix yields top vocabulary probabilities dominated by high-frequency tokens across model families.
2) Hypothesis: embedding anisotropy concentrates representations around a frequency-weighted centroid (“average token”), suppressing semantic variation.
3) Subspace discovery via Logit Spectroscopy: analyzing a reverse-engineered average-token representation reveals an “edge spectrum” subspace spanned by right singular vectors at both ends of the unembedding matrix’s singular value spectrum (largest and smallest singular values).
4) Fix: EmbedFilter, a simple linear transformation that truncates/removes embedding components in this edge-spectrum subspace. It is constructed from existing unembedding parameters and requires no training.
5) Claimed side benefit: approximately distance-preserving behavior enables dimensionality reduction for cheaper indexing and faster retrieval.c:T669,The paper has three layers of contribution. First, it identifies the mechanism. In tied embedding/unembedding decoder-only transformers, tokens that are unseen or rarely used during training develop nearly collapsed (un)embeddings. Even if the model learns the reasoning algorithm structurally, it cannot reliably distinguish U1 from U2 if their output representations are almost the same. Second, it provides theory. The authors show that under SGD with weight decay and LayerNorm, this collapse is not an accident but a natural consequence of the training dynamics. Intuitively, unseen tokens receive little task-specific gradient to keep them separated, while regularization and normalization push them toward a low-variance shared region. Third, it studies interventions. A copy-enabled architecture alone is insufficient, because copying does not solve the problem once multiple unseen tokens are already indistinguishable at the output layer. Reliable symbolic reasoning requires a combination of: a copy-capable architecture, sufficiently diverse symbolic training data, and a mechanism that prevents embedding collapse. The latter can be achieved by freezing the (un)embeddings or periodically resetting them. The paper also reinterprets active forgetting from earlier work: its benefit is not merely “preventing knowledge storage in embeddings,” but more concretely interrupting collapse. Finally, the authors connect the analysis to Gemma 3, which contains 99 deliberately unused tokens intended for downstream use; these reserved tokens are shown to have highly correlated embeddings and to be poor downstream initializations.d:T5b2,The main experiments use a controlled propositional-logic testbed where models must perform in-context reasoning from rules, facts, and queries, with variable names sometimes replaced by unseen tokens. The main findings are consistent and mechanistic. First, a copy-enabled architecture by itself does not solve the multi-unseen-variable setting, because the output layer still cannot separate those symbols. Second, the similarity among unseen-token (un)embeddings increases during training and tracks the observed generalization failure. Third, freezing or periodically resetting (un)embeddings substantially improves reasoning and copying with unseen symbols, provided the training data has enough symbolic diversity. Fourth, similar collapse is observed in Gemma 3: the reserved tokens are highly correlated, and this hurts downstream finetuning when they are used as initialization.
