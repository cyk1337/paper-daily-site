18:T6d0,The intervention is deliberately minimal. The authors define source-conditioned role relabeling: after a model produces an incorrect intermediate claim c*, they append an additional message containing the exact same bytes of c*, but place it under an external role such as user, tool, or system memory, while keeping the audit instruction fixed across conditions. This isolates the causal effect of the role wrapper from content changes.

The paper then adds several mechanism probes. A self-distrust control asks the model to verify its own thoughts directly. This performs much worse than relabeling, which argues against a simple “extra verification reminder” explanation. A handle-granularity decomposition separates two effects: giving the claim an explicit standalone wrapper versus assigning that wrapper a specific role tag. The results suggest that a bare wrapper already helps, and the role label adds further lift. This supports an addressability account: a claim embedded inside the model’s own continuous scratchpad is harder to target, quote, and reject than the same claim presented as a discrete external object. The authors also analyze logprob signatures to distinguish engage-then-verify behavior from naive recomputation.

A useful part of the paper is the safety boundary analysis. The authors test the reverse direction: if relabeling helps correction, can role manipulation also inject errors? By default, the answer is mostly no. But once a trust-framing instruction is added, such as telling the model to treat memory as ground truth and not verify it, the attack channel opens up. So the effect is real, but it is mediated by instruction hierarchy and trust framing rather than being an unconditional role prior.19:T554,The study covers 13 model-domain cells across 7 model families and 3 domains, with 30 paired tasks per cell. Relabeling the same wrong claim from the model’s own <thought> into an external role improves correction rates by 23 to 93 percentage points. Ten of the 13 cells show significant gains; the remaining three are ceiling cases where the baseline correction rate is already high, at or above 67%. One highlighted example is Llama-3.3-70B, where the lift reaches +77 points.

The adversarial mirror includes five attack settings with n=30 each. Under default conditions, all 20 attack-rate cells stay at or below 3.3%, so the correction channel does not automatically become an error-injection channel. However, adding a single trust-framing sentence can raise the math attack rate to 70%, which sharply limits any naive deployment claim.

In the self-distrust control, explicitly asking the model to verify its own thoughts yields only 0 to 23% correction, versus up to 70% under relabeling.
