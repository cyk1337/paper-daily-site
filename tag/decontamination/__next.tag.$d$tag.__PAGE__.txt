9:T618,The authors do not propose a new detector. Instead, they reuse an existing three-tier candidate-generation pipeline and evaluate eight fully specified cleaning policies on top of it. Tier 1 performs exact and near-exact matching after normalization of whitespace, casing, and LaTeX macros. Tier 2 performs lexical matching using 13-gram and 8-gram coverage plus a 128-permutation MinHash/LSH Jaccard estimate. Tier 3 performs semantic retrieval with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and cosine similarity. A subset of lexical and semantic candidate pairs is then adjudicated by an existing LLM judge into same, subsumes, variant, or different. The eight policies differ in how much they trust exact matches, lexical heuristics, semantic thresholds, and LLM-judge outputs.

The strongest part of the paper is the evaluation design. The authors build a new independently and blindly dual-reviewed reference set of 594 training-benchmark pairs, split in advance into calibration and held-out partitions. The held-out split is disjoint not only in benchmark items but also in training-example clusters, so policy tuning is tested on genuinely unseen corpora and questions. They also sample 80 candidate-bearing training items and review every candidate edge attached to each item. That enables item-level false-removal measurement: a training item counts as wrongly removed only if none of its candidate edges is truly contaminating. This is more faithful than pair-level precision, which can overstate conservativeness when one item has both true and false candidate matches.a:T49f,The audit covers 16 open RLVR math corpora and 6 benchmarks: MATH-500, GSM8K test, AIME24, AIME25, AIME26, and AMC23. After normalization and corpus-specific deduplication, the study analyzes 1,673,514 corpus-specific training items corresponding to 1,197,691 unique underlying problem texts. Evaluation uses 594 manually reviewed pairs plus 80 fully resolved item-level samples.

The central empirical result is clear: the common heuristic “remove if semantic similarity exceeds a fixed threshold” becomes unreliable as the threshold is relaxed. Candidate-frame recall improves, but manual review shows that at permissive thresholds, most sampled removals are false removals. In other words, semantic retrieval is useful for candidate generation, but weak as a direct removal rule. The paper also reports the cost of each policy in retained corpus size and source-composition shift, showing that cleaning decisions affect not only how much data remains but also which sources remain. The abstract does not expose the full policy-by-policy table, but the paper’s contribution is less about naming a single best policy and more about making the trade-offs visible and auditable.b:T6e4,The work has two technical pieces. First, it introduces a sample-level evaluation framework, mainly for multiple-choice benchmarks. Instead of relying only on accuracy, it compares output distributions on each sample. The intended criterion is not just whether the decontaminated model gets the same answer as a clean model, but whether its probability allocation over choices looks similar to the clean model. A contaminated model often over-concentrates mass on the gold answer; a successful decontamination method should reduce that distortion and recover a more natural distribution over alternatives.

Second, it proposes UBD, a family of uncertainty-based decontamination methods built on deep ensembles. The key idea is that loss is an unreliable proxy for contamination: both easy examples and memorized hard examples can have low loss after contamination. Ensemble-based predictive uncertainty is used to identify suspicious samples more selectively. On top of that signal, the paper develops two intervention styles: (1) debiasing, which adjusts the output distribution for likely contaminated samples to reduce contamination-induced overconfidence; and (2) unlearning, which applies a stronger forgetting-style update to move the model away from the contaminated behavior on those samples.

Compared with methods that minimize KL to a clean teacher, UBD does not require a clean reference model. Compared with paraphrasing or answer-permutation baselines, it does not try to evade memorization by changing the input surface form. It instead estimates contamination through uncertainty and then corrects the model behavior directly. That distinction is important: the former is closer to benchmark-side mitigation, while UBD aims at model-side debiasing.c:T4a3,From the available full-text excerpt, the experiments appear to support three claims. Third, uncertainty is shown to separate hard-but-memorized samples from genuinely easy ones, even when both have low loss under the contaminated model. That is the most interesting empirical point, because it argues that loss alone is not a sufficient contamination indicator.
