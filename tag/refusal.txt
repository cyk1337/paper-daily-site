18:T778,The paper has two technical components: a measurement framework and a decoding intervention.

For measurement, the authors build a step-wise and position-wise analysis framework for masked diffusion language models. At each denoising step, the model predicts clean-token distributions for all positions, but only a subset of still-masked positions is committed. Using this structure, the paper tracks refusal-related token mass across denoising steps and response positions under harmful prompts. It distinguishes several events: whether refusal evidence appears at all, whether refusal tokens are committed, whether that commitment persists or gets overwritten, and how early commitments affect the final safety outcome.

This leads to the paper’s main conceptual finding: shallow-step alignment. The claim is that early denoising steps themselves form a safety-sensitive axis in dLLMs. Tokens committed early can strongly influence the final safety outcome even when they are not all at the leading response positions. Compared with shallow alignment in autoregressive models, diffusion models introduce an additional temporal dimension tied to denoising rather than surface order.

Based on that analysis, the paper proposes Refusal-Aware Early Commitment (RAEC), a training-free decoding method. RAEC changes only the commitment decision during decoding. If a refusal signal appears early and remains sufficiently strong and persistent, the decoder commits it early before later denoising dynamics can wash it out. Importantly, RAEC does not inject an external refusal template; it acts on refusal probability mass already produced by the model. This design is well matched to the diagnosed failure mode. If unsafe trajectories often contain weak or transient refusal evidence, then intervening at commitment time is a more targeted fix than retraining the model or attaching an external safety classifier.19:T6fc,The evaluation uses harmful-instruction benchmarks including JailbreakBench and StrongREJECT, with Llama-Guard-3-8B as the safety judge and Attack Success Rate (ASR) as the main metric. The tested models include masked diffusion LMs such as LLaDA and Dream.

The empirical findings come in three layers. First, the measurement results show that refusal signals are concentrated in early denoising steps and leading response positions, but these are not the same thing. Early-step commitments have a strong effect on final safety, supporting the shallow-step alignment hypothesis. The paper also includes intervention-style analyses: forcing early refusal commitment makes an unaligned base dLLM safer, while forcing early compliance commitment can push an aligned model toward unsafe outputs. That suggests early commitment is not just correlated with safety; it is a causal control point.

Second, the paper analyzes unsafe trajectories and finds that many of them do contain refusal evidence. The issue is that the refusal mass is weak, transient, or overwritten before commitment. Safe trajectories, in contrast, show stronger and more persistent refusal mass and much higher refusal-commitment rates. This is an important mechanistic refinement: the failure is often not “no refusal capability,” but “refusal evidence does not survive to commitment.”

Third, RAEC reduces ASR on both LLaDA and Dream while largely preserving utility. The provided text does not include the full result tables, so it would be unsafe to quote exact deltas here. When reading the original paper, the key numbers to verify are the ASR reductions on each benchmark, the utility trade-off on benign prompts, and the sensitivity to the thresholds controlling early commitment and refusal persistence.1a:T57f,This is a good skim paper for readers interested in mechanism rather than only safety application. Start with the Introduction and Preliminaries to understand the commitment process in masked diffusion LMs: the model predicts all positions at every step, but commits only some masked positions. Without that, the later analysis is easy to misread.

Then focus on the measurement framework and the visualizations. The most informative figures are likely the ones showing: (1) refusal-token mass over denoising steps, (2) refusal-signal distribution over response positions, and (3) differences in commitment persistence between safe and unsafe trajectories. Those figures explain why RAEC works better than the final ASR table alone.

After that, read the RAEC section carefully, especially how the method decides that a refusal signal is strong and persistent enough to commit early, and exactly how the commitment policy is modified. For readers interested in modeling paradigms, this is the most valuable part of the paper because it shows that decoding policy in diffusion LMs is not just an implementation detail; it is part of behavior formation.

Finally, check the benchmark tables for gains on LLaDA and Dream over JailbreakBench and StrongREJECT, and inspect the utility cost. A first pass should take around 30 minutes.
