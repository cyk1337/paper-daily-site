17:T6ee,The paper does not propose a new confidence estimator. Instead, it takes the most basic available signal—the logit difference between candidate answers—and tests it against a normative theory. Under SDC, confidence is the posterior probability that the chosen answer is correct, conditioned on a latent decision variable that encodes noisy task-relevant evidence. If an observable signal is a monotonic readout of that latent variable, then it carries the information needed, in principle, to compute posterior confidence.

Using this framework, the authors derive a set of qualitative signatures that LD should satisfy if it behaves like a readout of the latent decision variable. The visible text explicitly mentions the psychometric function and, most importantly, the correct/error folded-X pattern. The folded-X signature is especially diagnostic: as stimulus evidence becomes stronger, confidence should rise on correct trials but fall on error trials, producing the characteristic folded X. A generic preference score need not show this structure.

The experiments are built around tasks with an explicit stimulus-strength axis, so that the normative process model is well defined. The paper studies three perceptual discrimination tasks and one memory-based decision task, using three multimodal non-reasoning models and one multimodal reasoning model. This setup lets the authors jointly analyze stimulus strength, model choice, LD magnitude, and correctness. They also add an abstention setting: when the model is allowed to withhold low-confidence answers, does LD support near-optimal selective prediction? That behavioral test is important because it asks not only whether LD correlates with correctness, but whether it can drive sensible decision policies.18:T553,The main result is that on simple perceptual and memory decision tasks, LD satisfies the key SDC signatures, including the correct/error folded-X pattern. That is the strongest evidence in the paper that answer logits are acting as monotonic readouts of a latent decision variable rather than as arbitrary preference scores. In those settings, the confidence signal appears to have a meaningful computational interpretation.

The abstention experiments strengthen that claim. When allowed to abstain, the models preferentially withhold low-LD cases, and the accuracy of committed responses rises to near ceiling. The point is not merely that abstention helps; it is that LD appears behaviorally useful in a way that is close to normative selective prediction.

The paper is also careful about failure modes. In complex visual reasoning, LD still predicts correctness even after accounting for objective task difficulty, so it remains an informative signal. But the full geometric signatures predicted by SDC no longer hold. That suggests the latent-variable story becomes incomplete once tasks no longer admit a simple normative process model or when reasoning is not well captured by a one-dimensional evidence variable.
