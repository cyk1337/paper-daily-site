17:T4cb,RSTG first analyzes GRPO, OPD, and SFT under a shared policy-gradient view. GRPO estimates advantages from group rewards; when all rewards in a group are identical, the advantage disappears. OPD minimizes reverse KL from the student to the teacher on student-generated trajectories and can be written as a token-level advantage based on the teacher-student log-probability gap. This dense signal is noisy and asymmetric because many student tokens have low teacher probability, producing mostly negative advantages. RSTG uses three controls. First, sample selection applies OPD only when the student fails and the teacher succeeds, focusing on negative zero-variance prompts and weighting the signal by teacher mastery. The paper reports that using only 3.63% of the full data in this way outperforms standard OPD over all samples. Second, token selection restricts updates to high-entropy student tokens or tokens with large teacher-student divergence, reducing noisy gradients and slowing premature teacher imitation. Third, auxiliary SFT uses teacher-generated correct trajectories on negative zero-variance prompts, adding a global positive trajectory signal that OPD lacks when it conditions on erroneous student prefixes.18:T77e,BiCAA builds a bidirectional process reward. The paper argues that a single process signal is insufficient. Forward-looking rewards encourage locally helpful search, but they do not distinguish necessary steps from redundant ones. Pure hindsight credit can identify pivotal decisions after the fact, but it does not tell the agent whether a step actually improved the information state when it happened. BiCAA combines both.

The first branch is forward solvability gain. For a question with ground-truth answer, each retrieval step is treated as incremental evidence acquisition. At step t, the method compares the model’s conditional log-likelihood of the correct answer given the trajectory prefix up to t versus up to t-1. This marginal change measures whether the newly retrieved evidence makes the answer more solvable. In other words, it is an immediate estimate of how much that search action improved the model’s belief in the correct answer.

The second branch is hindsight success criticality. This branch looks back over the full trajectory and estimates whether a step was actually pivotal for reaching a correct final answer, rather than merely producing a small local gain. The full formula is not visible in the provided excerpt, but the intended role is clear: steps that are causally important to successful trajectories should receive more credit, while accidental or redundant steps should be downweighted.

BiCAA then uses a modulation rule to combine the two signals. A step that both increases local solvability and proves critical in hindsight gets amplified credit. A step with only weak local gain or low retrospective importance gets reduced credit. Finally, the calibrated process reward is fused with the trajectory-level outcome reward through unified advantage estimation for RL updates. The novelty is therefore in reward decomposition and credit shaping, not in a new RL optimizer.19:T460,From the available full-text excerpt, the experimental story is centered on two outcomes: final answer quality and search behavior quality. The paper claims that BiCAA reduces redundant search behavior and achieves competitive answer performance on multi-step search tasks. Given the method, the most important evidence is not a single benchmark score but process-level analysis: fewer unnecessary search iterations, better search efficiency, more stable training, and less policy collapse compared with vanilla GRPO.

The excerpt does not include the full result tables or exact numbers, so the strongest confirmed conclusion is that combining dense process rewards with outcome rewards works better than outcome-only supervision. When reading the full paper, the key ablations to check are: forward solvability gain alone, hindsight criticality alone, and the fused BiCAA reward; plus the contribution of the modulation rule and unified advantage estimation. Without those ablations, it would be hard to tell whether the gains come from the bidirectional credit design itself or simply from adding more reward shaping.1a:T41a,Trajectory-level hindsight critique (TLHC) is added on top of GRPO. After each rollout, every failed trajectory is sent to a frozen judge (Qwen3.6-27B) together with the gold answer, producing a 1–2 sentence critique of what should have been searched differently. The critique is broadcast as a prefix to a frozen teacher (the student's initialization, also the GRPO KL anchor), whose log-probabilities supply an on-policy distillation (OPD) loss on the student's search-action tokens within failed trajectories. The OPD loss uses a clamp coefficient with a stop-gradient on the teacher-student logprob gap, so gradients flow only on tokens where the teacher is more confident than the student; a lower clamp restricts gradient flow and an upper clamp prevents outliers from dominating. Answer tokens are left to GRPO. Total objective is GRPO + λ_OPD·L_OPD with λ=0.01. Given a problem x and an on-policy trajectory y, every prefix is held fixed and each token y_i is rescored under two opposing privileged conditions: c^pos marks the whole trajectory as correct and c^neg marks it as wrong. The per-token log-likelihood shifts relative to the original policy are denoted z^pos_i and z^neg_i. If shifts truly encoded answer polarity, the two directions should have opposite signs and their magnitudes should distinguish correct from incorrect tokens. Instead, 82%–85% of tokens are insensitive under both conditions at λ=0.05; among jointly sensitive tokens, 91%–96% have the same sign under c^pos and c^neg, indicating that shifts mainly reflect generic reweighting of surface/connector tokens once a final-answer hint is provided, rather than answer polarity. Roughly 58% of the forward-KL optimization magnitude overlaps between opposing conditions, and large-shift tokens are dominated by substitutable surface-form tokens such as “Wait” and “There”. Building on this observation, the paper proposes Counterfactual Sensitivity Credit Reallocation (CSCR), which uses the difference between the two counterfactual shifts, rather than their absolute magnitude, to measure a token’s true sensitivity to the outcome, and reallocates token-level advantages accordingly, avoiding uniform gradients on non-polar surface tokens.1c:T4d9,SVR makes the model emit, at every turn, a solution, a discrete verdict, and a confidence estimate. At inference, the trajectory stops only when the verdict is Correct and the confidence exceeds a threshold; otherwise, the next prompt includes the original problem, the previous solution, and the model’s own self-verification. Correctness labels are used during training to build rewards, but they are never inserted into refinement prompts and are not available to the inference controller. Training uses fixed-horizon trajectories with a GRPO-style objective. Per-turn solve, verification, and format scores are averaged into a trajectory return, while optimization is applied to the final completion tokens rather than treating intermediate turns as separate samples. The confidence reward uses a Brier-style calibration term, penalizes confident mistakes, and encourages recognizing both error states and correct states that are eligible for stopping. The controller specifies the sequence of model calls, active roles, branching logic, aggregation, and verification steps. Training then samples from a distribution over controllers rather than optimizing for a single fixed protocol.

The central modeling assumption is compositionality. Different controllers may have different global structures, but they share recurring local modules such as CoT generation, verification, planning, critique, and related reasoning behaviors. If the model learns these modules well, it may generalize to unseen controller compositions instead of memorizing complete workflows. To study this, the paper explores four module-level training strategies under a turn-level GRPO objective: flat mixing of all turns, uniform weighting across module types, adaptive gradient-norm balancing, and single-role-per-step training. These variants are motivated by a real optimization issue: controllers differ in turn count and role composition, so some modules naturally dominate the gradient signal unless training explicitly compensates for that imbalance.

What makes the paper interesting is not merely that it uses multi-task RL, but that it treats controller structure as a source of reusable local behaviors and then asks how optimization should respect that structure. Once the goal is cross-controller transfer, module-level credit assignment and gradient balancing become first-order concerns.
