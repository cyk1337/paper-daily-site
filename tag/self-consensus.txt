18:T818,The paper has two technical components. The first is a mechanistic diagnosis of what the authors call the consensus–termination gap. They generate one main reasoning trajectory per problem, freeze it, and then append probes offline to different prefixes of that same trajectory. This frozen-trajectory design is crucial because it ensures that different probing schedules and stopping rules observe identical underlying reasoning rather than perturbing it. On top of this substrate, they run three direct measurements.

First, wording sensitivity: for the same frozen prefix, they vary the wording of the probe and check whether the extracted current answer changes. If early prefixes are highly sensitive to probe wording, then the answer is being elicited by the query rather than read out from a settled internal state. Second, a hand-labeled taxonomy of wrong stops: among cases where the rule stops and the committed answer is wrong, they distinguish trajectories that had genuinely converged to a wrong answer from trajectories that had not converged at all and were merely forced into a temporary placeholder by the probe. Third, non-terminal commitment analysis: they measure how often a consensus stop commits to an answer that the original trajectory later abandons, and whether the truncated continuation would have corrected that answer.

The second component is a preregistered exhaustive sweep over a family of windowed consensus rules. The search space includes window size, agreement-share threshold, and operational knobs, for a total of 3,520 rules. These are evaluated on frozen trajectories from two models and three benchmarks with problem-grouped splits and pre-fixed acceptance gates. This is a strong design choice: instead of showcasing a few hand-picked operating points, the paper asks whether the rule family contains any robustly usable point at all. A non-consensus baseline, DEER, is run through the exact same pipeline and token accounting, which isolates whether the failure comes from the signal or from the early-exit objective itself.19:T858,The empirical case is quite tight. On the mechanism side, re-probing the same frozen prefix with different wording yields different answers 54% of the time in the first tenth of a trajectory, versus 16% near the end. Early extracted answers are therefore highly probe-dependent. In a hand-labeled set of 134 wrong-stop cases, more than half are situations where the model had not actually converged and the probe forced a premature answer, while only about a quarter are cases where the model had truly settled on a wrong answer. The non-terminal commitment analysis is even more telling: at a consensus rule that still saves 32% of tokens, one stop in nine commits an answer that the trajectory later leaves behind. Among 216 such non-terminal stops, 155 cut off a correction the trajectory would have made, while only 16 banked a correct answer from a trajectory that would later end wrong. In other words, the computation being removed is mostly useful corrective computation.

The rule sweep then shows that this is not a threshold artifact. Across 3,520 preregistered consensus rules, none clears the pre-specified safety-and-savings gates on development, so none is carried forward. Yet 478 rules clear a gate in-sample on the training split, which explains how a less disciplined selection protocol could have produced optimistic conclusions. The frontier reproduces strongly on the held-out test split (r = 0.98) and on unseen models, including one at 4× scale and with a different architecture, so the negative result is stable. The contrast with DEER is the key control: under the same protocol, DEER clears all three gates and on unseen models can lose as little as 0.3 percentage points while saving 28% of tokens. At matched savings, consensus is much more expensive: at 28% net saving, the cheapest consensus rule still costs 6.0 points of accuracy, and at 33% saving it costs 8.3 points, versus 0.5 and 2.0 points for DEER.
