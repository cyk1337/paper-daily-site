5:T439,The authors replace aggregate post-pruning accuracy with a capability-retention view over four dimensions: General, Commonsense, Code, and Math. They then profile 15 calibration sources using information metrics from Objective Information Theory. The strongest predictors are Distortion, operationalized through perplexity, and Aggregation, measured through repeated 4-gram patterns. Broader measures such as Scope, Variety, Granularity, and Mismatch are less reliable. Based on this analysis, the paper proposes two calibration constructions. The first is mix_balanced, which samples uniformly from capability-aligned pools under the same 128-sequence budget used by the baselines. The second is IGSP, a self-generation protocol that uses the base model and the evaluation taxonomy to create capability-balanced calibration data, while controlling perplexity bands and reducing repeated-pattern bias. Compared with Self-Cal and SGS, IGSP treats self-generated calibration as a multi-source design problem rather than a single generated distribution filtered by surface statistics.6:T438,The main experiments use LLaMA-3.1-8B with SparseGPT and Wanda, under a fixed 128-sequence calibration budget. With SparseGPT at 60% sparsity, mix_balanced reaches a total retention score of 58.8, compared with 50.0 for the best single source, MetaMath, and 40.0 for the C4 default. The gain over C4 is 18.8 points overall, and the Code dimension alone improves by 51.9 points. The benefit of multi-source calibration increases with sparsity, from +4.6 points at 30% sparsity to +18.8 points at 60%. The correlation analysis reports Spearman ρ = +0.71 between calibration perplexity and General retention, but ρ = -0.53 for Math and ρ = -0.59 for Code, with p < 0.05. IGSP improves over Self-Cal by 2.4 points and over SGS by 4.8 points at SparseGPT 60%, while still trailing real multi-source calibration by 9.7 points. Wanda behaves differently: at 60% sparsity, all calibration variants fall within a narrow 3.8-point range, consistent with Wanda using calibration mainly as a local activation-magnitude estimator rather than as a global Hessian-driven weight-update signal.7:T43b,OP-Mix combines on-policy domain adapters with linear adapter interpolation. For each data domain, the method trains one LoRA adapter starting from the current model. This adapter approximates the update direction induced by training on that domain at the model’s current state. Candidate mixtures are then simulated by linearly interpolating these domain LoRAs, using the interpolated adapter as a proxy for the loss surface under full mixture training. A user-defined objective aggregates evaluation metrics, with maximization metrics negated so that mixture search can be posed as minimization. Compared with proxy scaling-law approaches that train many smaller models on sampled mixtures, OP-Mix needs only one adapter per domain and stays on-policy with the model being trained. When a new domain is introduced, the algorithm trains another domain adapter and refits the mixture, instead of rebuilding the whole proxy-model grid. This is also why the method naturally applies to continual learning: old and new domains are represented in the same mixture optimization problem.8:T53a,The setup has three stages. A model is pretrained on a broad corpus D_pre, post-trained on a smaller target corpus D_post, and then fine-tuned by a downstream user on a new objective D_ft. The main intervention is to mix part of D_post into the pretraining stage before the dedicated post-training stage. The evaluation is not based on immediate post-training loss alone. Instead, the downstream fine-tuning method is fixed, the learning rate is swept, and the resulting frontier between downstream loss, retained D_post capability, and general D_pre performance is compared across upstream recipes. This is the right measurement because two models with similar post-training loss can occupy different retention frontiers after fine-tuning. The paper also runs compute-matched allocation experiments, comparing all early exposure, all late exposure, and mixed schedules. The optimum tends to lie between the extremes. The proposed explanation is feature-level: early exposure lets the target capability be represented in more specialized and less interfering features, making it less vulnerable to later updates. Replay and dropout are evaluated under the same lens. Replay reduces overwriting by interleaving earlier data, while dropout discourages feature co-adaptation; both provide gains that are complementary to early data
