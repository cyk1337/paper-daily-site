9:T7d1,The paper first provides a mechanism-level diagnosis, then introduces a method. The diagnosis centers on how the so-called Lucky Hit mechanism behaves under different answer spaces. Standard TTRL implicitly assumes that if the model samples multiple reasoning trajectories, the correct answer is more likely to become the majority and therefore serve as a useful pseudo-label. The authors argue that this assumption is much more plausible in large free-form answer spaces, where incorrect trajectories tend to disperse across many distinct strings. In a small multiple-choice space, however, different wrong rationales can collide on the same wrong option, creating a false consensus that is then reinforced by training. The model becomes more answer-consistent without becoming more correct.

Based on this diagnosis, the paper proposes PROSE: Process Reward Guided Self-Training. PROSE keeps the basic test-time RL loop—sample trajectories on unlabeled test questions, compute rewards, update the policy—but replaces majority-vote answer reward with process-level reward. A medical process reward model scores each reasoning step, and the trajectory reward is defined as the minimum step score rather than the mean. This design choice is central. Minimum aggregation prevents a trajectory from hiding one critical reasoning error behind many fluent or locally correct steps. If the weakest step is poor, the whole trajectory should not receive a high reward. The method also includes an answer-format guard to prevent reward hacking through malformed or strategically formatted outputs.

A practical aspect of the method is that the PRM is used only during adaptation, not at deployment-time inference. The goal is to internalize process supervision into the policy parameters rather than rely on an external reranker or verifier at test time. That distinguishes PROSE from many inference-time selection methods, which often improve quality at the cost of additional runtime modules and compute.a:T836,The paper first shows the failure mode of standard TTRL on medical multiple-choice QA: sample-level accuracy improves only slightly, majority-vote accuracy stays flat, and output diversity drops sharply. In other words, the model becomes more self-consistent but not more correct.

The most convincing experiment is the controlled answer-space intervention. On AMC math problems, the authors keep the questions, model, optimizer, and training procedure fixed, and vary only the answer format: original free-form answering versus a matched four-option multiple-choice version. In the free-form setting, TTRL improves steadily. In the multiple-choice setting, accuracy rises briefly and then collapses. This strongly supports the claim that answer-space structure, not domain difficulty, is the main cause of failure.

The paper also introduces the protection gap, which measures the difference between pseudo-label reward accuracy and true answer accuracy. In free-form answer spaces, this gap provides some protection because wrong answers disperse. In small answer spaces, the gap nearly disappears, making it much easier for wrong trajectories to be rewarded through majority agreement. This quantitative analysis supports the Lucky Hit diagnosis.

Empirically, PROSE improves both sample-level and majority-vote accuracy relative to standard TTRL and avoids the rapid diversity collapse. Two additional findings matter. First, the gains remain after removing the PRM at inference time, indicating that the policy has absorbed the process signal. Second, the gains transfer to datasets that were not used during adaptation, suggesting that the method learns more general reasoning behavior rather than dataset-specific answer preferences. The paper also compares reward aggregation schemes and reports that minimum aggregation is clearly better than mean aggregation, which is more vulnerable to reward hacking.
