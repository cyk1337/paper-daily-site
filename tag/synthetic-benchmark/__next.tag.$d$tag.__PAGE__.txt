9:T497,SynthDocBench is a fully synthetic benchmark built around a dual-layer document design: each document is generated both as a visible D3.js rendering and as a hidden structured metadata block used exclusively for deterministic ground-truth derivation. The pipeline has three stages: (1) topic-grounded content generation that organizes text, tables, and charts into an intermediate representation with section boundaries and traceable evidence units; (2) design and visual grammar sampling over six layout archetypes, mixing topic-conditioned and uniform sampling (0.6/0.4) to avoid spurious topic-layout correlations; (3) manifest-driven QA generation covering three families—chart-reading over 24 D3 chart types requiring exact numeric reads that cannot be inferred from surrounding text, cross_modal questions where chart and textual evidence are placed in non-adjacent sections, and complex multi-hop questions at levels L1–L5 combining 2–4 evidence units across modalities and pages. Length, depth, modality composition, and question type are independently controlled. At evaluation time, models see only rendered page images, with no access to underlying metadata.a:T572,The method has three main components. First, problem generation: each instance contains a set of axioms and several candidate conclusions, and the model must choose which conclusion is logically derivable. This multiple-choice formulation is deliberate. Instead of asking for free-form proof generation, it keeps verification exact and cheap while forcing the model to evaluate several candidates that require comparable reasoning effort, reducing shortcut opportunities. Second, difficulty control: the framework exposes two explicit axes. One is proof-tree depth, the minimum reasoning depth needed to derive the correct answer. The other is logical expressiveness, starting from implication-only logic and progressively adding conjunction, disjunction, negation, and universal quantification. This separates “longer reasoning chains” from “richer proof search structure.” Third, RL post-training: the authors train on this verifiable environment with outcome-based RL and compare multiple RL methods and training distributions. A key design choice is curriculum training rather than mixing all depths and logic families uniformly. The paper’s novelty is less about inventing a new RL algorithm and more about reparameterizing the study of reasoning RL into a clean experimental system where scaling laws, curriculum effects, and transfer can be measured with much less confounding.b:T507,There are three headline results. First, scaling: the number of RL training steps T needed to reach 90% validation accuracy follows T∝D^γ with proof depth D, with R^2 above 0.99 over the observed range. More importantly, γ rises monotonically with logical expressiveness, from 1.04 to 2.60. In the simplest logic, training cost grows close to linearly with depth; in more expressive logics, the growth is much steeper. Second, robustness across methods and training distributions: the same power-law pattern appears across different RL algorithms, suggesting that the phenomenon is tied to task structure and training dynamics rather than a single optimization recipe. Curriculum training improves both efficiency and stability. Third, downstream transfer: training on ScaleLogic improves average accuracy across eight real-world math and general reasoning benchmarks by up to +10.66 points over the base model in the most expressive setting. A notable qualitative result is that low-expressiveness training plateaus early, while high-expressiveness training yields a steadily improving transfer curve.
