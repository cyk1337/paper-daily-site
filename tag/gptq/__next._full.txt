18:T422,The authors formulate PTQ as approximating a linear layer under a layer-wise reconstruction loss with round-to-nearest quantization. Their central result states that, for i.i.d. Gaussian weights and calibration activations with sufficiently large effective rank, the normalized RTN reconstruction loss converges uniformly over scales to a deterministic scalar objective: the mean-squared error of uniformly quantizing a standard Gaussian variable. They then analyze this limiting objective. It has a unique nondegenerate optimal scale, and the optimal scale decreases as quantizer resolution increases. They also study a scale-invariant curvature that captures local sensitivity around the optimum and observe that it decays roughly exponentially with bit-width. The paper connects this analysis to Hadamard incoherence processing, which spreads weight magnitudes through a random orthogonal transform and makes transformed weights closer to Gaussian. It also introduces Proxy-static, a computationally cheaper GPTQ-aware proxy objective for choosing scales.19:T622,The paper proceeds in three steps. First, it formulates two-sided GPTQ under a Kronecker-factored quadratic geometry. Standard GPTQ uses only the input-side covariance, corresponding to a Hessian of the form E[xx^T]⊗I. The two-sided extension allows a general A⊗B, which means quantization is guided by both input and output geometry and effectively minimizes ||(B^{1/2})^T(V-W)A^{1/2}||_F^2. Second, the authors extend the high-rate waterfilling view from prior work to this two-sided setting, yielding WaterKron. Instead of assigning uniform precision to all weights, WaterKron allocates row- and column-dependent scales and combines them with entropy coding. Under high-rate, Gaussian, and scalar-grid assumptions, the resulting allocation remains within an asymptotic gap of about 0.25 bits from the information-theoretic limit for the Kronecker distortion. Third, and most importantly, the paper analyzes WaterKron under the true full-Hessian geometry. This yields an explicit mismatch factor Φ that quantifies the multiplicative distortion penalty introduced by replacing the full Hessian with a Kronecker approximation. Choosing A and B is then cast as minimizing Φ. That optimization reduces to a Gaussian covariance-fitting problem and naturally recovers classical flip-flop updates, which motivates the proposed FlipFlop Hessian. The key distinction from prior Hessian choices in the quantization literature is that the approximation is selected to minimize distortion in the true geometry, not merely because it is easy to compute or empirically decent.1a:T7a9,The key idea is not to exempt important weights from quantization, but to quantize all weights into a compact ternary base first and then spend a small residual budget where ternary quantization hurts the most. In the sub-2-bit regime, this is a more efficient use of bits than simply bypassing quantization for selected weights.

The method has three main components.

First, a ternary base representation. Following PT2-LLM-style formulations, each quantization group uses a scale α and bias β, and weights are mapped to one of three values: {β-α, β, β+α}. This gives a compact ternary approximation for all weights.

Second, a salient residual design called column-semi sparse salient weight. QTEA first selects important columns, then keeps only a sparse subset of high-impact entries within those columns under a 1:4 semi-structured sparsity pattern. These retained entries act as residual corrections to the ternary base. This is a useful compromise: more regular than unstructured salient-weight preservation, but more bit-efficient than storing full salient columns.

Third, the paper modifies GPTQ-style column-by-column quantization in two ways. It adds column-wise rescale refinement, which alternates between updating a per-column rescale factor and the ternary assignment so that the ternary base better matches the local weight distribution after Hessian-aware error propagation. It also introduces error decay. The motivation is that GPTQ’s error propagation is order-dependent: early columns can spread their error over many later columns, while late columns have little room left, so using the same propagation strength everywhere can overcompensate near the end. Error decay gradually weakens propagation along the column order to reduce this imbalance.

Overall, QTEA is a combination of ternary base quantization, structured residual compensation, and a refined column-wise optimization procedure tailored to ultra-low-bit settings.1b:T5c7,The main experiments are on Qwen3-14B and Llama3-8B. On Qwen3-14B, QTEA improves average downstream-task accuracy by 16.7% over the strongest ternary PTQ baseline reported by the authors, while reducing perplexity by 1.40× on WikiText and 2.61× on C4. On Llama3-8B, it improves average downstream accuracy by 6.6% and reduces perplexity by 1.34× on WikiText and 1.95× on C4. The larger gain on Qwen3-14B suggests the method remains useful across model families and may be especially helpful where ternary quantization is more brittle.

The paper also reports a better size-versus-perplexity trade-off on Qwen3, which supports the claim that the residual budget is being allocated more effectively.

On the systems side, the authors implement dedicated CUDA kernels and combine them with CUDA Graphs. They report up to 7.2× generation speedup over an FP16 baseline with CUDA Graphs, and up to 13.3× over FP16 without CUDA Graphs. These numbers should be interpreted carefully because the gain comes from both quantization and systems optimization, but the paper does close the loop between representation design and practical inference speed.

The most important ablations to inspect are likely the contribution of the residual representation, the effect of column-wise rescale refinement, and the effect of error decay on late-column instability. The provided text does not include all ablation numbers, but those are the components most directly tied to the claimed gains.1c:T465,Start with the introduction.
