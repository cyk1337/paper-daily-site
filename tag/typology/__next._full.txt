18:T6fd,The paper uses Distributed Alignment Search (DAS) within the causal abstraction framework, instead of standard probing or sparse feature decomposition. The motivation is clear. Probes can read out information that is not actually used by the model, while SAE/CLT-style methods may surface interpretable features but still leave faithfulness and stability concerns. DAS searches for low-dimensional, causally relevant subspaces by intervening on internal states between paired examples and identifying the rotations and substitutions that best explain behavioral changes.

The experimental design is unusually important here. The authors build templated minimal pairs for three syntactic constructions across fifteen languages. Coverage is intentionally uneven where typology demands it: Chinese is excluded from number agreement because it lacks grammatical number, and German/Dutch require special handling in filler–gap templates because embedded verbs are clause-final. This matters because the paper is trying to compare mechanisms, not just outcomes, so template alignment and minimal-pair control are central to validity.

The workflow is: verify that the model behaviorally handles the construction, localize a causally implicated subspace within a source language, then transplant that intervention into the corresponding target-language position and measure whether behavior changes in the expected direction. The paper also includes controls such as random-label tests and mismatched interventions to show that the effect is not a generic perturbation artifact. A further step links transfer strength to typological similarity, leading to the claim that multilingual syntactic mechanisms are organized around typological hubs rather than arbitrary language-specific partitions.19:T59b,The study spans 4 multilingual LMs, 15 languages, and 3 syntactic phenomena. The models differ in family, size, and training setup, which helps the authors argue that the findings are not tied to a single architecture or checkpoint. Before any interpretability analysis, they run behavioral validation to ensure the models actually exhibit the target syntactic competence on the constructed stimuli.

The main empirical findings come in three layers. First, cross-lingual mechanism transfer is consistently observed across all three constructions, not just in a few closely related language pairs. Second, transfer is graded rather than binary: stronger transfer tends to occur between typologically closer languages. Third, some languages behave like hubs, with interventions localized in those languages transferring more broadly to others. That suggests multilingual models may organize syntactic abstractions around a small number of reusable structural centers.

The controls are important. Random-label and other non-specific intervention settings substantially weaken the effect, which supports the claim that the discovered subspaces are causally tied to the target syntactic behavior rather than reflecting generic representation damage.
