18:T66b,Ask-to-Distill (A2D) treats a prompt as a distribution elicitor: it determines which prefixes the student visits, hence where teacher token distributions become available for OPD.

Two-stage pipeline:
1) Train a prompt synthesizer before OPD.
- Initialize the synthesizer from the student.
- Optimize it with RL while keeping the scoring student and the teacher frozen.
- Crucially, the method avoids an expensive inner loop: it does not update the student per candidate prompt to estimate value.

2) A no-answer, distributional surrogate reward for prompt value.
- The reward aggregates token-level signals along student rollouts.
- Instead of rewarding pure disagreement (which can be dominated by support-mismatch), it uses a support-aware notion of teachability: combine teacher–student discrepancy with the teacher’s probability mass on the student’s candidate tokens, aiming to capture “corrective preference within student support” rather than “divergence outside support”.

Anti-gaming terms:
- Length control via sublinear length normalization plus a soft response-budget penalty to prevent inflating reward by longer outputs.
- A batch-level diversity term to prevent mode collapse into a narrow prompt family.

Curation:
- After training, prompts are format-filtered and lexically/semantically deduplicated; a frozen Qwen3-Embedding-4B is used only for semantic deduplication, not for reward computation.

Compared to synthetic SFT data, A2D does not need reference answers; compared to task-generation via success rewards, it optimizes the usability of teacher distributional supervision rather than task correctness.19:T4a4,RACE has two core operations. Referential Abstraction removes identity-revealing text and replaces it with image-grounded references. These can be specific visual references, such as position, appearance, occlusion, or spatial relation, or ambiguous relational references that require searching over multiple depicted candidates and selecting the one whose retrieved evidence satisfies a condition. The construction searches all candidates, requires a unique match, rules out alternatives, and uses a question-only probe to detect identity or answer leakage. Compositional Evidence then builds questions whose answer depends on every selected target’s evidence. The targets can come from one image or from several images, and the construction requires that removing any designated contribution leaves the answer underdetermined. The data starts from real images, including social media photos, Bilibili video frames, and MovieNet imagery. For unannotated images, filtering, visual analysis, and retrieval establish candidate identities; MovieNet provides identity-image provenance. A teacher solves the resulting tasks, and only trajectories with correct final answers are kept for SFT.1a:T5ac,Gradus separates (a) deciding what behaviors to teach from (b) producing valid tasks/solutions that pass environment checks, via three components:
1) Guarded operators: for each action, represent its preconditions (state/policy guards), its possible policy-dependent outcomes, and how each outcome changes the state and future action requirements. This turns an environment’s latent rule structure into composable primitives.
2) Coverage-guided blueprint search: compose operators into structured task blueprints that specify which outcomes/interactions a task should exercise, with explicit controls over structural complexity. Maintain corpus-level coverage statistics and prioritize underrepresented outcomes and combinations, preventing global skew even if local task proposals look diverse.
3) Environment-grounded joint task–solution refinement: instead of discarding failed generations, use environment execution as a shared anchor to iteratively repair both the task (unsatisfiable constraints, hallucinated conditions, ambiguous goals) and the solution trajectory (policy violations, state-tracking errors). The blueprint constrains the repair so the intended behavioral coverage is preserved while making the instance verifiable.
The paper also varies “interaction dimensions” (ambiguity, indirect references, social pressure, extra reasoning burden) to decouple surface interaction difficulty from the underlying policy requirements.1b:T432,A scaled difference-in-means recipe built on synthetic matched narratives:
- The atomic unit is a concept–antagonist pair, fixed by a one-sentence dichotomy guidance (because a concept’s direction depends on what it is contrasted against).
- Data generation is designed for generalization: for each of 1,036 pairs, generate matched story pairs across 5 languages, 10 genres, 10 setups, using two independent generator models. Generation first produces a shared setup, then writes the positive/negative story from that same setup to control confounders.
- Vector extraction: for a chosen model and layer(s), compute the mean activation for positive vs. negative stories and take their difference as the feature vector.
- Validation goes beyond the synthetic domain: (i) detection on natural chat/web text, and (ii) causal steering by adding the vector to activations.

- Diagnostics/ablations: convergence with story count, format sensitivity, cross-model geometry, and effective intervention layers (Sec. 2.2 + App. F.*).1d:T457,LongForge has an analyzer and a synthesizer. The analyzer reconstructs each QA example into an evidence-assertion reasoning graph. Evidence nodes are verbatim spans from the document. Assertion nodes are the answer sentences. A hyperedge represents a reasoning step where evidence spans and/or previous assertions support one assertion. This design prevents the analyzer from inventing external evidence or adding reasoning chains not present in the answer. Four graph-based metrics are then computed: breadth, the number of distinct evidence spans used; depth, the longest evidence-to-terminal-assertion path after paraphrase contraction; correctness, the fraction of supported reasoning steps; and completeness, the fraction of requested information points answered by supported assertions. The synthesizer uses correctness and completeness as gates, with failure reasons fed back for re-answering. Breadth and depth are measured and later controlled through dataset mixing rather than optimized directly during generation, which reduces the risk of producing answers that look multi-step only on the surface.1e:T825,The key move is to formulate the gap between reverse CoT and verified forward CoT as a translation problem rather than a prompting problem.

Let q be the question and a the correct answer. Forward CoT (FCG) samples reasoning from the teacher conditioned only on q. Rejection sampling (RS) keeps only those forward samples whose extracted final answer matches a. Reverse CoT (RCG) instead conditions the teacher on both q and a. The authors argue that the student should ideally learn from the RS distribution, not from raw RCG outputs.

To do this, they build a small paired dataset. For each question, they sample one reverse CoT response x, then sample forward CoTs until they obtain the first accepted response z with the correct final answer. This yields paired examples (x, z). A translator model G_phi is then fine-tuned with teacher forcing to predict z from x.

A crucial design choice is that the translator only receives the reverse CoT text x, not the original question q or answer a. This serves two purposes. First, it matches the paper’s distributional derivation, where the goal is to map source responses to target responses. Second, it blocks a direct question-answer shortcut and forces the model to learn how to rewrite answer-conditioned rationales into outputs closer to accepted forward reasoning.

Once trained, the translator is used at scale: each new question requires one reverse teacher call to produce x and one translator call to produce z-hat, and the student is then trained on (q, z-hat). Compared with RS, which may need many forward teacher calls per accepted sample, this pipeline compresses the expensive part into one cheap teacher sample plus one translation step.

Relative to prior semantic suppression or structural-guidance methods, the difference is conceptual. Those methods still generate under answer access and try to reduce leakage. CoTranslator instead learns an explicit distributional correction from RCG-style outputs toward RS-style outputs. The appendix further provides a distribution-recovery analysis to justify this framing.1f:T609,The experiments cover six student models and six math benchmarks, with transfer tests to a new math dataset, a different teacher model, and STEM problems.

The first headline result is quality. For students such as Qwen3-4B-Base, CoTranslator improves average accuracy over semantic suppression by up to 5.82 percentage points. That indicates that merely hiding answer cues is not enough; explicit translation toward the verified forward distribution works better.

The second result is the cost-performance trade-off. The two 4B students retain 95.0% of the average accuracy of rejection sampling while using only 35.9% of its estimated compute. This is the central empirical claim of the paper. RS remains the cleaner target, but it is expensive; CoTranslator uses a small paired set to train a translator and then amortizes that cost over large-scale data generation.

The third result is transfer. Without retraining, the translator generalizes to a new math dataset, a different teacher, and STEM tasks. Average STEM gains are 4.88 points for 4B students and 4.31 points for 8B students. This suggests the translator is learning a reusable transformation from answer-conditioned rationales to more question-grounded ones, rather than memorizing a dataset-specific template.

The paper also includes analysis showing that translated outputs are closer to RS responses and exhibit fewer answer-dependent shortcuts than raw reverse CoTs.
