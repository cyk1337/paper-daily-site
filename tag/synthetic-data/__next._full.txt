18:T893,FROST has two main ingredients: real-anchored utility estimation and conditional filtering.

For utility estimation, each training step draws both a real batch and a synthetic batch. The method maintains an exponential moving average of gradients from recent real batches, which serves as a smoothed reference direction for the real-data objective. Each synthetic sample is then scored by the alignment between its gradient and this real-gradient reference. The intuition is straightforward: if a synthetic sample pushes parameters in a direction similar to what recent real data prefers, it is more likely to be useful; if it points away, it is more likely to be harmful or off-distribution.

The second ingredient is conditional filtering. The authors argue that per-sample gradient utility is noisy, so filtering every batch aggressively can remove useful supervision. They first aggregate utilities at the batch level and compare the batch mean against recent history to determine whether the batch falls inside a moderate utility band. Batches inside the band are kept as-is. Only out-of-band batches trigger sample-level filtering, where interquartile-range thresholds are used to retain or discard examples within the batch. This design is meant to avoid overreacting to stochastic gradient noise while still rescuing good samples from otherwise weak batches.

Relative to common synthetic-data selection methods, FROST differs in three ways. It does not require a separate pretrained feature extractor for fidelity/diversity scoring, so it avoids expensive offline preprocessing of the full synthetic pool. It does not need a held-out validation set or an external judge, because the real training stream itself provides the anchor. And it is online: utility is recomputed as the model evolves, rather than assigned once in a static preprocessing stage.

From a pretraining perspective, the hidden assumption is that an EMA of real-data gradients is a stable proxy for the target objective. That seems plausible in moderate-scale fine-tuning, but it is less obvious in large mixed-corpus pretraining where gradient noise, domain conflict, and real/synthetic ratios can be very different.19:T621,The paper evaluates FROST on image classification, LLM fine-tuning for text-to-SQL, and a large industrial ads re-ranking system. On the public benchmarks, it filters out roughly 20% to 30% of synthetic data while outperforming training on the full synthetic pool.

The reported gains are 0.58% to 1.14% absolute accuracy improvement across three synthetic image generators, and 0.9% improvement on text-to-SQL fine-tuning. The industrial result is more telling in practical terms: using the full noisy synthetic pool causes a 0.211% relative NE regression compared with the real-only baseline, while FROST turns that into a 0.096% relative NE improvement. The authors note that gains around 0.02% are already considered meaningful in mature production recommenders, so the industrial effect size is not trivial.

Methodologically, the matched-drop-ratio comparison is important. The paper compares selection methods under the same synthetic-data discard rate, which makes the gains more attributable to better selection rather than simply using less data. Also worth checking is the ablation on batch-level gating plus sample-level filtering versus always-on per-sample filtering, since that is the part intended to control gradient-noise sensitivity.

The main caveat is scope. The LLM experiment is a downstream fine-tuning task, not large-scale language-model pretraining. The paper does not establish that real-anchored gradient utility remains stable or cost-effective for next-token pretraining with large synthetic corpora or reasoning-heavy synthetic mixtures.1a:T4d9,For a skim read, start with the Introduction and Method to understand the logic of real-anchored utility and conditional filtering. Then read the text-to-SQL experiment and the main ablations. Around 20 to 30 minutes should be enough to decide whether the idea is portable to your own data pipeline.

Focus on four details. First, inspect how utility is computed in practice: full gradients, projected gradients, or some approximation. That determines scalability. Second, check how the real-gradient EMA is updated and stabilized, because the whole method depends on the anchor being reliable. Third, read the calibration rule for batch means and the IQR-based sample filtering, since that is where the paper tries to avoid over-filtering under noisy gradients. Fourth, look for behavior across different drop ratios, synthetic-data quality levels, and training stages.

If your interest is pretraining data rather than downstream fine-tuning, treat this as a prototype idea: use training signals instead of static data features to select synthetic data. Before scaling it up, the open questions are compute overhead, gradient approximation quality, and whether the real-gradient anchor remains meaningful under multi-domain mixed training.1b:T441,QVAC Genesis III contains 191.43B synthetic STEM tokens across 19 domains and three difficulty levels, rendered in four styles: textbook, web article, QA, and dialogue. The pipeline samples seed passages from FineFineWeb, filters them with the Ultra-FineWeb quality classifier, and keeps roughly 500k high-quality seeds. QwQ-32B is then used to generate self-contained multiple-choice questions. A weak edge-scale student answers the questions, and an answer-extraction step decides whether the student matches the gold label. Wrong answers trigger a Failure Analysis pipeline, where a teacher model diagnoses the misconception and writes a targeted corrective explanation. Correct answers trigger an Option-Level reasoning pipeline, where the teacher explains the correct option and each distractor. This differs from standard synthetic textbook generation because the student response controls the form of supervision. In the enterprise setting, the workflow is authored in Agent Script, which defines routers, subagents, typed actions, tool scopes, and natural-language instructions. A simulation pipeline then expands each specification into scenario-conditioned and persona-conditioned multi-turn sessions. The scenario determines the user need and which workflows are exercised. Execution is handled by NeMo Gym. In environments without production backends, a frozen helper model plays three roles during rollout: customer simulator, tool or function emulator, and coverage judge. The policy model only acts in the environment. That makes the signal much closer to the real agent objective than token-level imitation.

第一阶段生成 persona 和 scenario。作者先用 DeepSeek-v4.1-pro 构造 persona seed pool 和 topic/scenario seed pool，再扩展成双人对话设定。设定里不仅有姓名、性别、年龄、职业、性格、角色，还包含 scene、tone、narrative type、scene medium、三段式事件链和分段情绪弧线。这里的关键不是“让 LLM 写对话”，而是先把约束条件结构化，减少后续脚本生成时的人设漂移和场景不一致。

第四阶段注入背景声和事件声。每个说话通道配一个背景通道，背景由环境噪声和短时事件声组成。环境噪声来自 7 类场景、600 段长音频；事件声来自 52 类、2203 段短音频。事件声按脚本中的触发词和词级时间戳对齐，再控制 SNR 注入。这样得到的不是简单的“语音+噪声”，而是和语义位置绑定的声学事件。

Stage 1 builds structured persona-scenario tuples. Using DeepSeek-v4.1-pro, the authors first create persona and topic seed pools, then expand them into two-speaker dialogue setups under consistency constraints. These setups include speaker attributes, scene, tone, narrative type, scene medium, a three-event progression chain, and an emotional arc. This matters because it separates high-level consistency control from later script realization.

Stage 2 generates dialogue scripts with explicit tags. Gemini-3.1-pro-preview writes spoken-style dialogues conditioned on the structured setup, while inserting three orthogonal tag families: duplex behavior tags (interrupt, backchannel, incomplete), emotion tags aligned with IndexTTS2’s supported emotions, and environmental/sound-event tags. This is a strong design choice: instead of inferring behavior during audio generation, the script already specifies what should happen.

Stage 3 performs expressive TTS and two-track assembly. The authors use IndexTTS2 because it supports disentangled control over speaker timbre and emotion. They synthesize each utterance separately, run forced alignment to obtain word-level timestamps, and then assemble two long-form speaker tracks. Duplex behaviors are realized during assembly rather than by training a specialized overlap synthesizer: incomplete utterances are rendered via ellipsis-induced pauses, interruptions are created by fading one speaker out while bringing the other in early, and backchannels are overlaid on top of the other speaker’s continuing speech. This is a pragmatic engineering solution with clear controllability.

Stage 4 injects background noise and sound events. Each speaker channel is paired with a background channel composed of environmental noise and short sound events. Environmental clips come from a 7-category bank; sound events come from a 52-category bank. Events are aligned to trigger words using the word-level timestamps from forced alignment, with controlled SNR. This makes the non-speech audio semantically grounded rather than random augmentation.

A quality validator filters low-quality dialogues before release, and the paper also uses dual LLM judges for script rationality checks.
