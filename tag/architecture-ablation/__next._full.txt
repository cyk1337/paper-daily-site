18:T43b,For pretraining research, the paper is useful in three ways. First, it provides a clean negative result against a common intuition: giving sparse attention a learnable spacing parameter does not automatically produce meaningful depth specialization. Second, it shows that static structural priors still matter in small-to-mid-scale language modeling. When model size and token budget are limited, a carefully chosen depth schedule can outperform a more flexible learned variant. Third, it offers a concrete explanation for length extrapolation behavior: structured sparse attention is more robust at 4× training length because it avoids exposing the model to a large mass of unseen relative positions, which dense attention with RoPE does encounter. That mechanism is relevant to long-context pretraining and attention pattern design. The scope should still be read carefully: the experiments are only at roughly 60M parameters and 426M training tokens, so the contribution is controlled evidence about inductive bias rather than a production-ready recipe for frontier-scale models.19:T60a,The method is intentionally simple. Each query attends to two sets of keys: a local dense window and a set of long-range positions sampled at Fibonacci-spaced offsets. Fibonacci spacing gives denser coverage at short range and progressively sparser coverage at long range, which is smoother than power-of-2 spacing. To make α learnable, the paper avoids a hard boolean mask and instead gathers key/value rows at fractional offsets using linear interpolation. That preserves gradients to α even when offsets land near integers. α is constrained to [0.5, 1.5], since the base Fibonacci ladder already spans the 1024-token context at α=1.

The most important schedule is the static linear stagger: α increases from 0.5 in the first layer to 1.5 in the last layer across 16 layers. This encodes a simple division of labor, with lower layers focusing on local composition and upper layers expanding the receptive field. The coprime stagger uses the same set of α values but permutes them across layers with stride 7, aiming to reduce regular coverage holes by making adjacent layers maximally different. The paper also includes a reach-matched power-of-2 control to separate the effect of the Fibonacci base from the effect of depth staggering itself. A strong point of the study is experimental control: same attention implementation, same projections, same RoPE and GQA setup, same training recipe, and only the spacing schedule changes. That makes the attribution to depth scheduling much more credible than many sparse-attention comparisons.1a:T909,All runs use the same small LM recipe: 16 layers, d_model=512, 8 attention heads, 2 KV heads, head dimension 64, sequence length 1024, tied embeddings, and 60.9M total parameters. Training lasts 13k steps for 426M tokens. The data mix is about 35% FineWeb-Edu, 22% Wikipedia, 20% TinyStories, and 23% math data. In total the paper trains 21 models and sweeps local window sizes W in {6, 8, 10, 12}.

The main empirical ordering is the key takeaway: static staggering beats both fixed and learned α, and this gain is base-agnostic. Applying the same stagger to a power-of-2 base lifts it above fixed Fibonacci and roughly to parity with learned Fibonacci. That means depth staggering is the dominant effect, while the Fibonacci base adds a separate but smaller benefit. Another important result is that learned α stays close to initialization, which supports the claim that the negative result is not due to poor reporting but to weak or ineffective optimization of the spacing parameter itself.

Length extrapolation is the other major result. Models are trained at length 1024 and evaluated up to 4× that length. Structured sparse attention remains comparatively robust, while the recipe-matched dense baseline degrades sharply. The paper explains this through relative-position distribution shift: sparse attention only uses a fixed set of relative distances, so longer sequences do not introduce many unseen relative positions; dense attention does, and RoPE-based representations then operate further out of distribution. Two negative findings are also worth noting. Dense attention still wins at the training length, so sparse structure does not close the in-distribution quality gap. And the position-resolved analysis shows that the gain from staggering is fairly uniform across positions rather than concentrated only at long-range tokens, suggesting that the benefit is about better layerwise organization, not merely stronger long-distance access.

The excerpt provided here does not include every table value, so exact perplexity numbers cannot be reconstructed fully.
