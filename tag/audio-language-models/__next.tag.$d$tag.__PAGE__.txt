a:T457,Two-layer contribution:
1) Taxonomy organized by intervention point in the audio-to-reasoning pipeline: semantic-layer attacks (literal spoken jailbreak prompts, narrative framing/role-play, content dilution), acoustic-layer attacks (speaker traits, emotion, accent, language switching), signal-layer attacks (DSP artifacts, perturbations, codec/resampling effects), and embedding/representation-layer attacks (manipulating intermediate representations or discrete tokens). Defenses are grouped by deployment: guard-based filters, training-free inference-time interventions, and training-based alignment/adversarial training. Benchmarks are categorized as cross-modal, audio-native, and interactive/agentic.
2) Controlled empirical study: evaluate representative attacks/defenses on 10 open-source LALMs under a unified protocol. The key design choice is to move beyond raw ASR by explicitly accounting for practicality: compute/latency, query budget, TTS/signal-processing overhead, and detectability. Defense evaluation similarly reports ASR reduction alongside benign refusal rate (BRR) and latency overhead.b:T49f,The work has two main components. First, it builds a controlled evaluation protocol on the Speech Accessibility Project dataset. The same audio samples are evaluated under matched prompt conditions with progressively richer clinical context: zero-context control, diagnosis guidance, condensed speech characteristics, speech production, voice quality, condensed full profile, speech plus voice, full clinical profile, and follow-up correction. This design isolates the effect of context from sample difficulty. Second, the authors benchmark nine promptable audio-language models and identify three failure modes: models that are robust but do not benefit, models that degrade as more context is added, and models that are highly format-sensitive. They also examine the apparent gains of Audio Flamingo 3 and argue that the effect is mostly reduced hallucination rather than genuine use of clinical information. To test learnability, they then perform context-dependent LoRA adaptation using a mixture of prompt formats. That choice is important: the model is trained to handle both contextualized and context-free cases, which helps preserve performance when metadata is unavailable.c:T4f3,Several results stand out. In Figure 1(a), Qwen3-ASR-1.7B is around 0.134, Qwen3-Omni 30B around 0.138, Voxtral-Small-24B around 0.139, Cohere Transcribe around 0.145, and Whisper-large-v3 around 0.171, while some instruction-heavy or thinking variants perform much worse, such as Gemma-4-4B Think at about 0.321 and Audio Flamingo 3 at about 0.498. Second, on 11,218 matched samples, adding clinical context produces near-zero gains for most frozen models and often hurts performance as prompts become richer. Figure 1(b) shows Qwen3-Omni moving roughly from 0.138 to 0.144, Voxtral-Small from about 0.15 to 0.18, Phi-4 Multimodal from about 0.152 to 0.160, and Qwen3-Thinking 30B from about 0.165 to 0.180, with larger degradation for Gemma 4-4B and Audio Flamingo 3. Third, LoRA adaptation changes the picture: the adapted model learns to convert clinical context into lower WER while maintaining performance when no context is provided, and achieves the best WER among all evaluated systems. The abstract does not expose the final exact number, but the full-text conclusion is clear that the capability is learnable once the training distribution includes the right conditional structure.d:T5c1,HeadRouter is a training-free, per-sample routing method that first infers whether an input behaves more like a semantic task or an acoustic task, then reweights attention heads accordingly before scoring tokens for pruning. The method has three main steps. First, for each attention head, it computes an entropy-based selectivity score measuring how concentrated the head’s attention is over audio tokens; sharper attention implies stronger selectivity. Second, it uses the standard deviation of these selectivity scores across heads as a compact routing signal. Low variance indicates a more semantic-like regime, while high variance indicates a more acoustic-like regime, matching the authors’ empirical observation that acoustic tasks induce more heterogeneous head behavior. Third, instead of hard-switching between discrete modes, it performs Gaussian soft mixing over three pre-calibrated head-weight profiles—semantic, uniform, and acoustic—to obtain a continuous sample-specific head weighting. These weights are then used to aggregate head-level attention into token importance scores for pruning. A notable implementation detail is the use of no-RoPE selectivity statistics to reduce positional bias; prior methods tend to over-retain tail tokens. Relative to methods such as FastV or temporal subsampling, the main difference is not a more complex scorer but an explicit use of task heterogeneity and head heterogeneity, without any additional training.e:T447,The evaluation is conducted on AudioMarathon and MMAU-Pro, covering both semantic understanding and acoustic analysis. The headline result is that HeadRouter can outperform the unpruned baseline while removing 30% of audio tokens: on AudioMarathon, it improves the average score by +1.2 points for Qwen2.5-Omni-3B and +2.1 points for Qwen2.5-Omni-7B. The paper compares against fixed head profiles, uniform cross-head averaging, temporal/positional subsampling, and prior attention-based pruning baselines, and reports more stable behavior under higher pruning ratios. The most useful ablations are: (1) semantic and acoustic head profiles are genuinely different, and a single fixed profile hurts one side or the other; (2) soft profile mixing is more robust than hard routing, suggesting task regimes are not cleanly discrete; and (3) no-RoPE selectivity better matches audio structure and reduces the tail-retention bias seen in prior methods.
