Conditional entropy, and more generally data-model cross-entropy, can be expressed as the integral of its derivative with respect to the noise parameter. The nontrivial step is to turn the derivative of a joint quantity into something local and usable. The paper imports the EXIT/GEXIT machinery from coding theory and proves that for memoryless channels, the derivative decomposes into a sum of coordinate-wise local derivatives.

The main result is a mismatched conservation law for cross-entropy. Instead of assuming the denoiser equals the true posterior, the analysis allows a learned posterior family and shows that the global data-model CE is exactly the integral of local information derivatives along the noise axis. This yields several concrete consequences. One, the framework is channel-agnostic at the level of admissible memoryless corruption and recovers familiar special cases such as the Gaussian I-MMSE relation. Two, there is a locality property: the relevant derivatives depend only on marginal posteriors P(X_i | Y(τ)), not on the full joint posterior P(X | Y(τ)). This is a major simplification for high-dimensional sequence modeling. Three, the paper derives an implementation-oriented implication: it is sufficient to model an extrinsic posterior of the form P(X_i | Y_{-i}(τ)) and combine it with the known channel likelihood through a tilt. That connects the theory to practical token-wise denoisers and to leave-one-out style parameterizations. Four, the paper makes a sharp distinction between matched and mismatched regimes. In the matched limit, all admissible channels conserve the same CE. In finite-capacity settings, however, different channels define posterior families of different complexity, which leads to channel-dependent performance gaps.1c:T68e,The experiments are designed to validate the finite-capacity claim rather than to optimize benchmark rankings.

The first set uses synthetic Markov sources with known entropy rates. This setting allows direct comparison between the theoretically conserved quantity and what finite denoisers recover under different channels. By varying alphabet size, the paper shows that channel-dependent gaps grow when the posterior family becomes harder to approximate. This is the cleanest empirical support for the main thesis.

The second set studies character-level language modeling on text8. The key comparison is between discrete noising paths and Gaussian noising paths under comparable model capacity. The paper reports substantial channel-dependent differences in CE/NLL, indicating that some channels align better with the discrete structure of text and are easier for finite denoisers to learn. The most useful plots here are the decompositions over noise levels, which show where the model pays the CE cost along the path.

The third set uses CIFAR-10 to show that the same phenomenon is not specific to text. High-dimensional image data also exhibits large finite-capacity, channel-dependent gaps. The practical message is that the conservation law is universal, but the approximation burden imposed on a given model family is channel-specific.

The abstract already states the main empirical takeaway: synthetic data, text8, and CIFAR-10 all show large finite-capacity, channel-dependent gaps.
