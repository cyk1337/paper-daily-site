9:T470,Two-part evaluation protocol:
1) Latent–concept matching (correlational): given binary human annotations Y (attributes) and SAE latent activations z_l across samples, binarize latents and match them to attributes. The key design is allowing many-to-one mappings (one concept ↔ a coalition of latents) to handle feature splitting. They propose a coalition-based objective and a Fully-Binary Matching Pursuit (FBMP) procedure that greedily builds a latent set per attribute to optimize a set-level F-score (they recommend F0.5 to emphasize precision).
2) Targeted Attribute Perturbation Alignment (interventional): correlational matching can be confounded by co-occurrence, so they introduce paired-image benchmarks where exactly one attribute changes. They build synCUB and synCOCO as synthetic minimal-pair datasets. TAPAScore then checks whether the matched latent set for an attribute responds selectively and in the correct direction on these targeted perturbations.
They additionally run sanity checks showing that several existing matching metrics can behave poorly under simple controls, while FBMP + TAPAScore is more robust.a:T42c,This matters for pretraining-oriented multimodal research in at least three ways. First, it gives a more precise diagnostic than accuracy or ablation. A model can score well while still relying mostly on language priors; PID can distinguish that from genuine cross-modal fusion. That is directly relevant to native multimodal pretraining, data mixture design, and alignment losses. Second, the paper pushes PID beyond analysis into training-time utility: local PID scores are used for sample reweighting during lightweight LoRA tuning, improving reasoning and grounding while shifting the model toward higher synergy and lower text-only reliance. That makes PID a candidate signal for curriculum design, data filtering, and shortcut suppression. Third, the tri-modal extension treats language as a control variable and audio/video as sensory evidence. This is a practical modeling choice for omni-modal systems because it avoids the combinatorial blow-up of full multi-source PID while matching the instruction-conditioned setting used by modern multimodal assistants.b:T735,There are three main technical ideas. First, the decomposition is computed on a model-induced joint distribution rather than directly on raw data statistics. The paper defines P(x_v, x_t, y) = P_D(x_v, x_t) p_theta(y | x_v, x_t), and all mutual information terms are measured on this empirical decision distribution. This makes the quantities reflect how the model functionally uses information in its predictions. Second, for the bimodal case, the authors adopt a PID formulation in the Bertschinger family, yielding non-negative unique, redundant, and synergistic components under constrained couplings that preserve selected marginals. To obtain unimodal predictive distributions, they do not simply drop a modality; instead they use calibrated embedding masking so that p_theta(y | x_v) and p_theta(y | x_t) stay closer to the model’s training manifold and are less distorted by crude out-of-distribution ablations. Third, for tri-modal systems they introduce Sensory PID by conditioning on language T and decomposing I(Y; V, A | T). This reframes language as task specification and focuses the decomposition on sensory evidence integration. It is a tractable compromise: full three-source PID is expensive and hard to estimate, while the conditional version still answers the practically important question of whether audio and video are jointly used once the instruction is fixed.

The training-side extension is deliberately modest but well chosen. The paper derives local PID-style diagnostic scores and uses them to reweight samples during LoRA fine-tuning: under-synergized examples are upweighted, language-shortcut examples are downweighted. Instead of directly optimizing a fragile information-theoretic objective, the method uses PID as a data-selection signal. That is much easier to integrate into realistic training pipelines.c:T7a6,The empirical study spans 20 vision-language models and 6 benchmarks, with the emphasis on diagnosis rather than leaderboard gains. Several findings stand out.

First, PID profiles align with task structure. Reasoning-heavy and grounding-oriented benchmarks tend to show higher synergy, indicating that correct decisions depend on joint visual-textual evidence. Expert or knowledge-centric settings show stronger text-unique contributions, suggesting heavier reliance on language priors or parametric memory. The paper argues that these profiles emerge from the decomposition itself rather than from benchmark labels, and that they recur across model families.

Second, different PID terms predict different behaviors under intervention. Total mutual information tracks overall accuracy reasonably well, but synergy is a better predictor of sensitivity to image removal. This is important because two models can have similar ablation drops for different reasons: one may rely on visual-unique information, another on genuinely joint evidence. PID separates those cases.

Third, the omni-modal analysis reveals a sensory synergy bottleneck. On audio-visual fusion tasks, current models are still dominated by modality-unique sensory information, especially visual information, while audio-visual synergy remains comparatively small. In practical terms, many systems that appear multimodal are still not integrating sensory streams strongly at the decision level.

Fourth, the PID-guided reweighting experiment shows that lightweight fine-tuning can move both performance and decision structure in the same direction. The method improves reasoning and grounding over uniform fine-tuning and matched reweighting baselines, while post-tuning PID profiles shift toward higher synergy and lower text-unique reliance.
