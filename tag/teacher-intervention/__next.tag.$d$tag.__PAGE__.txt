9:T7df,PIVOT can be summarized as online local correction followed by student continuation, plus KL supervision on the corrected span. During training, the student first generates a rollout prefix. At a chosen intervention point, the teacher produces a correction conditioned on that student-generated prefix. The student then continues generation from the corrected context rather than from its original mistaken continuation. This produces teacher-assisted trajectories that are more likely to end in verifier-approved solutions, while still preserving part of the student’s own rollout distribution. That is a key difference from simply injecting full teacher trajectories.

The optimization has two coupled parts. First, the resulting trajectories are still optimized with the standard RLVR objective, instantiated with GRPO and verifier-based rewards. This keeps the training target anchored to task correctness rather than pure imitation. Second, a teacher-student KL loss is applied only on the correction segments, providing dense token-level supervision exactly where the teacher intervened. Instead of distilling the entire sequence, the method concentrates supervision on the local regions that are most responsible for steering the trajectory back toward a valid reasoning path.

Mechanistically, the method addresses both bottlenecks at once. Online intervention changes the rollout distribution and increases access to reward-bearing trajectories. Local KL makes those interventions learnable by turning them into parameter updates the student can reproduce later. The paper further studies practical design choices: where intervention should happen, how much teacher guidance to inject, and how the KL term should be used. A recurring conclusion is that too-early intervention risks turning training into teacher-led generation, while too-late intervention cannot rescue already broken trajectories. Either intervention alone or token supervision alone is often insufficient; the combination is the point.a:T63e,The evaluation covers two hard reasoning settings: logic games from SynLogic and mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with both in-distribution and out-of-distribution testing on the math side. The headline result is on hard logic games, where RL-only training makes limited progress: PIVOT improves mean avg@8 from 19.5% for the best baseline to 35.6%, close to a 2x gain. That is a meaningful result because it directly targets the sparse-reward regime where standard RLVR tends to stall.

The most informative ablations are not just score comparisons but mechanism tests. The authors isolate trajectory-level intervention and token-level supervision, showing that each one alone can still fail to produce strong learning. Their combination gives the strongest gains, which directly supports the paper’s framing. They also study intervention placement and the KL design, showing that teacher correction is not simply “more is better.” The intervention has to occur at a point where the student can still continue productively, while being early enough to change the probability of eventual success. On mathematical reasoning tasks, PIVOT consistently beats the evaluated baselines on both ID and OOD benchmarks, suggesting that the gains are not limited to a single synthetic logic environment.
