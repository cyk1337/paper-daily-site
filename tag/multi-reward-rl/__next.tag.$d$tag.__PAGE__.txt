b:T6ca,The method has three main components.

First, policy decomposition. For N rewards, PRISM does not train one policy to maximize all of them jointly. It trains N positive policies, each dedicated to one reward, plus one global negative policy that captures undesirable behavior across rewards. The design choice is deliberate: desirable behavior is often reward-specific, while failure modes can often be pooled under a shared “do not do this” branch.

Second, efficient parameter sharing. PRISM does not instantiate N+1 separate full models. It uses one shared LM and conditions different sub-policies with different prefix tokens. This keeps training and serving practical while preserving a shared representation backbone.

Third, policy composition. At rollout and inference time, PRISM combines sub-policies by taking a weighted sum of their logits, producing a mixture policy. The merge weights become an explicit preference-control interface. Unlike reward weights, these coefficients act on already-optimized policy distributions rather than on raw reward scales, which is the authors’ main argument for improved robustness and interpretability.

Optimization is built on GRPO. Each reward mainly updates its corresponding positive branch, while the negative branch absorbs failure signals across rewards. Relative to linear scalarization, adaptive reward weighting, or constrained/Lagrangian formulations, the novelty is not in the policy-gradient estimator but in moving composition from reward space to policy space. The paper does not deny Pareto trade-offs; it argues that trade-offs are better handled after separate optimization, through policy mixing, than by collapsing all objectives into one scalar during training.c:T668,The evaluation covers three multi-reward settings: scientific QA, tool use, and helpfulness–safety alignment.

1. Scientific QA: On GPQA and ScienceQA, PRISM achieves the best overall score on every tested backbone. It improves over the strongest baseline by 17.8, 8.0, and 0.6 points on DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, respectively. The gains appear on both smaller and stronger instruction-tuned models, though the magnitude varies.

2. Tool use: On BFCL-v3, PRISM ranks first among RL methods. This is an important setting because the reward dimensions are more realistic and more entangled than in plain QA: task success, tool formatting, tool selection, and possibly safety constraints all interact.

3. Safety alignment: On Alpaca, HH-RLHF, and PKU-SafeRLHF, PRISM again outperforms competing RL baselines and remains more robust as reward complexity increases. The paper’s emphasis is less on one headline number and more on the fact that reward-space baselines degrade more sharply when the number of objectives and the degree of conflict grow.
