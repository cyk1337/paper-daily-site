18:T524,The method is Adaptive Multi-Reward Projection, or AMRP. Instead of using a fixed weighted sum of reward dimensions throughout training, AMRP updates the projection weights online based on training dynamics. It uses three signals.

The first is relative shortfall, which measures whether a reward dimension is lagging behind the others. If a dimension remains under-optimized, its weight is increased. The second is reward volatility, which tracks instability in a dimension. High volatility suggests that the policy has not stabilized there, so AMRP keeps attention on it. The third is recent progress, which measures whether a dimension is improving, stagnating, or regressing. Saturated and stable dimensions get down-weighted; stagnant or regressing dimensions get up-weighted.

This design addresses the two identified failure modes in a practical way. It does not eliminate the information loss of scalarization entirely, but it reduces persistent aliasing by making the projection time-varying rather than static. More importantly, it counters shortcut lock-in by reallocating learning pressure toward neglected dimensions. Compared with manual reward tuning or simple normalization, the key difference is that AMRP is driven by observed reward dynamics during training rather than by fixed prior assumptions.19:T4a0,The evaluation covers three multi-reward settings: rule-based mathematical reasoning, citation-grounded generation with automatic evaluators, and open-ended alignment using learned reward-model scores. The main empirical claim is that AMRP consistently improves downstream performance across all three, with especially strong gains in math reasoning and citation-grounded generation, and smaller but still consistent gains in the more correlated open-ended alignment setting.

The paper also provides mechanism-level evidence. It shows that under static aggregation, training is often dominated early by dense rewards such as format or length, while harder dimensions such as correctness remain under-optimized. AMRP changes the reward-profile dynamics by shifting weight toward lagging or unstable dimensions, which leads to more balanced profiles over time. Another useful result is compatibility: the method composes with PPO, GRPO, and GDPO, suggesting that it is best viewed as a reward-aggregation module rather than a replacement for the RL optimizer itself. The emphasis is less on one dramatic benchmark number and more on consistent gains plus healthier reward trajectories.1a:T6ca,The method has three main components.

First, policy decomposition. For N rewards, PRISM does not train one policy to maximize all of them jointly. It trains N positive policies, each dedicated to one reward, plus one global negative policy that captures undesirable behavior across rewards. The design choice is deliberate: desirable behavior is often reward-specific, while failure modes can often be pooled under a shared “do not do this” branch.

Second, efficient parameter sharing. PRISM does not instantiate N+1 separate full models. It uses one shared LM and conditions different sub-policies with different prefix tokens. This keeps training and serving practical while preserving a shared representation backbone.

Third, policy composition. At rollout and inference time, PRISM combines sub-policies by taking a weighted sum of their logits, producing a mixture policy. The merge weights become an explicit preference-control interface. Unlike reward weights, these coefficients act on already-optimized policy distributions rather than on raw reward scales, which is the authors’ main argument for improved robustness and interpretability.

Optimization is built on GRPO. Each reward mainly updates its corresponding positive branch, while the negative branch absorbs failure signals across rewards. Relative to linear scalarization, adaptive reward weighting, or constrained/Lagrangian formulations, the novelty is not in the policy-gradient estimator but in moving composition from reward space to policy space. The paper does not deny Pareto trade-offs; it argues that trade-offs are better handled after separate optimization, through policy mixing, than by collapsing all objectives into one scalar during training.1b:T668,The evaluation covers three multi-reward settings: scientific QA, tool use, and helpfulness–safety alignment.

1. Scientific QA: On GPQA and ScienceQA, PRISM achieves the best overall score on every tested backbone. It improves over the strongest baseline by 17.8, 8.0, and 0.6 points on DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, respectively. The gains appear on both smaller and stronger instruction-tuned models, though the magnitude varies.

2. Tool use: On BFCL-v3, PRISM ranks first among RL methods. This is an important setting because the reward dimensions are more realistic and more entangled than in plain QA: task success, tool formatting, tool selection, and possibly safety constraints all interact.

3. Safety alignment: On Alpaca, HH-RLHF, and PKU-SafeRLHF, PRISM again outperforms competing RL baselines and remains more robust as reward complexity increases. The paper’s emphasis is less on one headline number and more on the fact that reward-space baselines degrade more sharply when the number of objectives and the degree of conflict grow.

The most informative ablations are the following:
- policy-space composition vs reward scalarization;
- with vs without the global negative policy;
- inference-time controllability under different merge weights;
- stability as reward complexity increases.
