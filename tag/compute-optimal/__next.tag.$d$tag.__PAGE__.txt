9:T8c7,The article starts from older learning-curve literature, including Amari, Hestness, and Rosenfeld, to make the point that power-law behavior predates modern LLMs. In that framing, scaling laws are empirical descriptions of how error changes with capacity, data, and compute. It then revisits Kaplan et al.: test loss scales as a power law with parameter count N, dataset size D, and compute C, and the joint form implies that overfitting is largely controlled by a ratio like N^(alpha/beta)/D. The important caveat is that Kaplan’s compute-optimal recommendation depends on the experimental range and fitting procedure, which pushed the optimum toward larger models trained on relatively fewer tokens.

The Chinchilla section is the core. It breaks down three fitting strategies. Method 1 fixes model size and varies the token budget to identify the best token count per model. Method 2 uses IsoFLOP profiles, comparing different N–D allocations under fixed total compute. Method 3 fits a parametric joint loss function directly. The article explains why these are not interchangeable: token sweeps reveal undertraining more directly, IsoFLOP studies align better with real compute allocation, and parametric fits are efficient but sensitive to functional assumptions and sampling coverage.

A particularly useful part is the discussion of fitting pitfalls. If experiments are concentrated in a narrow region, especially one that is effectively data-infinite or strongly model-limited, the fitted exponents can be systematically biased. If partially trained runs are treated as if they reflected compute-optimal behavior, the resulting recommendation will favor oversized, undertrained models. The toy simulation makes this concrete: even when the ground-truth law is known, limited coverage and noise can produce stable but wrong exponents. That is the real reconciliation between Kaplan and Chinchilla: not a simple contradiction, but different estimation procedures operating under different regimes.

The final section on why power laws appear is interpretive rather than formal.
