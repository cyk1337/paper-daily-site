a:T7d8,There are three important design choices. First, evaluation is suite-level rather than item-level. Each episode is a six-problem contest under one total budget, spanning mathematics, competitive programming, and abstract reasoning, with 50 contests per domain. Problems are drawn from sources such as Omni-MATH, MathNet, and LiveCodeBench Pro, and mixed to induce heterogeneous resource demands. Second, the benchmark has both tool-free and agentic settings. In the tool-free setting, the budget is output tokens; in the agentic setting, the budget is actions in an interactive shell environment. This lets the authors compare pure generation-time allocation with tool-using allocation. Third, and most importantly, the paper uses matched single-problem calibration. For each problem, the same model is run in isolation under varying budgets to obtain empirical response curves. From these runs, the authors build two controls: an equal-allocation replay and a same-model empirical oracle. Equal allocation simply divides the total budget evenly across the six problems. The oracle is an offline diagnostic that selects a feasible set of observed single-problem successes under the contest budget, representing what the model has already shown it can do if allocation were ideal. Because the oracle is built from demonstrated successes of the same model, the gap between oracle and contest score is evidence of allocation failure rather than missing capability. The paper also studies lightweight online schedulers. Some improve mean performance in certain model-domain cells, but no fixed scheduler transfers uniformly across domains, suggesting that allocation policy is strongly domain-dependent. The trajectory analysis is also useful: under strong pressure, failures are often caused by exhausting the shared budget on other problems; under moderate pressure, failures more often come from stopping after partial progress. Both are allocation errors on problems the model can already solve in isolation.b:T537,The main empirical pattern is unusually clean. Across 72 flagship model-setting-pressure-domain cells, the empirical oracle matches or exceeds actual contest performance in all 72 cells and is strictly better in 71. That indicates a persistent realization gap under shared budgets. A stronger result is that under moderate tool-free pressure, even naive equal allocation outperforms the model’s own contest policy for 3 of the 6 models. In other words, some models allocate compute online worse than a trivial uniform baseline. The paper also reports that five of six models cover more than half of the suite, yet only DS-Pro exceeds the descriptive 50% reference for resource-rational strategy updates, showing that broad coverage is not the same as rational allocation. Another important result is that general capability does not explain the gap: models within two points on a composite built from more than fifty benchmarks can differ by as much as 60.6 percentage points in Gap Ratio. So shared-budget competence is not well captured by standard capability rankings. In the online scheduler study, 6 of 9 model-domain cells show higher mean performance, but no scheduler works reliably across domains. Empirically, group states cluster into indifference, polarization, and consensus, while individual trajectories include frozen opinions, one-time switches, reversals, and repeated oscillations. Interaction tends to increase conviction and move communities toward more ordered states. On objective math questions, communication improves average collective accuracy: initially wrong majorities switch to the correct answer more often than initially correct majorities become wrong. Parameter analysis indicates a low-temperature ordered regime, stronger concordant than discordant interactions, and greater fitted influence from agents currently holding the correct answer on objective questions.d:T688,The central idea is a harness-guided benchmark construction framework. Instead of starting from a fixed task set and asking models to optimize harnesses on it, the authors first induce a diverse set of candidate harnesses on auxiliary tasks, then use those harnesses to identify tasks that are genuinely responsive to harness quality. This design choice is important: if tasks are not harness-sensitive, the benchmark will mostly measure base-model strength.

The framework has three main components. First, harness-sensitive task construction: candidate tasks are retained only if performance varies meaningfully across different harnesses under a fixed policy model. Second, sensitivity-aware stratified splitting: tasks are split into validation and evaluation sets not by naive random partitioning, but by preserving distributions over sensitivity and related task characteristics, while keeping the suites disjoint. This is meant to reduce task-specific overfitting to the validation set. Third, controlled long-horizon evolution: at each iteration, the evolver sees cumulative validation-side evidence from previous harnesses, including aggregate scores and task-level outcomes, then edits the executable harness code and reruns the fixed policy model. This keeps the benchmark focused on iterative scaffold improvement under a fixed underlying model.

Methodologically, the paper is less about a new evolver algorithm and more about controlling the coupling among evolver, policy model, and task suite. A notable framing is that evolved harnesses are treated as transferable reasoning structures, closer to reusable external programs than to model-specific prompts.e:T6e9,The main evaluation covers three domains—Search, Office, and General Agent tasks—built from five established benchmarks, with 9 frontier and open-weight models used as evolvers. Under a fixed policy model, the strongest evolvers are GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 4.8, improving over the seed harness by 16.6 and 16.1 points respectively, approaching a human-engineered baseline of 47.5. The result shows that current top models can make substantial scaffold improvements, but still leave a noticeable gap to manual engineering.

The analysis is more informative than the headline numbers. First, evolution shows clear early saturation: models discover useful structures quickly in early rounds, then often introduce harmful modifications later. That suggests current systems are better at finding obvious fixes than at maintaining and extending a working scaffold without regressions. Second, gains are strongly domain-dependent. Search benefits the most, likely because web-navigation structure can be replicated and stabilized. General tasks sometimes allow evolved harnesses to surpass manual engineering. Office tasks remain difficult because they require highly specific processing workflows that are hard to infer from limited validation feedback. Third, transfer experiments show that synthesized harnesses improve multiple policy models, including Qwen, DeepSeek, and GLM families. This supports the claim that the evolved artifacts are not merely prompt hacks tied to one base model, but more general execution structures.
