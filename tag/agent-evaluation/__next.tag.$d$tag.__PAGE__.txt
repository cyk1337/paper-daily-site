9:T4c4,There are three main design choices. First, the environment connects Civilization VI to an MCP interface through FireTuner over TCP and exposes 76 tools spanning state queries, unit control, city management, diplomacy, research, religion, trade, and lifecycle operations. This makes the agent operate in a tool-mediated setting rather than a fixed observation API. Second, the narration layer converts visual game state into structured text, but crucially does not automatically inject all relevant information into context. Information is representable, but not passively observed; non-local state must be explicitly queried. This allows the benchmark to distinguish unavailable information from available-but-unretrieved information. Third, the paper introduces behavior-level metrics instead of relying on leaderboard-style aggregate outcomes. PMR, Proactive Monitoring Rate, measures whether the agent actively checks latent strategic state such as victory progress or rival status. RAG@K measures whether a textual commitment is followed by corresponding actions within the next K steps. PMR captures whether the agent keeps checking the dashboard; RAG@K captures whether stated plans are translated into execution.a:T7d8,There are three important design choices. First, evaluation is suite-level rather than item-level. Each episode is a six-problem contest under one total budget, spanning mathematics, competitive programming, and abstract reasoning, with 50 contests per domain. Problems are drawn from sources such as Omni-MATH, MathNet, and LiveCodeBench Pro, and mixed to induce heterogeneous resource demands. Second, the benchmark has both tool-free and agentic settings. In the tool-free setting, the budget is output tokens; in the agentic setting, the budget is actions in an interactive shell environment. This lets the authors compare pure generation-time allocation with tool-using allocation. Third, and most importantly, the paper uses matched single-problem calibration. For each problem, the same model is run in isolation under varying budgets to obtain empirical response curves. From these runs, the authors build two controls: an equal-allocation replay and a same-model empirical oracle. Equal allocation simply divides the total budget evenly across the six problems. The oracle is an offline diagnostic that selects a feasible set of observed single-problem successes under the contest budget, representing what the model has already shown it can do if allocation were ideal. Because the oracle is built from demonstrated successes of the same model, the gap between oracle and contest score is evidence of allocation failure rather than missing capability. The paper also studies lightweight online schedulers. Some improve mean performance in certain model-domain cells, but no fixed scheduler transfers uniformly across domains, suggesting that allocation policy is strongly domain-dependent. The trajectory analysis is also useful: under strong pressure, failures are often caused by exhausting the shared budget on other problems; under moderate pressure, failures more often come from stopping after partial progress. Both are allocation errors on problems the model can already solve in isolation.b:T537,The main empirical pattern is unusually clean. Across 72 flagship model-setting-pressure-domain cells, the empirical oracle matches or exceeds actual contest performance in all 72 cells and is strictly better in 71. That indicates a persistent realization gap under shared budgets. A stronger result is that under moderate tool-free pressure, even naive equal allocation outperforms the model’s own contest policy for 3 of the 6 models. In other words, some models allocate compute online worse than a trivial uniform baseline. The paper also reports that five of six models cover more than half of the suite, yet only DS-Pro exceeds the descriptive 50% reference for resource-rational strategy updates, showing that broad coverage is not the same as rational allocation. Another important result is that general capability does not explain the gap: models within two points on a composite built from more than fifty benchmarks can differ by as much as 60.6 percentage points in Gap Ratio. So shared-budget competence is not well captured by standard capability rankings.
