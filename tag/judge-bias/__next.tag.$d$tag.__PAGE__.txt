9:T6a4,The methodology is carefully controlled. The authors introduce JUPE, a VQA-style benchmark where each item fixes the image, question, candidate answer, and correctness label, while varying only the explanation condition. This isolates the causal effect of explanation text on judgment. Judges output one of four ordinal labels—definitely incorrect, probably incorrect, probably correct, definitely correct—which are also collapsed into reject versus accept for binary analyses. To reduce prompt artifacts, the option order is randomized, the model answers with A–D choices, and the prompt uses an image-first layout. The benchmark spans three visually grounded domains: medical VQA, chart reasoning, and hallucination-prone visual questions, drawing from VQA-RAD, PMC-VQA, ChartQA, ChartQAPro, HaloQuest, and PRE-HAL. The main set contains about 180 queries, plus a 70-item induced-wrong easy set built from questions that profiling models usually solve correctly and then paired with intentionally wrong answers. This is a strong design choice because it tests whether explanations can flip judgments even on items that are otherwise easy. Explanation conditions include jointly generated chain-of-thought rationales, plain post-hoc justifications, and persuasion-oriented arguments built using 41 persuasion techniques. A key analytical move is the use of signal detection theory. Rather than relying on accuracy alone, the paper separates shifts in decision criterion from changes in discriminability. That matters because a judge can become more accepting of both correct and incorrect answers, leaving overall accuracy nearly unchanged while substantially weakening wrong-answer rejection.a:T5d0,The evaluation covers 14 MLLM judges from 5 model families. The main finding is consistent: ordinary plausible explanations are enough to materially reduce rejection of incorrect answers. On average, plain justifications reduce the rejection rate of wrong answers by 12.7 percentage points, chain-of-thought explanations by 13.8 points, and explicitly persuasive explanations by 12.5 points. The similarity across these conditions is important because it shows the problem does not require specialized persuasion attacks; normal-looking explanations already trigger it. A stronger result is that among wrong answers judged definitely incorrect without explanations, 36% to 39% become accepted once a plausible explanation is added. The signal-detection analysis indicates that the dominant effect is a shift in decision criterion toward acceptance, not a clear mean improvement in discriminability. In other words, the judges are not becoming better at reading the image; they are becoming more willing to trust the answer. The paper also checks robustness across domains, an out-of-distribution grounding set, different explanation generators, image degradation settings, and rating protocols. Common mitigations such as caution prompting and solve-first verification show limited or inconsistent gains. For anyone using judge models in training loops, the practical implication is straightforward: answer-plus-explanation formats can systematically inflate pass rates for wrong outputs.b:T864,This is a perspective and framework paper rather than a single-method contribution. Its technical content has three main layers.

First, it formalizes LLM development decisions in the potential-outcomes framework. A system choice is treated as an intervention or treatment A, observed context as X, and the outcome of interest as Y. In this setup, A may represent a pretraining data mixture, prompt template, routing assignment, retrieval policy, judge configuration, or alignment strategy; Y may be perplexity, task success, preference score, safety metric, latency, or a combined utility. This turns common engineering questions into estimands such as E[Y(a)] or treatment effects.

Second, the paper maps this framing across the LLM pipeline: pretraining data decisions, alignment and preference collection, deployment-time routing, evaluation through arenas and LLM-as-a-judge, and agentic workflows. The recurring argument is that these settings rely heavily on observational logs rather than randomized data, and those logs are shaped by prior policies, annotator behavior, and evolving environments. As a result, naive comparisons can be badly confounded. The routing example is especially clear: if harder prompts are systematically sent to larger models, raw logged outcomes can make the larger model look worse.

Third, the paper discusses practical principles for applying causal inference in LLM settings, including identifiability, confounding control, overlap, robustness, and the use of modern causal ML tools in high-dimensional regimes. It does not present one unified estimator, but it does make a concrete case for why observational analysis is often insufficient and where causal methods can improve statistical validity and data efficiency.

From a pretraining perspective, the most useful move is the explicit treatment of data-mixture design as an intervention problem. That framing naturally leads to counterfactual questions: what would have happened if a domain had been excluded, or if its weight had changed, after accounting for interactions with tokenizer behavior, training budget, curriculum, and evaluation overlap?c:T41e,This is best read with a concrete problem in mind rather than linearly as a general survey.
