Its main contribution is a practical alternative to explicit paired cross-modal supervision: use text as an intermediate semantic space, rely on pretrained contrastive encoders for cross-modal priors, and learn bridge modules that map proxy descriptions into representations consumable by the language model. This matters because in many realistic settings, single-modality or modality-text data are abundant, while high-quality jointly sampled non-text data are the real bottleneck. The paper is also careful about its claim: MSP does not remove cross-modal priors altogether. It still depends on CLIP/CLAP-like encoders and modality-text supervision. So the reduction is in the need for real paired non-text supervision, not in the need for pretrained cross-modal structure. For native multimodal pretraining, the key takeaway is that text-mediated alignment may be sufficient for some forms of modality composition, though likely more at the semantic level than at fine-grained temporal or spatial correspondence.19:T646,The method has four components: frozen modality encoders, modality-specific projectors, text-to-modality bridges, and a language model. The encoders are pretrained contrastive models, such as CLIP-like vision encoders and CLAP-like audio encoders. Training proceeds in two stages. Stage one is per-modality projector training, where standard modality-text data are used to train projectors so that each modality can be mapped into a shared language embedding space. Stage two is self-play composition training, which is the core idea. Given one observed modality, say an image, the system generates proxy text for an unseen modality, for example a textual description of what the corresponding audio might contain. This proxy text is encoded by a pretrained text encoder and then passed through a bridge plus an MLP projector to produce a representation in the shared language space. During training, the model sees the real observed modality together with the proxy-derived representation of the missing modality, but never the true image-audio pair. The goal is not to reconstruct the unseen modality exactly. The goal is to provide a stable semantic anchor that lets the model learn how to combine modalities in the shared space. Compared with approaches based on shared encoders or modular concatenation, MSP explicitly uses text as the bridge and adds a learned calibration layer to correct the mismatch between proxy-text embeddings and real modality embeddings. That calibration is important because raw text-encoder outputs do not naturally follow the same distribution as actual modality features.1a:T601,The main experimental setting is strong and clean: no real audio-vision pairs are used in training, only image-text and audio-text supervision, while evaluation requires joint multimodal reasoning. The paper reports competitive and in some cases leading results on standard multimodal benchmarks, supporting the claim that zero-shot modality composition can emerge. The most informative findings are in the ablations rather than in any single leaderboard number. First, removing the learned bridge weakens alignment between proxy-derived embeddings and real modality representations, and multimodal reasoning performance drops accordingly. Second, proxy quality matters: if the generated proxy text is vague or semantically unstable, the model tends to fall back to single-modality reasoning or language priors. Third, the method benefits heavily from the pretrained contrastive encoders, which means MSP is better understood as composition learning on top of existing cross-modal geometry rather than fully unsupervised multimodal grounding.
