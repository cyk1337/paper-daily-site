9:T90d,Methodologically, this is a survey, but not a standard taxonomy survey. The authors first narrow the scope to the generative route of world models: models that map current state and action to a distribution over future observations or states. Policy-route systems are discussed as context, but not treated as simulator substitutes because their interface is fundamentally different from a state-feedback simulator. They then define an eight-capability framework: C1 asset construction, C2 physics engine, C3 interaction, C4 controllability, C5 stability, C6 state feedback, C7 diversity, and C8 evaluation metrics. This framing asks what responsibilities a model can actually assume in a simulator stack, rather than what model family it belongs to.

The main analytical innovations are threefold. First, the benchmark is external: traditional simulator capabilities, not self-referential world-model categories. That makes the gap between “looks plausible” and “simulates correctly” much easier to expose. Second, the paper uses paper-level coding to build a reproducible evidence map rather than a purely narrative review. Third, it treats state feedback as a first-class capability and quantifies it with a B1–B5 labeling scheme. This is one of the strongest parts of the paper. Many world models can emit pixels, tokens, or latent states, but they do not provide explicit object states, physical variables, queryable interfaces, or structured feedback that planners, controllers, or verifiers can reliably consume.

The paper’s overall diagnosis is also sharper than the usual “long-horizon generation is hard.” It argues that current progress is relatively stronger on interaction, controllability, and some aspects of stability, especially in short-horizon action-conditioned prediction and interactive rollouts. The structural gaps remain asset construction, physics, and state feedback. The reason is straightforward: data-driven generative models fit observation distributions well, but they do not naturally encode explicit geometry, contact dynamics, conservation laws, compositional object structure, or editable internal states. Hallucination, geometry drift, implausible collisions, and inconsistent consequences under identical actions are all treated as symptoms of missing invariants.a:T624,This is not an experimental methods paper; its empirical contribution is a corpus audit. The motivation comes from empirical analysis: hidden features in diffusion models show strong heterogeneity across models, timestep segments, and feature dimensions. Some components evolve smoothly and are suitable for higher-order extrapolation; others are unstable and exhibit abrupt changes, making direct prediction unreliable. The difficulty is that these components are interleaved in the original feature space, so simple manual partitioning does not work.

To address this, LinCa introduces a lightweight invertible network that maps cached features into a new representation space. In that space, features are decomposed into sub-components with different continuity and predictability properties. Stable components are predicted from historical caches using polynomial extrapolation, and the prediction order is matched to the component rather than shared globally. Unstable components are not extrapolated; they are simply reused from the nearest cache. The processed sub-components are then mapped back to the original feature space through the inverse transform. The role of invertibility is important: it prevents information loss during decomposition and allows the training objective to be defined directly on reconstructed features.

A second key design is timestep segmentation. Instead of training one global predictor for the entire denoising trajectory, the method splits timesteps into several segments and trains isomorphic but independently parameterized predictors for each segment. This matters because early, middle, and late denoising stages often have very different hidden-state dynamics, and a single predictor tends to fail in some regions.

Training overhead is intentionally small. LinCa does not need to load or finetune the original diffusion model. It only uses a small set of pre-generated intermediate features as supervision. The paper reports 100-200 feature samples, less than one hour of training on a single 12GB GPU, and parameter overhead below 0.2% of the base model. From a systems perspective, this keeps much of the deployment convenience of training-free caching while replacing brittle heuristics with a lightweight learned predictor.d:T782,The evaluation covers FLUX.1-dev, Qwen-Image, Qwen-Image-Edit, and HunyuanVideo, and also tests compatibility with distilled and quantized models. The main result is that LinCa preserves generation quality much better than prior caching methods at high acceleration ratios while keeping nearly the same runtime profile. Reported speedups include 5.51× on FLUX.1-dev, 6.95× on Qwen-Image, 7.08× on Qwen-Image-Edit, and 5.50× on HunyuanVideo, with the paper describing the quality loss as near-lossless.

The most important evidence is not the headline speedup table but the validation of the paper’s core premise: heterogeneous feature dynamics. Full attention allows bidirectional interaction and may help holistic representation learning, but causal attention works better in practice for semantic-token generation.
