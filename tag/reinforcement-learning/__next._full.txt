18:T6b4,The method, FactoSR, follows a two-stage recipe: supervised spatial fine-tuning first, then factorized reinforcement learning. In the SFT stage, the model is taught to produce structured outputs that separate observation, localization, reasoning, and final answer. In the RL stage, the authors build on RLVR with GRPO-style optimization, but replace the usual single correctness reward with a multi-objective reward design.

There are five reward components. A format reward keeps outputs structurally valid during RL. An accuracy reward preserves standard final-answer supervision. The key novelty lies in three geometry-aware rewards. The XY reward enforces cross-view planar consistency and correspondence, encouraging the model to identify the correct region before answering. The Z reward targets 3D localization and relative depth ordering, which is critical for front/back, near/far, and occlusion reasoning. The T reward enforces temporal cycle consistency and reversible camera-motion reasoning, pushing the model to remain coherent over sequences rather than treating frames independently.

This is not an architectural change to the backbone. It is a change in task decomposition and training signal design. Relative to prior spatial RLVR work, the differences are clear: it moves from final-answer-only rewards to factorized rewards, from mostly single-view reasoning to multi-view and temporal settings, and from implicit language-space reasoning to explicitly geometry-constrained optimization. The trade-off is also clear: the approach depends on datasets and annotations that support verifiable XY/Z/T rewards, so scalability and transfer will depend on how broadly those rewards can be instantiated.19:T59b,The headline results reported in the paper are gains of 5.9% on VSI-Bench and 4.5% on All-Angles-Bench, while maintaining decent general multimodal capability. The experimental story is less about one benchmark and more about whether the factorized rewards produce separable gains on separable spatial skills.

The key ablations compare SFT only, SFT plus standard RLVR, and SFT plus FactoSR-RL. The paper’s main claim is that answer-only RL helps somewhat, but remains unstable on depth, cross-view correspondence, and temporal consistency. Adding XY/Z/T rewards yields larger improvements on tasks that require explicit geometric reasoning chains. Reward ablations further support the decomposition: removing XY hurts cross-view localization and correspondence; removing Z weakens relative depth and occlusion judgments; removing T degrades camera-motion and temporal reverse-reasoning tasks. The authors also emphasize stronger generalization than pure SFT, arguing that RL encourages strategy learning rather than memorization of static patterns.

From the provided full-text excerpt, there is no reliable complete table of model size, training steps, or dataset scale, so those details should not be overstated. What is clear is that this is an SFT-plus-RL enhancement on top of an existing VLM, with the main contribution in reward design and spatial-temporal data construction rather than large-scale pretraining from scratch.1a:T543,LeanGRPO removes update-stage recomputation through a systems redesign with two pieces. First, it changes the data-parallel layout. Instead of assigning different prompts to different GPUs and generating multiple local samples per prompt, all GPUs process the same prompt while each rank generates a different sample. This reduces the need for each rank to retain multiple computation graphs for the same prompt group.

Second, it introduces two recompute-free schedules. LeanGRPO-Retain simply enables gradient tracking during rollout and keeps the selected-timestep graphs alive until terminal rewards and group advantages are available, then reuses those graphs for backpropagation. It is simple but activation memory grows with the number of selected timesteps.

LeanGRPO-Reweight is more subtle. During rollout, each selected-timestep graph is immediately consumed through a provisional backward pass, producing a provisional gradient and allowing saved activations to be released right away. Once the terminal reward and advantage are known, the provisional gradient is corrected using that advantage, recovering the same policy gradient in exact arithmetic. This shifts the memory burden from retained activations to stored provisional gradients. The paper positions both schedules as objective-preserving rather than approximate shortcuts.1b:T7a7,The pipeline has four components. First is data curation. The authors collect 22,000 problems from 16 regional and international competition families plus online platforms, package them into executable evaluation environments, and explicitly exclude and deduplicate evaluation problems. They then use DeepSeek-V4-Flash to generate large synthetic reasoning-trace corpora: about 1.2M traces for the Nano model and 477,642 for the larger model. The key point is not just scale, but the organization of problems, reasoning, code, and feedback into long-context supervised examples.

Second is supervised fine-tuning. They start from Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B and Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B-A55B and perform long-context SFT to obtain competition-specialized models. Nano is a 30B-parameter MoE with 3B active parameters; Ultra is 550B with 55B active. The supervision appears to target not only final code generation but also intermediate problem-solving traces.

Third is reinforcement learning. RL is applied to Nano-CC, with rewards derived from executable evaluation, namely whether the generated program passes tests and how much score it obtains. This reward is attractive because it is automatic and closely aligned with the task objective. The downside is that it may favor search-friendly behaviors rather than robust one-shot generalization. The paper places RL after SFT, which is sensible: the action space in competitive programming is too large for pure RL to be stable without a strong initial policy.

Fourth is GenCorrect, the test-time closed-loop strategy. It is more than self-consistency. The system generates diverse candidate solutions, evaluates them, uses the feedback to refine later attempts, and operates under a limited submission budget. This is essentially verifier-guided search plus iterative program repair. It is especially well matched to IOI-style settings, where multiple submissions and partial credit are part of the environment.1c:T704,The main results are straightforward. On IOI 2025, Nemotron-3-Nano-CC improves from 130 Score@1 at baseline to 280 after SFT and 291 after RL. So SFT provides the dominant gain, while RL adds a smaller but real improvement. With five rounds of GenCorrect, the score rises to 468, above the gold threshold of 438.3. The size of that jump makes it clear that test-time search is a major contributor, arguably larger than RL in this setup.

Nemotron-3-Ultra-CC does not use code-specific RL. It reaches 304 with SFT alone, and 502.0 after five GenCorrect rounds. This suggests two things: larger base models still benefit from domain-specific SFT on hard coding tasks, and a strong base model plus strong inference-time search may be a more efficient path than a smaller model with additional RL.

The authors then build a competition-specific system for IOI 2026 and evaluate it live, before public release of the problems, under matched time, internet, and submission constraints. The system scores 535.4/600, above the gold threshold of 361.12 and above the top official human score of 498.27. As a research result, this should be interpreted carefully. It reflects a full specialized system, including task-specific adaptation and inference strategy, not just the intrinsic capability of the underlying model.

The most useful ablation insight is the gain decomposition. Synthetic data plus long-context SFT establish the main capability step. RL further aligns the model to executable objectives. GenCorrect then amplifies final benchmark performance when the environment allows iterative feedback. If one only looks at Score@1, the model improvement is much smaller than the final competition score, which is exactly why the distinction between parameter updates and test-time compute matters.1d:T53b,This is a skim paper. Read it for pipeline decomposition, not for competition storytelling. Focus on three parts.

First, inspect the data curation and contamination-control pipeline. For foundation-model researchers, this is more interesting than the IOI headline because it shows how executable synthetic supervision is assembled, filtered, and kept evaluation-clean.

Second, read the tables that separate SFT, RL, and GenCorrect gains. Pay close attention to the gap between Score@1 and final multi-round scores. That gap tells you how much of the benchmark result comes from model updates versus inference-time search.

Third, read the GenCorrect algorithm and examples. It captures a broader post-training pattern: when the environment provides a verifier and actionable feedback, the model does not need to solve the task in one shot. It can iteratively repair its own outputs. That idea transfers beyond coding to math, tool use, and other verifiable domains.

A reasonable reading budget is 20–30 minutes. If you only want the research signal, read the introduction, method overview, main results, and ablations. Keep one framing in mind: this is not a pretraining paper. It is a strong post-training and inference-system paper whose value for pretraining lies in what it reveals about executable supervision and reward quality.1e:T867,The setup is simple and clean. Given a pretrained model of size N and a total annotation budget B, an allocation ratio r sends rB examples to SFT and (1-r)B to RL. Performance is written as P(N, r, B; tau). A standard formulation would optimize r* = argmax_r P, but the paper argues that this is the wrong target because post-training curves are often flat near the top. A tiny gain in measured score may not justify a full retraining run, and the exact argmax can be unstable under noise. The paper therefore defines the epsilon-near-optimal region: the set of ratios whose performance is at least (1-epsilon) times the best observed performance.

This shift enables three analyses. First, region characterization: the authors sweep allocation ratios across tasks, model families, and RL methods, then measure how wide the near-optimal region is under tolerances such as 2% to 10%. Second, scale transfer: instead of asking whether the exact optimum ratio matches across scales, they ask whether the near-optimal region found on a small proxy model covers high-performing ratios on a larger model. This is a much more stable transfer criterion. Third, asymmetric annotation cost: the default setup measures budget in number of labeled examples, but the paper extends the framework to cases where SFT and RL labels have different unit costs. Under that view, the effective optimum shifts, and the acceptable region can widen further when SFT data is relatively more expensive.

Relative to prior work, the novelty is in the problem formulation and the transfer criterion. Earlier work mostly reported broad trends, such as SFT being more important in low-data regimes and preference optimization becoming more useful at larger scales, but did not provide a principled near-optimal allocation framework or establish transfer from small to large models. Work on data mixture optimization and scaling-law extrapolation usually focuses on single-stage training, especially pretraining mixtures, not sequential SFT-then-RL coupling. Larger models are therefore more forgiving to imperfect SFT/RL splits. Third, transfer works better at the region level than at the exact-optimum level: a near-optimal region identified on a small proxy model tends to reliably contain high-performing allocations for a larger target model, even when the exact best ratio shifts. 再看奖励构造。论文的关键不是直接给 assert 打启发式分，而是从 group rollouts 里估计事实的后验效用。具体做法是先把语义等价的事实聚成 cluster，避免同一证据因表述不同被分散统计；然后基于一组 rollout 的最终 outcome，用 Bayesian estimation 推断每个 fact cluster 与成功结果的统计关联。直观上，如果某类事实经常出现在成功轨迹里、较少出现在失败轨迹里，它的 posterior utility 就更高。随后，这个 fact-level utility 被映射回 step-level dense rewards，用来训练 policy。

On the agent side, the paper departs from standard ReAct-style agents that keep only raw text history. The state explicitly includes a fact store. After a search action returns an observation, the agent can take an assert action that extracts structured facts from the observation and writes them into the store. The state is therefore represented as the question, the current observation, and the accumulated fact set. These facts are treated as discrete semantic evidence units rather than plain text snippets. This is important because the method assigns credit at the level of evidence semantics, not at the level of surface-form actions.

On the reward side, the paper estimates fact utility from grouped rollouts. Semantically equivalent facts are first clustered so that the same evidence is not split across multiple textual variants. Then, using final outcomes from a group of trajectories, the method performs Bayesian estimation of the posterior utility of each fact cluster. Intuitively, if a fact cluster appears disproportionately often in successful trajectories, its posterior utility increases. These fact-level utilities are then converted into dense step-level rewards for policy optimization.

This differs from standard outcome-based GRPO, where all steps in a trajectory inherit the same coarse trajectory-level signal. Here, group rollouts are still used, but the reward is redistributed through an explicit evidence layer. That makes the method better matched to search tasks, where the meaningful intermediate unit is often not a token or a reasoning sentence, but a retrieved and validated piece of evidence.

The design also avoids training an external reward model and does not require human annotation of intermediate steps. The trade-off is that the whole pipeline depends on the quality of fact extraction and fact clustering. If those are noisy, the posterior utility estimates can be corrupted.
