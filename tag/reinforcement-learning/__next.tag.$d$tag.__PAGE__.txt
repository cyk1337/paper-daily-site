The main novelty is the training recipe rather than the architecture. Stage II, Hard Exploration (HE), is the key step. Reinforcement learning is applied only to queries on which the uncompressed RAG fails. This is a targeted exploration strategy. Instead of spending RL budget on the full data distribution, including easy cases already handled well by imitation, the method concentrates optimization on the subset where imitation is provably insufficient. The intended effect is not just error correction. It is to encourage the compressed model to discover computation patterns better suited to dense compressed inputs, and therefore capable of surpassing the token-based teacher.

Methodologically, this is a selective optimization recipe: use high-confidence supervision to enter a good basin, then use exploration only on high-value hard cases. First, it changes the data-parallel layout. Instead of assigning different prompts to different GPUs and generating multiple local samples per prompt, all GPUs process the same prompt while each rank generates a different sample. This reduces the need for each rank to retain multiple computation graphs for the same prompt group.

Second, it introduces two recompute-free schedules. LeanGRPO-Retain simply enables gradient tracking during rollout and keeps the selected-timestep graphs alive until terminal rewards and group advantages are available, then reuses those graphs for backpropagation. It is simple but activation memory grows with the number of selected timesteps.

LeanGRPO-Reweight is more subtle. During rollout, each selected-timestep graph is immediately consumed through a provisional backward pass, producing a provisional gradient and allowing saved activations to be released right away. Once the terminal reward and advantage are known, the provisional gradient is corrected using that advantage, recovering the same policy gradient in exact arithmetic. This shifts the memory burden from retained activations to stored provisional gradients. It is a strong post-training and inference-system paper whose value for pretraining lies in what it reveals about executable supervision and reward quality.14:T867,The setup is simple and clean. Given a pretrained model of size N and a total annotation budget B, an allocation ratio r sends rB examples to SFT and (1-r)B to RL. Performance is written as P(N, r, B; tau). A standard formulation would optimize r* = argmax_r P, but the paper argues that this is the wrong target because post-training curves are often flat near the top. A tiny gain in measured score may not justify a full retraining run, and the exact argmax can be unstable under noise. The paper therefore defines the epsilon-near-optimal region: the set of ratios whose performance is at least (1-epsilon) times the best observed performance.

This shift enables three analyses. First, region characterization: the authors sweep allocation ratios across tasks, model families, and RL methods, then measure how wide the near-optimal region is under tolerances such as 2% to 10%. Second, scale transfer: instead of asking whether the exact optimum ratio matches across scales, they ask whether the near-optimal region found on a small proxy model covers high-performing ratios on a larger model. This is a much more stable transfer criterion. Third, asymmetric annotation cost: the default setup measures budget in number of labeled examples, but the paper extends the framework to cases where SFT and RL labels have different unit costs. Under that view, the effective optimum shifts, and the acceptable region can widen further when SFT data is relatively more expensive.

Relative to prior work, the novelty is in the problem formulation and the transfer criterion. Earlier work mostly reported broad trends, such as SFT being more important in low-data regimes and preference optimization becoming more useful at larger scales, but did not provide a principled near-optimal allocation framework or establish transfer from small to large models. Work on data mixture optimization and scaling-law extrapolation usually focuses on single-stage training, especially pretraining mixtures, not sequential SFT-then-RL coupling. The paper’s contribution is to turn a brittle tuning problem into a region-finding problem with practical transfer value.15:T6f4,The most important empirical findings are structural rather than benchmark-centric. First, the performance-versus-allocation curve typically has a broad plateau near its maximum. Even with small tolerances, such as a few percent from peak performance, the near-optimal region is already wide. Second, this region generally widens with model scale. Larger models are therefore more forgiving to imperfect SFT/RL splits. Third, transfer works better at the region level than at the exact-optimum level: a near-optimal region identified on a small proxy model tends to reliably contain high-performing allocations for a larger target model, even when the exact best ratio shifts. Fourth, the pattern is observed across tasks, model families, and both off-policy and on-policy RL settings, which argues against the effect being tied to one specific algorithm.

The paper also includes a useful cost sanity check. Compared with human annotation costs around 0.5 to 1.0 USD per example, compute is lower by roughly three to five orders of magnitude. Even compared with cheap synthetic annotation around 1e-3 USD per example, compute is still usually below annotation. This supports the paper’s decision to treat annotation as the dominant first-order budget axis.

On the agent side, the paper departs from standard ReAct-style agents that keep only raw text history. The state explicitly includes a fact store. After a search action returns an observation, the agent can take an assert action that extracts structured facts from the observation and writes them into the store. The state is therefore represented as the question, the current observation, and the accumulated fact set. These facts are treated as discrete semantic evidence units rather than plain text snippets. This is important because the method assigns credit at the level of evidence semantics, not at the level of surface-form actions.

On the reward side, the paper estimates fact utility from grouped rollouts. Semantically equivalent facts are first clustered so that the same evidence is not split across multiple textual variants. Then, using final outcomes from a group of trajectories, the method performs Bayesian estimation of the posterior utility of each fact cluster. Intuitively, if a fact cluster appears disproportionately often in successful trajectories, its posterior utility increases. These fact-level utilities are then converted into dense step-level rewards for policy optimization.

This differs from standard outcome-based GRPO, where all steps in a trajectory inherit the same coarse trajectory-level signal. Here, group rollouts are still used, but the reward is redistributed through an explicit evidence layer. That makes the method better matched to search tasks, where the meaningful intermediate unit is often not a token or a reasoning sentence, but a retrieved and validated piece of evidence.

The design also avoids training an external reward model and does not require human annotation of intermediate steps. The trade-off is that the whole pipeline depends on the quality of fact extraction and fact clustering. If those are noisy, the posterior utility estimates can be corrupted.
