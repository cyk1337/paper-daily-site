9:T4ec,Cord is “confidence-anchored residual decoding.” Instead of replacing confidence decoding with a full token-level planner, it keeps the base confidence ranking (top-2 margin) and learns bounded geometric corrections:
- Residual families: (i) generation direction bias (left-to-right, right-to-left, etc.), (ii) proximity-to-revealed-context (local continuation vs non-local seeding), and (iii) two-sided infilling preference (positions bracketed by revealed context).
- A lightweight prompt-conditioned network outputs a low-dimensional coefficient matrix; state features of the partially revealed response modulate how residuals apply over time, enabling within-rollout phase changes (seed anchors early, fill locally later).
- Training avoids step-wise discrete actions. For each prompt, Cord samples a single coefficient matrix that deterministically induces the entire state-dependent reveal trajectory, then receives a sequence-level reward. This reframes learning as a contextual bandit in a low-dimensional continuous space, sidestepping temporal credit assignment and reducing variance.
- The dLLM backbone stays frozen; only ~0.53M policy parameters are trained. When coefficients are zero, Cord exactly reduces to the original confidence decoder.a:T435,Reinforced Adversarial Learning (RAL) is a two-player loop:
- Judge: given (x, y), learns to discriminate whether y comes from the reference set or from the current generator’s on-policy samples, producing a “probability of being reference” score.
- Generator: optimized with RL (implemented on top of Prime-RL) to maximize the judge score.
No verifiers, no rubrics, no chain-of-thought traces—only outcome-level reference answers.
The central hypothesis is an implicit curriculum: once the generator matches superficial cues (length/format/lexical style), those cues stop separating the distributions, forcing the judge to rely on deeper task-relevant structure; stronger judges and longer co-training should therefore yield better rewards and better policies.
Key contrasts:
- vs RLVR: reward is learned from examples, not computed by a checker.
- vs frozen LLM judges: the judge is trained jointly, with a moving decision boundary.
- vs demonstration distillation / trace-based adversarial training: supervision is reference answers only, not reasoning trajectories.b:T476,Evaluated in five settings:
1) Three “verifiable but verifier-withheld” tasks: integer multiplication, code-based arithmetic-expression synthesis, and SMILES-to-formula translation. RAL with judge-only reward improves held-out correctness and in some settings matches or exceeds training with exact rewards (including variants that augment exact rewards with a generator-sized judge).
2) Two open-ended scientific generation tasks: biomedical abstract generation from in-domain references, and NIH grant abstract generation. RAL shifts outputs toward the target reference distribution without explicitly labeling the target domain, while maintaining semantic diversity; on NIH abstracts, outputs become harder to distinguish from real references for both LLM judges and human evaluators.
Ablations emphasize: judge capacity matters; longer co-training continues to improve the generator, consistent with the curriculum narrative.

The first is CoC-CFG. The model runs the frozen action expert twice: once with the full context C_full, and once with a reference context C_ref where only the reasoning span is removed. At Euler step k, it computes two velocities, v_with and v_without, and interpolates along the line between them with a guidance weight w_k. Setting w_k=1 recovers the original policy, w_k=0 removes the reasoning influence, and w_k>1 amplifies it. This turns trust in reasoning from a binary keep/drop decision into a controllable scalar.

The second part is Q-adaptive guidance. The choice of w_k at each step is formulated as an MDP over a finite action set of guidance weights. The key move is to avoid standard bootstrapped RL. Because the expert is frozen, the initial noise is fixed, and the generation process can be reset to any step, the value of taking one candidate weight at one step can be measured exactly: branch for one step with that weight, then finish the rollout under the current controller, and use the final task score as the action value. Small critics are trained to regress these measured Q-values, with an added penalty for deviating from a reference weight. The controller is then improved, new values are re-measured under the improved controller, and the critics are refit on the accumulated measurements.

Relative to prior work, the differences are clear. It does not try to detect hallucinated reasoning. It does not fine-tune the VLA or update the expert online. And it does not rely on SAC-style or GRPO-style bootstrapping from sparse terminal rewards when exact one-step branching is available.d:T599,The evaluation has two main parts: Alpasim and Waymo. In Alpasim, a simulator reconstructed from real driving logs, Q-adaptive guidance reduces open-loop displacement cost by 16.5% relative to the original w=1 policy. In closed-loop evaluation, it passes 262 out of 300 scenes, compared with 252 for the baseline. Under the same reward signal, it also outperforms SAC and GRPO, which supports the paper’s claim that measured-Q fitting is better matched to this setting than generic online RL.

On the Waymo end-to-end driving benchmark, the paper compares against fixed guidance weights, four adaptive-guidance baselines, SAC, GRPO, and the original Alpamayo 1.5. The reported result is that the first three stages of the proposed controller already beat all fixed-weight settings and the adaptive/RL baselines on held-out frames. After rule-based conversion to the Waymo vehicle, Alpamayo 1.5 with Q-adaptive guidance reaches 8.135159 RFS on the hidden test set, above the published 8.079 baseline.
