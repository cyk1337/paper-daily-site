18:T410,1) Dataset design: 500 handwritten document images spanning three structures (free text, tables, formulas), four language settings (mostly Simplified Chinese plus English, Traditional Chinese, and mixed CN-EN), and nine real-world scenarios. It also provides per-sample difficulty annotations along six axes (e.g., degradation, cursiveness, structural complexity).
2) Structured ground truth: Markdown for free text, LaTeX for formulas, and HTML tables for tabular content—pushing evaluation from token/character correctness toward layout/structure fidelity.
3) PDE (Prior-Driven Error): an error attribution metric intended to quantify whether a model’s mistake is better explained by language priors than by the visual evidence, capturing confident but unsupported “plausible” transcriptions.
4) Evaluation protocol: 18 contemporary MLLMs / OCR-oriented VLMs are tested under a unified setup, alongside calibrated human baselines, enabling qualitative comparison of error types (humans being conservative vs models hallucinating).19:T7f9,The main idea is to replace generic richness proxies with per-sample checklists. A rubric is not a reference answer. It is a structured list of required and optional key points for a given question, plus a one-sentence completeness summary. The model’s richness is scored by rubric coverage, while grounding is scored with a sentence-level support signal. The paper uses “grounding” broadly to cover both faithfulness to provided context and factual support from reliable world knowledge.

The study compares five reward variants built from three ingredients: grounding, rubric coverage, and pairwise relevance. The important result is that the composition matters as much as the ingredients. Pure grounding rewards improve support but push the model toward shorter, safer answers. Pure rubric rewards preserve coverage but weaken support. Multiplicative gating inherits the conservatism of grounding-heavy objectives. Simple additive combinations help but only partially. The best balance in their experiments comes from a soft three-term reward: a non-gating grounding term plus rubric coverage plus pairwise relevance.

Rubric construction is also part of the contribution. Instead of relying on a single rubric writer, the authors use a multi-generator union pipeline: GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3-Flash-Preview independently draft candidate rubrics, and GPT-OSS-120B merges and deduplicates them into a final union rubric. The goal is to reduce omissions from any single generator while keeping the rubric compact enough for practical judging. The RL training pool contains 4k prompts, split evenly between grounded prompts with context and open prompts from WildChat, so the model does not learn a purely reference-grounded, refusal-heavy policy.

Methodologically, this is less about a new RL algorithm and more about reward specification. The paper cleanly separates two objectives that are often conflated in long-form generation: whether each sentence is supported, and whether the answer covers the information the question demands.1a:T60b,The experiments use two open-weight model families: Qwen3-4B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B. The comparison is controlled carefully: same base checkpoint, same prompt pool, same rollout setup, same evaluation splits, same judges; only the reward changes.

Training uses 4,000 RL prompts: 2,000 grounded prompts with context from a reliability-oriented pool, and 2,000 open prompts from WildChat. Evaluation covers FACTS Grounding, LongFact, FActScore closed-book and open-book, CL-bench-style checklist tasks, and out-of-distribution settings such as creative writing and pairwise preference.

The main findings are consistent. First, strict grounding-only rewards quickly shorten responses within a small number of RL steps, showing that the model discovers refusal or under-answering as the safest way to avoid unsupported claims. Second, rubric-only rewards preserve coverage much better, especially on checklist completion and open-ended writing, but grounding degrades. Third, hard fact-rubric gating remains weak out of distribution because it preserves the same conservative bias. Fourth, the soft three-component reward gives the best trade-off between in-distribution grounding and out-of-distribution completion / preference behavior.

The important experimental lesson is structural: the shape of the reward determines the shape of the learned policy, and all-or-nothing grounding terms naturally favor saying less.1b:T4a0,Start with the introduction, especially the refusal-to-richness trade-off. The key is not a single best score, but the directional pattern across in-distribution grounding and out-of-distribution checklist / creative-writing behavior.

The most interesting follow-up questions are whether the reward overfits to rubric-writer preferences, how stable the multi-generator union pipeline is, and whether the same reward design transfers to reasoning RL, RAG answer generation, or continual post-training. For readers interested in the pretrain interface, the practical lesson is simple: if the training signal only punishes errors and does not reward completeness, the model will shrink its answer support by default.1c:T4ef,The method is deliberately lightweight and training-free. Instead of training a separate verifier, it reads how much each generated token attends back to visual tokens and uses that as a proxy for visual reference usage. The scoring has two levels. First, token-level calibration: each token is scored using both its generation likelihood and its visual lookback strength. This matters because plain likelihood over-rewards generic fluent tokens, while many visually decisive words are object, attribute, or relation tokens. Second, response-level aggregation: token scores are not simply averaged. They are aggregated under a visual relevance distribution that gives more weight to tokens with stronger visual reference usage. This separation is one of the paper’s better design choices. It distinguishes whether a token is visually supported from how much that token should matter for the final response score. Compared with prior approaches, LookBack avoids extra reward models, preference labels, and additional verification passes. Compared with pure confidence scoring, it explicitly injects image sensitivity. Compared with vision-only heuristics, it still preserves the model’s own uncertainty signal rather than replacing it with raw attention alone.1d:T702,The method has two layers. The first is the diagnostic metric, Contextual Preference Gain (CPG). For the same response pair, the model is evaluated with and without additional helpful context. CPG measures how much the preference margin increases once that context is added. The margin follows the usual DPO or Bradley-Terry view, based on the relative reward or log-probability gap between preferred and dispreferred responses. If a model truly leverages context, the correct response should become more strongly preferred when grounding information is available, so CPG should be positive and meaningfully large. Empirically, the authors show that higher CPG is strongly associated with lower hallucination, while standard DPO and several variants produce CPG distributions concentrated near zero.

The second layer is C²-DPO. On top of the original preference objective, the authors add a calibration term that explicitly rewards the preference margin under enriched context being larger than the margin without that context. This is the key difference from vanilla DPO. The change is not about a new reward parameterization; it is about writing context sensitivity directly into the optimization target. The same idea is also attached to SimPO and RDPO, which suggests that the proposal is a fairly general calibration add-on rather than a method tied to one specific DPO implementation.

Mechanistically, the method is well matched to settings where the preferred answer is only clearly preferred once grounding evidence is present. If chosen and rejected responses are already easy to separate without extra context, the gain may be limited. But for visual grounding, factual evidence, and other evidence-conditioned preference tasks, the objective is well aligned with the failure mode.1e:T5aa,The experimental story is straightforward. First, the paper validates CPG as a diagnostic: models with higher CPG consistently show lower hallucination rates. That gives the proposed optimization direction a credible empirical basis. Second, C²-DPO reduces hallucination on multimodal benchmarks by a clear margin. The headline numbers reported in the paper are a 36% reduction in response-level hallucination rate and a 60% reduction in mention-level hallucination rate on Object HalBench, with consistent gains on HallusionBench as well. Third, the paper checks whether this comes at the cost of general multimodal ability and reports that performance on ScienceQA, MM-Vet, and TextVQA is preserved.

Two additional results are worth noting. The calibration idea transfers to SimPO and RDPO, which supports the claim that the issue is objective-level rather than implementation-specific. The authors also test a text-only setting and find improved factual alignment there too, suggesting that context blindness is a broader weakness of DPO-style training, not only a multimodal problem.
