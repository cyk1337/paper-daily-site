5:T4ef,The method is deliberately lightweight and training-free. Instead of training a separate verifier, it reads how much each generated token attends back to visual tokens and uses that as a proxy for visual reference usage. The scoring has two levels. First, token-level calibration: each token is scored using both its generation likelihood and its visual lookback strength. This matters because plain likelihood over-rewards generic fluent tokens, while many visually decisive words are object, attribute, or relation tokens. Second, response-level aggregation: token scores are not simply averaged. They are aggregated under a visual relevance distribution that gives more weight to tokens with stronger visual reference usage. This separation is one of the paper’s better design choices. It distinguishes whether a token is visually supported from how much that token should matter for the final response score. Compared with prior approaches, LookBack avoids extra reward models, preference labels, and additional verification passes. Compared with pure confidence scoring, it explicitly injects image sensitivity. Compared with vision-only heuristics, it still preserves the model’s own uncertainty signal rather than replacing it with raw attention alone.6:T702,The method has two layers. The first is the diagnostic metric, Contextual Preference Gain (CPG). For the same response pair, the model is evaluated with and without additional helpful context. CPG measures how much the preference margin increases once that context is added. The margin follows the usual DPO or Bradley-Terry view, based on the relative reward or log-probability gap between preferred and dispreferred responses. If a model truly leverages context, the correct response should become more strongly preferred when grounding information is available, so CPG should be positive and meaningfully large. Empirically, the authors show that higher CPG is strongly associated with lower hallucination, while standard DPO and several variants produce CPG distributions concentrated near zero.

The second layer is C²-DPO. On top of the original preference objective, the authors add a calibration term that explicitly rewards the preference margin under enriched context being larger than the margin without that context. This is the key difference from vanilla DPO. The change is not about a new reward parameterization; it is about writing context sensitivity directly into the optimization target. The same idea is also attached to SimPO and RDPO, which suggests that the proposal is a fairly general calibration add-on rather than a method tied to one specific DPO implementation.

Mechanistically, the method is well matched to settings where the preferred answer is only clearly preferred once grounding evidence is present. If chosen and rejected responses are already easy to separate without extra context, the gain may be limited. But for visual grounding, factual evidence, and other evidence-conditioned preference tasks, the objective is well aligned with the failure mode.7:T5aa,The experimental story is straightforward. First, the paper validates CPG as a diagnostic: models with higher CPG consistently show lower hallucination rates. That gives the proposed optimization direction a credible empirical basis. Second, C²-DPO reduces hallucination on multimodal benchmarks by a clear margin. The headline numbers reported in the paper are a 36% reduction in response-level hallucination rate and a 60% reduction in mention-level hallucination rate on Object HalBench, with consistent gains on HallusionBench as well. Third, the paper checks whether this comes at the cost of general multimodal ability and reports that performance on ScienceQA, MM-Vet, and TextVQA is preserved.

Two additional results are worth noting. The calibration idea transfers to SimPO and RDPO, which supports the claim that the issue is objective-level rather than implementation-specific. The authors also test a text-only setting and find improved factual alignment there too, suggesting that context blindness is a broader weakness of DPO-style training, not only a multimodal problem.

Compared with POPE or HallusionBench, the main difference is the combination of fine-grained taxonomy, dynamic multimodal mutation, and structured verification.9:T86b,The paper has two technical components: a controlled perturbation benchmark and an evaluation protocol designed to separate visual reading from language-driven rewriting. The authors sample raw text from mC4 in English, Chinese, and Korean, apply character-level perturbations directly in text space, and then render the perturbed text into single-page document images. This is a strong design choice because the ground truth becomes the perturbed text itself, making it possible to tell whether a model transcribes what is present or silently corrects it.

They use three perturbation families. Scramble preserves the first and last character while shuffling the middle, probing reliance on lexical priors. Random substitution replaces characters with random same-script alternatives, creating severe corruption. Visual substitution replaces characters with visually similar ones, probing the competition between visual evidence and language prior. English and Korean are perturbed at the word level; Chinese uses non-overlapping 4-character windows. The perturbation rate is controlled, with clean documents retained as a baseline.

The evaluation spans three system classes: general-purpose VLMs, OCR-specialized VLMs, and traditional OCR pipelines. This taxonomy is central to the paper’s argument because it roughly orders systems by the strength of their language prior. General VLMs have broad multimodal and text pretraining and are most likely to hallucinate corrections. OCR-specialized VLMs are constrained by document extraction fine-tuning. Traditional OCR systems operate much closer to character recognition and have little generative prior. The authors also avoid adding anti-correction instructions to the prompt, so the model’s default behavior is exposed rather than suppressed.

Beyond aggregate error rates, the paper probes Qwen3-VL-4B layer by layer. The mechanistic claim is that rewriting tends to occur when the internal representation of a perturbed word remains close to the representation of the original clean word. They also report a word-length effect: short words are much more likely to be rewritten than longer ones.a:T606,The benchmark contains 1,455 single-page documents across English, Chinese, and Korean, and the evaluation covers 15 systems. The main result is clean and consistent: general-purpose VLMs degrade the most under perturbation, with up to 4.5 WER points on English; OCR-specialized VLMs degrade by about 0.2 to 2 points; traditional OCR pipelines degrade by less than 0.6 points. This strongly supports the paper’s central claim that stronger language priors increase the risk of sacrificing faithfulness on imperfect text.

A particularly important finding is the non-local effect. Corrupting only around 5% of words can increase errors on the untouched remainder by 5–10x relative to the clean baseline for general VLMs. That means the issue is not just local character confusion. The model’s page-level generation process is being pulled toward a different textual interpretation, which is much more concerning for unified document models.

The multilingual results are also informative. The same qualitative pattern holds across English, Chinese, and Korean, but the most damaging perturbation type differs by script. This suggests that rewriting is shaped both by language priors and by script-specific visual and tokenization structure. The probing analysis on Qwen3-VL-4B further shows that rewriting is more likely when the perturbed token remains representationally close to the original word.
