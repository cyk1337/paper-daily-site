9:T83a,The method, Side-by-Side (SxS) Interleaved Generation, keeps the setup deliberately simple. It uses a standard autoregressive model, a single context, and the same hidden-state evolution. The only addition is lightweight tagging that distinguishes two action types inside one token stream: think tokens and speak tokens. Think tokens update the context but are not disclosed to the user. Speak tokens are both part of the context and an early-released prefix of the final answer. The key constraint is not merely to speak early, but to speak only when the disclosed prefix is supported by the reasoning generated so far.

Training has two stages. The first is entailment-aligned interleaved supervision. Starting from ordinary triples (x, r, a), where r is reasoning and a is the final answer, the paper aligns answer prefixes with reasoning prefixes so that each early disclosure is justified by the available reasoning prefix. This is the central mechanism that avoids two degenerate solutions: unsupported early commitments and low-information filler text that improves superficial latency metrics without helping the user.

The second stage is SFT followed by RL. SFT teaches the model the semantics of the dual-action format and the pacing structure. But the paper argues that SFT alone is insufficient because the interleaved format changes the rollout distribution: the model may learn the format while losing some reasoning quality under the new commitment constraints. RL is then used to recover task performance and optimize the trade-off between correctness and content latency. In other words, RL is not just a score booster here; it helps the model re-adapt its reasoning policy under the new disclosure regime.

Relative to prior work, SxS differs from template-based interleaving because disclosure timing is learned rather than fixed. It differs from dual-channel or latent-reasoning systems because there is no separate hidden channel or second decoder. It also differs from system-level latency optimization because the target is supported content timing, not just earlier token emission.a:T67f,The evaluation spans two Qwen3 architectures, one MoE and one dense, at two scales including 30B-A3B and 4B. The benchmarks are AIME25 for in-domain mathematical reasoning and GPQA-Diamond for out-of-domain scientific QA. Importantly, the paper does not evaluate only final accuracy. It also reports token-level content-latency proxies, such as when the first supported visible progress appears and how long users wait between updates. That matches the problem formulation much better than plain TTFT.

The main empirical claim is consistent across architectures, scales, and domains: SxS improves the accuracy-latency trade-off without changing the model architecture. At a similar final accuracy, the model can disclose useful supported progress earlier; at a similar content latency, it can achieve better final task performance. The evidence is presented as a Pareto improvement rather than a single scalar gain.

Two ablation-level findings matter most. First, entailment-aligned supervision is necessary. If the objective naively rewards earlier output, the model tends to produce filler or unstable early commitments. Second, RL is necessary. SFT alone teaches the interleaving format but hurts reasoning quality, which confirms that the new format induces a nontrivial distribution shift. RL restores reasoning performance while preserving better pacing behavior.
