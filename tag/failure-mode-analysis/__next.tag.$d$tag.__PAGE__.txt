b:T880,Revelio shifts the search space from continuous pixels to discrete semantics. Images are abstracted as scene graphs with entities, relations, and attributes. Domain concepts are defined as subgraph patterns, for example a pedestrian in front of the ego vehicle at close range, or a barrier co-occurring with rainy weather. These concepts are not used merely as annotations. They are compiled into scenario distributions through a scene specification layer, using Scenic and a renderer such as CARLA. As a result, the unit of evaluation is not a single rendered image but a family of physically plausible scenes satisfying the same semantic constraints.

The search problem is formulated as black-box constrained combinatorial optimization. For any candidate concept set, the framework samples scenarios from the corresponding distribution, queries the VLM with a task prompt, and estimates a failure rate. The objective is to discover multiple high-risk concept combinations under a limited query budget. To do that, the paper introduces two search procedures. The first is a diversity-aware beam search. It expands concept combinations greedily, but the scoring is not based on failure rate alone; it also encourages diversity so that the beam does not collapse onto near-duplicate modes. This makes it useful for rapidly mapping the failure landscape. The second is a Gaussian-process global search with Thompson sampling. The GP models uncertainty over untested concept combinations, and Thompson sampling balances exploitation of known risky regions with exploration of uncertain but promising ones. Physical compatibility constraints are enforced throughout, so the search does not waste budget on impossible scene combinations.

A notable design choice is that interpretability is built into the search object itself. The method does not first find arbitrary failures and then try to explain them post hoc. It searches directly over human-readable semantic factors. Another important point is that the framework is black-box by construction: it does not rely on gradients, hidden states, or token-level likelihoods, which makes it usable for frontier closed-source VLMs.c:T738,The experiments cover at least two safety-relevant domains: autonomous driving and indoor robotics. Qualitatively, the paper surfaces several concrete failure patterns. In driving scenarios, VLMs show brittle spatial grounding, including incorrect stop-versus-continue decisions around nearby pedestrians. They also miss hazardous elements such as barriers even when those elements should be central to the decision. In indoor robotics, the dominant pattern is different: the models can become excessively cautious, hurting task performance, while still failing to properly account for sharp or dangerous objects. That distinction matters because it shows the framework is not only finding reckless behavior; it also captures maladaptive over-caution.

The main quantitative result is that Revelio discovers on average 3–5 times more failure modes than unguided random search under the same budget. That is the key evidence that the search procedures exploit semantic structure rather than merely sampling more. The paper also positions the two search strategies differently: diversity-aware beam search is better for quickly surfacing a broad set of high-risk modes, while GP-based Thompson sampling is more useful for global exploration as the budget grows. Another important experimental choice is that failures are measured over scenario distributions, not single examples, which makes the discovered modes more stable than one-off adversarial cases.

The abstract does not expose all low-level numbers such as exact model names, sample counts per concept set, or absolute failure rates on each benchmark. Those details need to be checked in the full tables and appendix.
