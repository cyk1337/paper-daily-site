19:T471,ClosureBench is built around “logic primitives + executable semantics + controlled instance generation.”
- Task space: 26 categories derived from explicit graph-relational primitives such as reachability/transitive closure, degree, set operations, connectivity (e.g., SCC-related queries), and aggregation over reachable sets.
- Exact labels: each query is implemented as a short program whose execution yields the reference answer. The paper mentions implementations in Ein (tensor-contraction style graph ops) and an equivalent Python/NetworkX version.
- Constructive generator: instances are produced on demand with separate structural and surface seeds, allowing independent control of graph structure and linguistic realization.
- Difficulty control: three orthogonal axes—number of nodes, edge density, and query depth/compositional level—so evaluators can hold two fixed and sweep the third to localize failure modes.
- Interface: models only see natural-language questions and must output JSON; the underlying program is hidden, keeping the benchmark focused on reasoning rather than program induction from code exposure.1a:T447,The benchmark partitions items into frozen zones. Zones A–C are answer-expected real-public questions, sourced from CommonsenseQA, TriviaQA, MedMCQA, HotpotQA, and SciQ, with different levels of answer popularity and boundary difficulty. Zone D is abstention-expected and built from style-matched synthetic unknowns. Each item includes a question, gold answer set, grading rule, zone label, abstention expectation, origin, contamination-risk tag, provenance, and review notes. Evaluation uses a locked answer-or-abstain prompt, answer-only controls, and prompt-template variants. Outputs are parsed with an official strict parser and a normalized robustness parser. The primary metric, reliability, gives credit for correct answers on answer-expected items and correct abstention on unknowns. Auxiliary reports include answered accuracy, abstention rate, productive abstention on Zone D, refusal rate, expected calibration error on answered items, and boundary sharpness. The important design choice is to avoid rewarding generic non-answer behavior as if it were calibrated epistemic caution.1b:T69a,The paper contributes mainly through formalization and argumentation. First, it defines contamination resistance at an abstract level: given a transformation phi applied to a plaintext dataset D, the transformed dataset phi(D) should preserve inference utility while being unlearnable as training data for a model class. In other words, the same transformed artifact should support valid task performance at evaluation time but fail to provide a useful optimization signal during pretraining. Second, the feasibility argument is tied to Transformer training–inference asymmetry. During training, teacher forcing exposes the full prefix and optimizes next-token likelihood locally at each position. During inference, generation proceeds autoregressively and may rely on higher-level structure, external rules, or reversible mappings. The authors suggest that one could design representations that disrupt local token prediction and gradient alignment during training, while still allowing task information to be recovered at inference time. Third, the paper discusses interoperability: contamination resistance should not be a brittle property that only holds against one architecture or one training recipe. The transformation must remain resistant across a family of models and pipelines, otherwise the benchmark simply becomes model-specific obfuscation.

A key caveat is that the paper does not present a concrete, mature construction for text CRDs. It also does not provide a new training algorithm. The novelty lies in the design principle itself—support inference, resist training—and in connecting that principle to the asymmetry induced by teacher-forced Transformer training.1c:T477,This is a skim paper. Focus on the Introduction and the section on contamination resistance formalization. The Introduction explains why decontamination, privatization, and dynamic benchmarks each have practical limits. The formalization section contains the actual contribution: benchmark design should target simultaneous inference utility and training unlearnability. If reading time is limited, keep three points in mind. First, the paper is not advocating private benchmarks as the main answer. Second, the central mechanism is the asymmetry between teacher-forced training and inference. Third, there is no finished construction yet, so it should be read as a research agenda rather than a deployable method.

Two parts are worth extra attention. One is the discussion of why text is harder than images for unlearnable data: discreteness, semantic-preserving rewrites, and LLM denoising all weaken perturbation-based defenses. The other is interoperability, which identifies the real difficulty: a benchmark is not truly contamination-resistant if the transformation only fools one model family.
