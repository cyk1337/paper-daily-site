9:T71e,The paper has three methodological parts.

First, a controlled measurement study. The authors benchmark nine production tokenizers and evaluate five languages that have standardized Cyrillic and Latin representations: Ukrainian, Serbian, Kazakh, Azerbaijani, and Moldovan, totaling 8.37 million word forms. They use corpus-level fertility and define Ukrainian overhead relative to English as R(t)=F(t,BrUK)/F(t,Brown). The cross-script setup is important: comparing the same language in Cyrillic versus Latin helps isolate tokenizer-script mismatch from semantic or corpus-content differences.

Second, tokenizer retraining rather than post-hoc vocabulary patching. The paper argues that modern tokenizers already contain Cyrillic characters, so the bottleneck is not character absence but insufficient higher-order merges. To test this directly, they train balanced byte-level BPE tokenizers with different English-Ukrainian allocations under a fixed 200K vocabulary budget. The mechanism is straightforward: if Ukrainian appears often enough during tokenizer training, BPE can learn longer and more reusable Cyrillic subwords, reducing fragmentation. The best balanced tokenizer converges at 158,184 actual entries, which also suggests that effective allocation matters more than simply pushing vocabulary size upward.

Third, inference-time mitigation via prompt compression. They evaluate LLMLingua-2 because its XLM-R backbone is multilingual and can be applied to Ukrainian without language-specific fine-tuning. This is framed correctly as a mitigation, not a root-cause fix. Second, the cross-linguistic script comparison. For the five languages with standardized Cyrillic and Latin forms, the overhead tracks the script that is dominant in web usage and, by implication, in tokenizer training data. The model has two coupled sets of variables: token-selection variables indicating which candidate substrings enter the vocabulary, and segmentation-choice variables indicating which candidate segmentation is chosen for each pretoken. The objective minimizes frequency-weighted total token count.

The technical core is the greedy-consistency constraint. If a pretoken is assigned a particular segmentation, then at every segment start there must not exist a longer selected token that also matches the bytes at that position; otherwise GL2R would emit the longer token and the chosen segmentation would not be realizable. This is the mechanism that ties segmentation feasibility to vocabulary content.

A second important piece is scalability. A pretoken of length n has 2^(n-1) possible segmentations, so full enumeration is impossible. JOLT therefore works with a restricted candidate set and adds segmentations on demand, based on the optimization state, instead of instantiating the full combinatorial space. The corpus is first split into pretokens with a regex and aggregated by count; optimization is then run on the top-N most frequent pretokens. The LP solution is rounded to a fixed-size vocabulary, and the paper argues that rounding is not the main source of suboptimality because the rounded solutions stay extremely close to the LP bound. Relative to BPE, JOLT removes the dependence on local merge heuristics. Relative to UnigramLM, it is not a probabilistic latent-segmentation model.
