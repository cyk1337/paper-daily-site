a:T652,The methodology rests on three intentionally independent measurement paths. First, the authors build a 64-shape sweep of LLM primitives that preserve semantics while changing expression form, and they record supported-device sets per operation rather than a vague residency score. This is how they establish that placement is a property of expression, not semantics. A representative example is that fused RMSNorm is fully eligible while an equivalent subln decomposition is not admitted to ANE execution. Second, they train matched real models across architecture families, sizes, and precisions. Quantized checkpoints are byte-identical in structure to their fp16 counterparts, so deployment comparisons isolate encoding rather than graph differences. Third, they read ANE memory-controller byte counters during inference, which lets them verify what actually ran and how many bytes were moved, instead of trusting compile-time plans or fragile power telemetry. From these measurements they derive several mechanism-level conclusions. Placement is jointly determined by architecture, size, and encoding rather than parameter count alone. Once resident, decode behaves like streaming the full weight set every token, so latency is largely a function of bytes streamed per token. They also show that heavily grouped GQA, especially with 2 KV heads, is a smaller and more compiler-friendly operator family on ANE than the conv-heavy alternatives examined. This reverses the usual CPU-side intuition: on this accelerator, the advantage comes from grouping and byte economy, not from an abstract preference for attention.b:T5ff,Several results carry most of the paper’s weight. On placement, semantically equivalent formulations can diverge completely: fused RMSNorm is reported as 100% eligible while the decomposed form is 0%, which directly supports the expression-vs-semantics claim. On architecture and encoding, a 22.3M attention model in fp16 runs on ANE, while a 25.9M conv-heavy fp16 model does not; yet the same conv graph in int8 or 2-bit reaches roughly 83% on-ANE residency. This shows that parameter count is not the governing variable. On decode cost, hardware counters measure about 1.539 / 0.753 / 0.197 bytes per parameter for fp16 / int8 / 2-bit, versus nominal 2 / 1 / 0.25, indicating real-system packing and runtime effects but confirming that streamed weight bytes dominate. On operator size, heavily grouped GQA with 2 KV heads is around 2.5d^2, whereas the LIV conv family is around 4d^2, and the grouped-attention family is admitted at fp16 while the conv-heavy family is the one that falls off ANE. On the end-to-end frontier, ternary models occupy the best size/speed tradeoff among the tested artifacts: around 25M ternary models span roughly 10.0–10.8 MB and 0.62–0.64 ms across operator mixes, while 50M ternary models are about 16.8 MB and 0.86 ms. Relative to the conv-heavy fp16 starting point, the reported gains are 9.8×/6.1× smaller and 3.0×/2.2× faster. Standard symmetric quantization uses a signed alphabet with z = 0 and a positive absmax scale. The authors observe that because the alphabet is inherently skewed by one extra negative value, allowing the scale to take either sign effectively lets that extra endpoint protect either the negative tail or the positive tail. Flipping the sign of the scale does not introduce a zero point, so the deployed format remains symmetric and avoids the offset-handling overhead of asymmetric quantization.

The main contribution is not merely allowing negative scales, but providing a closed-form sign selection rule that is data-free in deployment and metadata-free when scales are already stored as signed real values. Intuitively, the rule aligns the extra representable endpoint with the dominant outlier direction, reducing clipping-driven error. The paper also provides theory: a worst-case l2 error bound, conditional bound-optimality of the proposed sign rule, and an analytical equivalence between a scale-sign flip and a unit zero-point shift on the same signed alphabet. That equivalence is important because it clarifies what the method gains: a restricted form of asymmetric alignment while preserving the symmetric deployment path.

From a systems perspective, the proposal is compelling because many real formats already store scales as signed floating-point values. In that setting, the change is nearly free. The authors also implement corresponding 2-bit and 4-bit formats in a llama.cpp/GGUF-style setup to show that the idea survives contact with real inference code.e:T657,The evaluation covers model families including Llama3, Qwen3, and Qwen3.5, with metrics spanning WikiText2 perplexity, few-shot accuracy recovery, and actual deployment throughput and memory. The most striking result is on Llama3 8B at 2 bits: WikiText2 perplexity drops from 103.1 under the conventional positive-scale symmetric quantizer to 17.8 with the proposed signed symmetric variant, and few-shot accuracy recovery improves by 7.9 percentage points. That is a large effect for such a small change, and it confirms that endpoint assignment can dominate error in the ultra-low-bit regime.

The comparison to asymmetric quantization is equally important. On an AMD EPYC Turin CPU, the paper reports that a 4-bit symmetric format can use about 9% less memory and achieve around 2.21x higher prefill and 2.03x higher decode throughput than its asymmetric counterpart. The abstract also reports that the proposed signed symmetric method retains a 1.24x prefill advantage, a 1.08x decode advantage, and 14% memory savings over asymmetric quantization while substantially improving over the conventional symmetric baseline. So the goal is not to beat asymmetric quantization on every accuracy metric, but to recover much of its alignment benefit without giving up the symmetric deployment profile.

A useful pattern in the full results is that gains are largest at the lowest bit widths. As precision increases, the benefit shrinks because one-sided clipping is no longer the dominant error source. The inclusion of few-shot accuracy recovery, not just perplexity, also makes the evaluation more relevant to real LLM usage.f:T411,This is a good paper for a fast but careful read.
