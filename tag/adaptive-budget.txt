18:T918,StepPrune models token selection as an autoregressive policy over subsets, P(S|X)=∏t πθ(at|a<t,X). The action space contains all visual tokens plus a learned STOP action. The policy selects tokens one step at a time, conditioned on the image, the text prompt, and the previously selected tokens. The first time STOP is chosen, the episode ends, so the retained token count becomes input-adaptive rather than a manually fixed hyperparameter.

The selector is implemented as a cross-modal pointer-style decoder. This is the key departure from one-shot top-K ranking. Once one token has been selected, the value of nearby or redundant tokens should decrease, while complementary evidence may become more valuable. A sequential policy can model that changing marginal utility; independent scoring cannot.

Training is nontrivial because argmax-based subset selection is discrete. The paper introduces a trajectory-aware differentiable surrogate built around a variance-preserving noise gate. Aggregated pointer probabilities are used to smoothly interpolate each visual token between its original representation and isotropic Gaussian noise. High-probability tokens remain close to the original signal; low-probability tokens are pushed toward noise. This creates a differentiable pathway from the language-modeling objective back to the selector. To prevent the model from reconstructing masked information through neighboring tokens, the method adds a locally masked denoiser that limits cross-token leakage.

A second practical issue is selector overhead. Pure token-by-token decoding can become expensive for long visual prefixes, especially with AnyRes, dynamic-resolution, or multi-tile representations. To reduce recurrent selection latency, StepPrune allows grouped sequential selection: each pointer step selects a small group of tokens instead of exactly one. The paper uses five tokens per step as a default trade-off. This keeps the history-conditioned nature of the policy while making the selector scalable enough to be useful in practice.

So the main distinction from standard pruning baselines is not just a better scoring function. The decision structure itself changes: StepPrune optimizes a selection trajectory with adaptive stopping, rather than assigning independent relevance scores under a fixed budget.19:T537,The evaluation spans four MLLM backbones with different visual sequence organizations: LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-NeXT, Qwen2.5-VL, and InternVL3. That matters because the method is meant to work across fixed-resolution, AnyRes, dynamic-resolution, and multi-tile visual prefixes.

The headline result reported for LLaVA-1.5 is strong from an efficiency perspective: after pruning 88.9% of visual tokens, the model still retains 94.6% of normalized full-prefix performance. With an average of 64 retained tokens, StepPrune achieves a 1.50× prefill speed-up. This indicates substantial redundancy in the original visual prefix for many inputs.

The paper emphasizes two empirical advantages. First, the accuracy-compression trade-off is more stable than with fixed top-K pruning. Second, adaptive stopping allocates fewer tokens to simple inputs and more to visually complex ones, which is more sensible than enforcing the same K for every sample.

The most informative ablations are likely the following: sequential selection versus one-shot selection, with and without the STOP action, and different group sizes for grouped selection.
