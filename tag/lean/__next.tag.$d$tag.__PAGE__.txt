9:T61f,The framework has two stages. In stage one, the system starts from roughly 17.8K theorem statements collected from public formal corpora, mainly NuminaMath-LEAN and Lean-Workbook. For each theorem, a model is prompted to produce a proof attempt in a single pass, as numbered natural-language steps paired with corresponding Lean tactics. A key design choice is that the model never receives Lean execution feedback during generation. This preserves genuine model errors instead of repaired trajectories, which is exactly what a verifier needs to learn from.

In stage two, the formal proof is compiled in Lean. All steps before the first failing tactic are labeled correct; the first failing step and all subsequent steps are labeled incorrect. The authors recognize that Lean failure is not always a mathematical failure: some failures are purely syntactic or due to formalization mismatch. To reduce such false negatives, they add a second model call that reads the Lean error message, attempts one syntax repair, and classifies the failure as mathematical versus merely formal. Only proofs whose first failure is judged to be mathematical are kept. For fully compiling proofs, there is an additional consistency check to ensure the natural-language steps actually express the same mathematics as the formal proof.

This yields 10,634 labeled proofs and 152,005 step labels, plus a held-out 200-proof benchmark called LeanPRM-Bench. Verifiers are then trained purely on natural-language inputs, so Lean is only used once during data creation, not at inference time.a:T535,The main empirical result is a controlled comparison against PRM800K-style human supervision. With the same model, the same training recipe, and the same number of updates, LeanPRM-Verifier-4B outperforms the PRM800K-trained verifier on four out of five benchmarks. The gains are large: +14.4 exact step-label accuracy on LeanPRM-Bench, +9.7 on Hard2Verify, and +9.4 on IMO-GradingBench.

The paper also evaluates proof selection in a best-of-N setting on IMO-level problems. The LeanPRM-trained verifier selects correct proofs noticeably more often than random, while the PRM800K verifier stays close to chance. This is an important sanity check: the model is not merely learning stylistic preferences, but something closer to proof validity.

Another useful result is scale consistency. The improvement from LeanPRM supervision appears across all tested model sizes, from 0.6B to 8B, on LeanPRM-Bench, Hard2Verify, and IMO-GradingBench. Given that the dataset is only 10.6K proofs, this suggests that supervision quality and domain alignment matter more than raw label count in this setting.

The most informative ablations are the ones around filtering and label construction: whether syntax-repair filtering is used, how much noise comes from formal-only failures, and how robust the gains are across model scales and benchmarks.b:T588,The method is a staged verification pipeline. First, an LLM rewrites the original problem using strategies such as numerical reparameterization, logical restructuring, constraint modification, and contextual reconstruction. Importantly, the rewriter is instructed not to solve the rewritten problem, which helps separate problem generation from answer generation. Second, RePro performs conservative semantic screening. This stage filters out candidates that are ambiguous, under-specified, contradictory, unrealistic, or infeasible. The paper is careful about the notion of feasibility: it is not merely formal derivability, but semantic and constraint consistency of the rewritten problem. Third, the surviving candidates are translated into executable Lean specifications, and Lean-oriented neural ATPs such as Goedel-Prover and DeepSeek-Prover search for candidate proof scripts. A proof is accepted only if it passes Lean kernel-level verification.

This design differs from prior rewriting-based evaluation in three ways. It verifies answers through formal proof rather than heuristic checking. It evaluates reliability along three axes—well-definedness, feasibility, and answer correctness—rather than treating validity as a single vague notion. And it explicitly trades recall for precision by retaining only proof-verified instances. That trade-off is deliberate and sensible for benchmark construction.c:T4a3,The experiments are conducted on GSM8K and MATH. The headline result is straightforward: among the retained rewritten instances, RePro achieves 100% well-definedness, feasibility, and answer correctness, whereas prior rewriting methods still leave invalid questions or incorrect reference answers in the benchmark. This is the central empirical claim, and it supports the paper’s argument that formal verification changes the reliability ceiling of rewritten evaluation sets.

A second important result concerns reformulation sensitivity. Using proof-verified rewrites, the authors show that several models suffer noticeable accuracy drops when mathematically equivalent problems are restated in new forms. Because the rewritten items are verified, the drop is more plausibly attributed to sensitivity to formulation and possible memorization dependence, rather than to benchmark corruption. The paper also makes clear that not every rewrite survives the pipeline: the released benchmark is a retained subset of candidates that can be formalized and proved. That gives high precision, but limits coverage and may bias the benchmark toward problems that are easier to formalize in Lean.d:T519,For each intended theorem T, the authors construct a complete shadow set 𝒮={S1,...,Sn}. Each shadow theorem Si is chosen so that T implies Si, giving a forward checker. The set is complete only if the conjunction S1∧...∧Sn implies T, giving a backward checker. Both directions are encoded and mechanically verified in Lean, so completeness is not an informal annotation claim but a machine-checked property. During evaluation, the generated candidate theorem \hat{T} is appended with these checkers. It receives credit only if it type-checks and passes the implication tests induced by the shadow set. A notable design choice is the annotation workflow: experts do not write everything from scratch. LLMs first draft candidate shadows, experts revise them, and Lean verifies completeness. This keeps expert labor focused on correction and validation rather than blank-page authoring. The method is especially well suited to multi-conclusion theorems, bundled conditions, existence/uniqueness statements, and other structures where partial formalization can still compile. In localization, the system decomposes coarse benchmark steps into finer EdgeUnits using a proof-state-tree representation. Each transition in this tree corresponds to a local reasoning responsibility, which makes the evaluation target much sharper than judging an entire paragraph-like step. In verification, the method extracts typed proof obligations from suspicious transitions. These obligations are not treated uniformly; they encode different kinds of reasoning responsibilities such as algebraic transformation, implication transfer, existence construction, boundary-condition handling, or case transitions. The system then gathers evidence through both lightweight checks and Lean-based validation. Lean is used as a structured evidence source rather than a direct judge. A key safeguard is the semantic alignment score, which measures whether the formalized statement preserves the assumptions, directionality, quantification, and strength of the original informal obligation. Lean evidence is only trusted when this alignment is high enough, preventing drifted or weakened targets from producing false reassurance. In synthesis, local evidence is aggregated back to the original step level to predict the first erroneous benchmark step. The experimental story is important because it tests whether local formal evidence, when filtered for semantic faithfulness, actually improves early-error localization.
