18:T769,The core idea is replay-driven decoupling. Rather than forcing the trainer to wait for a newly collected on-policy batch, ReFlow maintains a buffer of recent trajectories and allows optimization to proceed continuously by sampling from that replay pool while rollout workers asynchronously add fresh data. The point is not to import generic off-policy RL wholesale, but to exploit short-horizon sample reuse without letting policy mismatch grow too large.

The design appears to have three layers. First, execution-level decoupling: rollout and training are disaggregated so the learner is no longer tightly gated by fresh generation. This directly reduces long-tail bubbles caused by variable decoding and environment latency. Second, algorithmic support for replay: recently generated samples are reused under staleness-aware controls and correction mechanisms so that replay improves efficiency without drifting too far from the current policy. The paper also claims a theoretical result that replay can reduce gradient variance, which is relevant because LLM RL suffers from noisy rewards and large sequence-level variance. Third, runtime adaptivity: instead of fixing rollout/training resource splits and generation concurrency, ReFlow adjusts them as workload characteristics shift over time.

Relative to standard asynchronous actor-learner systems, the differences are clear. Existing designs usually tolerate only mild stale on-policy data to hide some generation latency. ReFlow goes further and treats replay as the main lever for reducing rollout demand. Existing systems also tend to treat resource allocation as a static tuning problem; ReFlow makes adaptation part of the system design. At the same time, the paper seems careful not to push into fully off-policy RL with very old data.
