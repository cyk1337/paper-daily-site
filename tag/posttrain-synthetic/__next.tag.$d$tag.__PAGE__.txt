这个设计有几个关键点。第一，cross-problem control 的作用是消掉“给模型额外看一段解答文本”带来的通用风格偏移，只保留与当前题目相关的 peer-induced change。没有这个参照，模型可能学到的是 generic solution-format bias，而不是题目局部推理信息。第二，取最小支持幅度而不是平均，是为了抑制错误 peer 带来的过强偏移，属于一种保守的交集式蒸馏。第三，学生训练时只看题目和自己的前缀，不看 peer；peer 只在训练时用于构造 teacher，因此推理时没有额外开销。这一点和 privileged-information distillation 的设定一致。

The key idea is not to imitate any single peer solution. Instead, SRD measures how different same-problem peers consistently shift a frozen model’s token distribution relative to a shared control.

For a problem x, the current policy samples K trajectories. When training on a student-generated prefix, the method selects M same-problem peers and one cross-problem solution as a control. A frozen copy of the initial model predicts next-token distributions under two conditions: the student prefix plus a same-problem peer, and the student prefix plus the cross-problem control. Their difference is a peer-induced residual shift. SRD then aggregates these residuals conservatively: it keeps only coordinates whose direction agrees across peers, and sets the retained magnitude to the smallest supporting magnitude among those peers. This shared residual is added back to the frozen model’s distribution under the original student input, producing a teacher target. The student is trained with forward KL on that target.

Three design choices matter. First, the cross-problem control removes generic distribution shifts caused by simply appending a solution-like text, so the retained residual is more problem-specific. Second, the minimum-supported magnitude acts as a conservative intersection rule, reducing the influence of noisy or wrong peers. Third, peers are used only to construct the teacher during training; the student itself conditions only on the problem and its own prefix, so inference cost does not increase.

Let q be the question and a the correct answer. Forward CoT (FCG) samples reasoning from the teacher conditioned only on q. Rejection sampling (RS) keeps only those forward samples whose extracted final answer matches a. Reverse CoT (RCG) instead conditions the teacher on both q and a. The authors argue that the student should ideally learn from the RS distribution, not from raw RCG outputs.

To do this, they build a small paired dataset. For each question, they sample one reverse CoT response x, then sample forward CoTs until they obtain the first accepted response z with the correct final answer. This yields paired examples (x, z). A translator model G_phi is then fine-tuned with teacher forcing to predict z from x.

A crucial design choice is that the translator only receives the reverse CoT text x, not the original question q or answer a. This serves two purposes. First, it matches the paper’s distributional derivation, where the goal is to map source responses to target responses. Second, it blocks a direct question-answer shortcut and forces the model to learn how to rewrite answer-conditioned rationales into outputs closer to accepted forward reasoning.

Once trained, the translator is used at scale: each new question requires one reverse teacher call to produce x and one translator call to produce z-hat, and the student is then trained on (q, z-hat). Compared with RS, which may need many forward teacher calls per accepted sample, this pipeline compresses the expensive part into one cheap teacher sample plus one translation step.

Relative to prior semantic suppression or structural-guidance methods, the difference is conceptual. Those methods still generate under answer access and try to reduce leakage. CoTranslator instead learns an explicit distributional correction from RCG-style outputs toward RS-style outputs.
