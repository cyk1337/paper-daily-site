17:T622,The work has two components. The first is Social Gym, a unified environment for multi-agent social games. Its design follows two principles: every game must have an algorithmically verifiable outcome such as win/loss, score, or survival; and the game suite must cover multiple facets of social intelligence rather than a single domain. System-wise, it extends the SOTOPIA interaction loop to arbitrary N-agent settings, uses a finite-state-machine engine for complex phase transitions, and introduces a visibility layer with public, team-private, and private channels. This makes hidden-state inference, deception, coalition reasoning, and Theory-of-Mind explicit parts of the environment. The 21 games are organized by information structure and communication mode, and a unified Elo tournament produces both per-game and aggregate rankings.

The second component is SPaRTan, a training-free self-improvement loop. A model first plays self-play games, then reads its own trajectories and writes a transferable strategic playbook, and finally injects that playbook into the system prompt for future games. The method is framed as a form of in-context fine-tuning: the trajectories act as training data, and the playbook acts as the updated non-parametric state. The choice of natural-language playbooks instead of parameter updates is deliberate: it avoids the cost and instability of RL or SFT, and it makes transfer across games, roles, and even models directly testable. The paper also studies distillation, where a stronger model writes playbooks for weaker students.18:T5f6,The benchmark evaluates 7 LLMs across 21 social games using tournaments, reporting per-game win rates and an overall Elo leaderboard. GPT-5-mini ranks highest overall, but the more important result is the strong ranking inversion across games and roles: no single model dominates every game type. That suggests social reasoning is not a single scalar capability; hidden-information inference, negotiation, deception, cooperation, and reputation tracking vary independently.

SPaRTan is evaluated along four axes: iterative within-game reflection over rounds R1–R4, transfer from one source game to held-out target games, hold-one-out multigame transfer, and distillation of strong-model playbooks into weaker students. The main finding is that GPT-5-mini-generated playbooks can improve the structurally weaker side of asymmetric games, and these gains can transfer across games and into weaker models. However, the effect is strongly capacity-dependent and largely disappears for Qwen3-32B. That is an important result: writing down strategic rules is not enough; the receiving model must be capable of interpreting and applying them in context.

The excerpt also makes clear that Social Gym’s rewards are verifiable, which makes the environment suitable not only for benchmarking but also for downstream value estimation and RLVR-style training. The deployed student has no inference-time memory dependency. Level-0 experience memory stores raw trajectories, rewards, and feedback. It has the highest fidelity but weak transfer. Level-1 insight memory reflects over multiple attempts on the same problem and extracts compact strategies and lessons, removing much of the trajectory-level redundancy. Level-2 behavior memory groups semantically related problems and distills reusable reasoning patterns across them; this has the strongest transfer but also the highest risk of becoming too coarse. Training proceeds in a co-evolving loop: the current policy generates rollouts, receives verifier feedback, writes new information into memory, and the refreshed memory then conditions a self-teacher that supervises the next policy update. This is the main departure from SDPO. In SDPO, the teacher is still largely episode-local, built from sibling rollouts or local feedback. In PMD, the teacher is additionally informed by what the model has learned in prior epochs, so supervision carries cross-episode procedural structure. The co-evolution claim is central. A frozen memory becomes stale as the policy changes its visited states; a frozen policy prevents memory from improving in quality as capability grows. Another important design choice is to avoid inference-time memory use. Many reflection or memory-agent methods improve performance by carrying external memory into deployment, but that adds system complexity and a train-test mismatch. PMD instead treats memory as a training scaffold whose purpose is to be internalized.1b:T5cd,The headline results are fairly clear from the provided text. Relative to GRPO and SDPO, PMD improves performance by 3.8–5.5% on SCIKNOWEVAL and 7.9–13.6% on LIVECODEBENCH. The most important evidence is mechanistic rather than just end-task gain: freezing either the memory while updating the policy, or freezing the policy while updating the memory, causes more than a 10% drop on SCIKNOWEVAL. That directly supports the claim that policy-memory co-evolution is the main source of improvement rather than an implementation detail. The paper’s design logic is:
1) Textual trace replay is bottlenecked: raw CoT traces (even with reflection-style refinement) are surface artifacts and do not reliably transfer; hidden states may contain more abstract structure than what is verbalized.
2) Internal-reward learning can provide a learning signal without labels, but prior approaches are mostly offline and can degrade as more test data accumulates.
3) Modularity isolates updates: per-instance memories are stored separately and composed at retrieval time, aiming to mitigate interference and forgetting compared to full-parameter RL/finetuning.
Operationally it looks like retrieval (select relevant memories) + injection (compose soft prompts) + tiny online optimization (memory-only).1e:T516,The paper formalizes a harness h as a persistent collection of tools, prompts, and skills. Given a task t, solve(h, t) produces a trajectory τ containing observations, reasoning traces, tool calls, and the final answer. The true utility U(t, τ) is latent in deployment because there is no representative future validation set. RHO replaces this unavailable utility with a self-preference estimator: the agent compares multiple trajectories for the same task, produces a ranking, and explains the preference. The pipeline has three stages. First, coreset selection chooses diverse and challenging tasks from past trajectories, so the optimization budget is spent on informative cases rather than easy or redundant ones. Second, group rollout re-solves each selected task multiple times in parallel, producing alternative trajectories under the current harness. Third, best-of-N harness proposal uses diagnostic signals to generate candidate harness updates and selects among them with pairwise self-preference. The two key diagnostics are self-validation within a trajectory and self-consistency across parallel trajectories. The method differs from simple experience accumulation: it does not merely append past traces to context, but turns them into targeted changes to skills, tools, and workflows.1f:T402,The evaluation covers three agent domains: software engineering, technical work, and knowledge work. The clearest reported result is on software engineering: a single round of retrospective harness optimization improves SWE-Bench Pro pass rate from 59% to 78% without grading against a validation set. The paper also reports consistent gains across all three domains, outperforming straightforward experience accumulation and validation-feedback-driven evolution under a comparable budget. Diagnostic analysis shows that RHO tends to design skills and tools aimed at typical failure modes found in past trajectories. These updates change the agent’s action patterns and help maintain higher accuracy in long-horizon sessions. Ablations indicate that the stages contribute progressively: coreset selection concentrates the budget on useful tasks, group rollout creates comparable alternatives, self-validation and self-consistency isolate improvement signals, and pairwise self-preference selects the final harness candidate.20:T8d9,The method, Bidirectional Evolutionary Search (BES), has two tightly coupled components.

The first is bidirectional search. The forward side generates candidate solutions in the usual search sense. The backward side starts from the goal or terminal condition and decomposes it into finer-grained sub-goals, which provide denser intermediate verification signals. The motivation is straightforward: if the verifier only gives a terminal binary reward, search has little guidance on whether an unfinished trajectory is structurally promising. Backward decomposition turns a sparse end reward into a set of local constraints, making partial trajectories more comparable.

The second is the use of evolutionary operators. The authors argue that expansion-only search is fundamentally limited because every new candidate must be produced by extending an existing prefix. That keeps search near the model’s own continuation distribution. BES introduces four operators: combination, translocation, deletion, and crossover. The recombination-style operators merge useful segments from different trajectories, while deletion removes the least reliable segment from a candidate. This means new candidates no longer need to arise from a single left-to-right rollout; they can be assembled through discrete structural edits over existing traces. The paper also provides a theoretical argument that expansion-only search is confined to a relatively narrow entropy shell, whereas evolutionary operators can escape that region. It is not a full optimality result, but it gives a clear mechanism for why recombination may outperform pure tree expansion.

In implementation terms, BES treats the LLM as a proposal and mutation engine. The model generates trajectories, rewrites segments, decomposes goals, and helps score local structure, while the search framework maintains the candidate pool and applies bidirectional and evolutionary operations. Compared with Tree-of-Thought, beam search, or MCTS, the key difference is that BES allows cross-branch recombination rather than only branch-wise expansion. Compared with coding-oriented evolutionary systems such as OpenEvolve, BES adds backward goal decomposition so that verification is not only applied at the terminal state.21:T762,The experiments are split into post-training and inference settings. The most important empirical claim is not a small average gain on easy benchmarks, but a failure-mode difference on hard reasoning tasks: baseline methods often cannot find enough high-quality samples to improve the model at all, and sometimes even degrade it, whereas BES continues to surface usable trajectories.

In the post-training setup, the paper studies logical reasoning and multi-hop reasoning tasks where GRPO, MaxRL, and Tree-GRPO struggle because positive samples are too rare under the base policy. The central result is that BES substantially increases the discovery rate of high-scoring trajectories, which in turn makes downstream RLVR updates effective. The key thing to inspect is not only final benchmark scores, but the regime where baselines barely move or regress while BES still produces measurable improvement.

In inference, BES is evaluated on three open problem-solving benchmarks against open-source frameworks including OpenEvolve, GEPA, and ShinkaEvolve. The reported outcome is that BES finds more stable and higher-quality solutions. The paper also includes component ablations showing that both the bidirectional design and the evolutionary operators matter. Removing backward decomposition makes search rely too heavily on terminal verification. Removing recombination operators makes search more likely to stay trapped in the model’s existing modes. The case studies are useful because they show that some successful solutions are not obtained by gradually extending a single trajectory, but by recombining two incomplete trajectories that each contain locally correct pieces.
