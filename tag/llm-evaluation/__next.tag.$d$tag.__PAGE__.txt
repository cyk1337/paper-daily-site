a:T42f,Reasoning Jury is a two-phase evaluation system for step-segmented reasoning traces marked with [STEP-x] tokens. Phase 1 is independent judgment: a panel of jurors from diverse model families each read the problem and reasoning trace independently, and output a structured defect list including description, affected step IDs, severity, and supporting evidence. This ensures no anchoring effect and decorrelated judgment biases across jurors. Phase 2 is consensus building, with two available modes: Consolidation mode passes the original trace and all Phase 1 verdicts to a judge model to produce a unified consensus. Deliberation mode uses a moderator to run multi-turn debates between jurors, who discuss defect points until consensus is reached or discussion stalls. The moderator never accesses the original problem or reasoning trace — it only maintains consensus state at the claim level, selects speakers, and decides termination timing, then synthesizes the final consensus. All outputs are grounded to specific step markers for auditability and actionability.b:T5fc,The evaluation covers 56,476 inferences: 4 models, 2,017 items, and 7 budgets. Non-monotone behavior appears in roughly 3% to 19% of items under the main controlled analyses, and reaches 25.8% in some all-item settings. It is also model-specific: the same item rarely induces overthinking across models, with cross-model overlap around 6% to 14%. Model rankings reverse across budgets. On GSM8K, LLaMA-3.3 70B leads at 256 tokens with 62.4% accuracy, while GPT-OSS 20B reaches 94.8% at 4,096 tokens and becomes the strongest model under that budget. On GPQA, LLaMA-3 8B is nominally first at 512 tokens with 21.2%, while GPT-OSS 20B reaches 51.0% at 4,096 tokens, close to another model at 50.0%; the small GPQA sample limits significance at that endpoint, but several intermediate reversals are significant. Truncation is a major confound: Qwen-3 32B remains truncated on 59.7% of GPQA items even at 4,096 tokens, while LLaMA-3.3 70B is truncated on only 0.5%. Oracle analysis finds large complementarity. On GPQA, the oracle exceeds the best single model by up to 27.8 percentage points. On GSM8K, the oracle gap peaks at +16.9 points at 256 tokens and then declines as models converge. The budget-aware router improves cross-domain GPQA performance by +2.67 points over the best-per-budget baseline, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.94, 4.40], capturing 14.1% of the oracle gap. Budget features help within-domain by +1.6 to +5.7 points but hurt cross-domain transfer by 1.2 points, indicating domain-specific budget effects.c:T764,The setup distinguishes the as-of date t0 from the model training cutoff T. Text written after t0 but before T is bridge data. It can contain information caused by the later world state that also determines the final outcome Y. A model trained on this bridge data may obtain outcome information without ever seeing the benchmark item itself. The paper formalizes this as a back-door leakage path from the later world state through bridge data into the model prediction.

The first technical result is a non-identifiability theorem. A passive backtest observes forecasts, outcomes, timing relative to the cutoff, and possibly covariates, but it cannot separate recency from leakage. The identified set for leakage inflation is a sharp interval, and collecting more items does not make it collapse. A flat pre/post profile is therefore not proof of cleanliness, and a non-flat profile is not proof of leakage.

The second part derives a per-question leakage law. Inflation is the product of honest uncertainty and an extraction factor. The extraction factor has a strong nonlinear payoff: extracting half of the leaked signal already yields about three quarters of the full inflation. This explains why weak recall can still move benchmark scores. The analysis predicts that leakage concentrates on questions that surprised the crowd and had dense training coverage.

The measurement strategy requires an external reference. With a known cutoff, the paper uses a regression-discontinuity design to identify cutoff-localized leakage. With a matched clean control, it uses a difference-in-differences design to estimate global leakage and report a leakage-adjusted score. Matching is central: naive pre/post comparisons are replaced by comparisons that control for timing, question characteristics, and baseline difficulty. Paraphrase probes are treated as detection tools, not measurement tools.d:T4ee,The empirical section starts with ForecastBench. The authors evaluate five flagship models on the official panel, restricted to questions that resolved after each model’s documented training cutoff. These outcomes could not have been memorized under the documented cutoffs. Even so, four of the five models fail the standard pre/post check, with spurious leakage gaps as large as +0.061. A matched-window check collapses the gaps across all five models, supporting the interpretation that the apparent signal is recency rather than leakage.

The estimators are validated using twin models with planted leakage. One model is kept clean, while the other receives controlled leakage. The proposed estimators recover the planted dose, location, and per-question profile, and return a null result on the clean twin. On deployed frontier systems, the estimators identify one cutoff-localized signature and clear five models whose apparent advantages were explained by recency at the design’s power floors.

The most important reported numbers are: five flagship models; four false failures under the standard check on post-cutoff-resolved questions; a maximum spurious gap of +0.061; and successful recovery of planted leakage in controlled twin-model experiments.e:T5ce,The benchmark has three main components. First, the authors derive a structure-level constraint taxonomy from real prompts. They start from PromptSet, filter GitHub LLM application prompts by repository popularity, prompt length, and explicit output constraints, and retain 1,232 prompt templates. GPT-5.4 is used to annotate output-format constraints, with manual review for new categories. This yields 10 structure-level constraint types grounded in actual application prompts rather than purely synthetic design.

Second, they synthesize hierarchical prompts as constraint trees. Starting from IFEval seed tasks, they sample trees in a depth-first manner, while a sibling-conflict matrix prevents incompatible sibling constraints from co-occurring. Compatible sibling constraints are then merged into natural-language instructions so the final prompt resembles a realistic single-call specification rather than a raw concatenation of rules.

Third, they build deterministic hierarchical checkers. Each constraint type has a Python checker, and these are composed along the tree. The key idea is scope-aware verification: the checker first identifies the relevant region, then evaluates inner constraints only within that region. This avoids the scope leakage that affects flat benchmarks. The technical contribution is therefore not a new model, but a careful benchmark-construction pipeline: real-world constraint extraction, tree-based synthesis, and deterministic scoped checking.f:T4d6,The benchmark contains 600 prompts, evenly stratified across constraint-tree depths 0 through 3, with 150 prompts per depth. It covers 35 constraints in total: 10 structure-level and 25 content-level. The paper evaluates seven leading models.

Two findings matter most. First, even the best model only slightly exceeds 50% prompt-level accuracy, far below the near-saturated numbers often seen on flat instruction-following benchmarks. Second, performance degrades sharply as hierarchy depth increases. This indicates that the difficulty is not just “more constraints,” but maintaining nested scope and structure over the full response.

Prompt-level accuracy is intentionally strict: a prompt is counted correct only if the entire constraint tree is satisfied. That makes the metric closer to deployment reality than averaging independent constraint checks. The most informative breakdowns are likely the depth-wise curves and the error categories where outer structural failures invalidate inner content checks. If the appendix includes prompt examples and checker traces, those are useful for understanding whether failures come from missing outer sections, misplaced content, or local keyword compliance without correct structure.10:T442,LivingArena implements a bidirectional, multi-round game between two models. In each round, the asker must output a structured tuple: question q, gold answer a*, and verification logic v*. A three-judge model panel then performs:
1) Validation: strict veto rule—any INVALID vote rejects the item. Rejection triggers a self-harm penalty for the asker (-1) and ends the round, discouraging ambiguous/unverifiable prompts.
2) Grading: if validated, the answerer sees only q and produces response r. Judges compare r against a*; majority vote decides correctness.
Scoring rewards the asker only when the question is validated and the opponent answers incorrectly; otherwise the answerer is rewarded. Each pair plays in both directions to remove role bias. A key design choice is “router-like preservation” of truthfulness: since the asker must supply a verifiable gold answer that survives independent validation, models are constrained from reliably asking beyond their own knowledge boundary, making failures more interpretable as genuine knowledge/skill gaps rather than pure trickery.11:T516,The method operates in a strict query-only setting. Given only a query q, it uses no benchmark responses, preference labels, reference answers, human-written rubrics, or model training. The framework has three LLM roles: a rubric proposer, response generators, and a rubric-blind pairwise judge. A deterministic controller maintains the active rubric set, a tree structure, and evolution memory. At each step, the proposer emits either an Add patch for one atomic rubric or a Split patch for a bundled rubric. The central design choice is to avoid asking an LLM to compare two abstract rubric sets. Instead, each candidate rubric is tested in response space. The local pair starts from a strong answer satisfying the background rubrics and the candidate rubric, then minimally edits it to violate the candidate. This tests whether violating the rubric makes an otherwise similar answer worse. The alternative pair is generated from scratch: it first creates the strongest answer that satisfies the background rubrics while violating the candidate, then minimally repairs it to satisfy the candidate. This catches over-specific, optional, or style-only rubrics. The judge compares both pairs in both presentation orders.
