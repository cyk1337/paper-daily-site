9:T601,The main strength is the controlled design. The authors build a matched story-continuation setup: they truncate human stories at controlled prefix lengths and ask four checkpoints from the same model lineage to continue them. The human corpora are chosen to span distinct writing domains: StoryStar as public-platform fiction with low editorial filtering, TMAS as prompt-guided story writing, and The New Yorker as professionally edited literary fiction. This is not just a dataset choice; it is a way to test whether post-training adapts to domain-specific narrative texture or instead pulls all continuations toward a single default regime.

Measurement is also more structured than standard diversity metrics. Rather than relying on preference judgments or lexical variety alone, the paper decomposes “flatness” into three sentence-level dynamic facets. Thematic motion tracks how topics move through a story, including whether the text alternates between dwelling and abrupt shifts or progresses in a more regularized way. Affective prevalence measures the distribution of emotional states, especially whether high-arousal states such as surprise, curiosity, and conflict are suppressed. Linguistic diversity captures stylistic range and cross-domain differentiation. The authors are careful about the scope of inference: the design provides stage-wise geometric evidence about where post-trained checkpoints move in distributional space, but it does not identify which exact data mixture or reward component caused the movement.a:T656,The key experimental asset is the four-checkpoint OLMo 32B path: Base, SFT, DPO, and RLVR. Because they share the same pretrained backbone, observed differences can be attributed much more cleanly to post-training. The main findings are threefold. First, variation contracts monotonically across all three measured dimensions as post-training progresses. Thematic progression becomes more uniform, with within-story topic-jump variance falling below human levels by RLVR. Affectively, high-arousal states are reduced while neutral affect becomes more prevalent. Stylistically, outputs collapse toward a narrower region of language space. Second, this contraction is directionally stable across all three human corpora but differs in magnitude. The New Yorker is compressed the most, because its human baseline is farther from the post-trained model’s default continuation regime. StoryStar and TMAS show smaller gaps, not because the model is especially good at those domains, but because those domains already sit closer to the post-training attractor. Third, post-trained endpoints converge strongly across corpora. One of the clearest numbers reported is that cross-domain style divergence drops by more than 90% from the human level to RLVR. That suggests post-training is not learning domain-sensitive continuation behavior; it is imposing a common writing mode.

The paper’s contribution is therefore diagnostic rather than benchmark-driven. For each compositional instance of depth k, the authors define the required atomic facts, multiple paraphrased probes for each atom, and aligned sub-questions that match how each atom appears inside the chain. An example passes the atomic gate only if every atomic fact is answered correctly and consistently across paraphrases. It then passes the sub-question gate only if every aligned sub-question is answered correctly in isolation. Residual composition failure is measured only on examples that pass both gates but still fail on the main compositional question.

This changes the estimand. Instead of attributing the entire single-hop to multi-hop drop to composition, the protocol conditions on stable atomic access and explicit local verbalization. The paper then decomposes post-training gains into three channels: Δatom for atomic stability, Δcomp for residual composition at matched atoms, and Δdepth for the depth at which residual failure first exceeds 50%. This decomposition is the main conceptual tool of the paper.

The benchmark is D4v2, a temporal compositional dataset with 390 main questions and 2,490 atomic sub-questions spanning four task families and depths 2 through 11. The evaluation covers six open-weight models in the 7B–13B range across several post-training recipes: base, RLHF, SFT reasoning-trace distillation, and native outcome-verified RL. A particularly strong part of the design is the controlled same-base LoRA study comparing SFT-answer, SFT-trace, and GRPO-style training, which helps isolate objective-level effects from base-model confounds. The authors also note an important caveat: residual composition failure is still an upper bound on pure composition failure, because formatting issues, distractor attention, and decoding-path degeneracy can also contribute.d:T636,There are four results worth retaining. First, base models are mostly filtered out by the atomic gate rather than by composition. The paper reports that base models admit fewer than five cases at any depth, which means many apparent multi-hop failures are really failures of stable atomic access.

Second, once atomic stability is matched within 2 percentage points, residual composition failure can still vary from 0% to 47% at depth 2 depending on the post-training recipe. This is the empirical signature of composition collapse: nearly identical atomic knowledge, sharply different composition behavior. Under matched data and compute, outcome-verified RL substantially outperforms SFT trace distillation on this metric.

Third, training on a specific composition depth closes much of the residual failure at that depth but transfers only weakly to nearby held-out depths. That suggests the learned behavior is not a broadly extrapolating compositional procedure.

Fourth, enabling chain-of-thought reduces residual failure by roughly 3–5×. This is an important clue about mechanism: a large part of the bottleneck lies in generation-time computation, not only in static knowledge representation. The model may already possess the required facts, yet still fail to assemble them without enough inference-time workspace.
