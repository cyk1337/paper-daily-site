18:T60e,CLR is a training-free two-stage framework. In Stage 1, the model samples K full solution traces. Each trace must output not only a final answer but also exactly M decision-critical claims: concise intermediate propositions whose failure would undermine the answer. These are meant to be semantic anchors such as key constraints, transformations, maximality conditions, or intermediate conclusions, not generic summaries or answer restatements. The purpose is to compress a long reasoning trace into a small set of high-information, checkable propositions. In Stage 2, the same model is not asked to solve the problem again. Instead, it receives only the original problem and the extracted claims, and it actively searches for contradictions, counterexamples, missing conditions, factual mistakes, or unsupported inferences. The central design choice is falsification rather than proof. There is a useful asymmetry here: constructing a fully correct solution is hard, but invalidating a wrong trace only requires finding one decisive flaw. After verification, CLR maps claim-level verdicts into a nonlinear trace-level reliability score and aggregates answer groups by total reliability. This allows a reliable minority to overturn an incorrect majority. Relative to self-consistency, the method reallocates part of the budget from generating more traces to verifying the semantic anchors of existing traces. Relative to step-level verification, it avoids exhaustive checking and does not require process supervision or a separately trained verifier.19:T4ca,The evaluation spans four LLMs and multiple difficult reasoning benchmarks, with comparisons made under matched model-call budgets. The budget accounting is clean: CLR@K uses K generation calls plus K verification calls, matching the 2K generation calls of Cons@2K. Two headline results are especially informative. On HMMT25, Gemma-4-12B-it improves from 76.67% with Cons@64 to 88.75% with CLR@32, a gain of 12.08 points under the same call budget. On CMIMC25, GPT-OSS-20B improves from 77.50% with Cons@64 to 82.19% with CLR@32 while using 37.0% fewer generated tokens, and it outperforms Pass@1 by 27.15 points. The paper’s stronger point is not just accuracy but token efficiency: Stage 2 verifies compressed claims instead of regenerating long chains, so the method can improve both correctness and compute efficiency. The qualitative analysis argues that whole-trace scoring is easily dominated by long stretches of routine reasoning, whereas claim-level falsification exposes decisive errors more directly.
