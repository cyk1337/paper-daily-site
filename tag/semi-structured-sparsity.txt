18:T8b3,The technical contribution is a carefully controlled comparison framework. The paper compares 2:4 sparsity against dense quantization in two ways. The first is equal bits: every stored component is counted, including values, indices, scales, and zero points. Under this accounting, 2:4 is far less storage-efficient than the naive 'half the weights' intuition suggests, because each retained value carries metadata. The paper makes this concrete: 2:4 with INT8 values costs about the same as dense INT5, 2:4 with INT4 values about the same as dense INT3, and 2:4 with FP16 can even exceed FP8 in cost. The second is equal tensor-core tiers: both lower precision and 2:4 may promise similar throughput classes on paper, but the paper measures actual kernel latency and end-to-end cost rather than relying on theoretical peak throughput.

A major strength is that the comparison spans all six GEMM operand families in a transformer layer, not just static weights. Dynamic operands such as activations, Q/K/V, and attention probabilities must be pruned and compressed online, so their preprocessing overhead is part of the real deployment cost. The paper also enforces the hardware-imposed sparsity axis, which is crucial because 2:4 is not a free-form pruning pattern.

For error analysis, the paper introduces 2:4 energy loss: within each hardware-defined group of four along the reduction axis, 2:4 drops two values, and the lost energy fraction can be computed directly from the tensor. This quantity tracks the observed accuracy gap well and explains why Hadamard rotation often hurts 2:4: rotation spreads energy more evenly, so each forced local drop removes more useful signal. This is a more faithful predictor than global tensor statistics such as kurtosis, because the hardware constraint is local and axis-specific.

At the quantization floor, the paper separates one-shot pruning from retraining. The conclusion is that 2:4 can provide real speedups there, but usually only with retraining; one-shot pruning is not viable. The paper also evaluates Blackwell sparse FP4, noting that its pairwise 4:8 pattern runs faster than dense NVFP4 but discards more information and cannot directly reuse models retrained for 2:4.19:T7b6,The experiments cover every layer of two 8B models and run on three GPU generations: Ada, Hopper, and Blackwell. The results are best understood in three parts.

First, under equal-bit comparisons, moving to a lower dense precision is consistently more accurate than adding 2:4 sparsity at the same storage budget. Part of this comes from proper bit accounting: once metadata is counted, 2:4 competes against lower-bit dense formats than many prior papers imply. The deeper reason is the error structure: 2:4 removes signal energy that cannot be recovered by increasing the precision of the surviving values, while quantization error decreases with bitwidth.

Second, under equal tensor-core tiers, the paper finds that for every throughput tier there exists a dense format that is both faster and more accurate than the corresponding 2:4 option. This remains true when online pruning and quantization costs are included, which matters because dynamic operands are expensive to sparsify on the fly.

Third, at the quantization floor, 2:4 starts to make sense. Stacked on FP8, it speeds up linear layers by about 1.3x to 1.7x. A retrained 2:4 model served with vLLM is faster than the dense FP8 model in both prefill and decode, while maintaining roughly comparable GSM8K accuracy. The paper includes an important control: if the same sparse weights are served with dense kernels, the speedup disappears, confirming that the gain comes from sparse tensor-core execution rather than unrelated implementation effects.

The retraining results are especially informative. Perplexity recovers within roughly 250M tokens, but GSM8K lags far behind and remains well below models such as Sparse-Llama that were retrained much longer on different data. This shows that perplexity can overstate functional recovery. For Blackwell sparse FP4, the kernels run about 1.4x faster than dense NVFP4, but reasoning accuracy does not recover to a practically usable level at the tested budget.1a:T72b,The core proposal is the Spense format. Instead of forcing the entire weight matrix into standard 2:4 sparsity, the matrix is split into two contiguous regions: one remains dense, the other is pruned with the standard 2:4 pattern. The practical advantage is immediate: the dense region can be executed with existing dense GEMM libraries such as cuBLAS, and the sparse region with existing 2:4 sparse GEMM libraries such as cuSPARSELt. This avoids custom compiler work and avoids activation expansion or expensive runtime transformations used by prior relaxed-sparsity methods.

The reason this contiguous split is possible is the permutation invariance of the intermediate dimension in GLU-style MLPs. If the same permutation is applied consistently across the gate, up, and down projections, the MLP function is unchanged. That lets the method choose a subset of intermediate channels to keep dense, reorder them into a contiguous block, and prune the rest into the 2:4 region.

On top of this format, the paper proposes two one-shot pruning methods. SpenseGPT is the simple baseline: keep the first p% of intermediate indices dense and apply SparseGPT to prune the remaining region into 2:4 sparsity. SpenseGPT+ improves this by selecting dense indices using an activation-aware importance score inspired by Wanda, combining each intermediate index’s contribution across the gate, up, and down projections. SparseGPT is then used on the remaining weights to minimize layer output reconstruction error.

A central empirical point is that dense-index selection matters enormously. The paper reports that with the same dense budget, AIME can vary from 76.67 down to 8.89 depending on which indices are kept dense. So the method is not just about relaxing sparsity; it is about identifying the right subspace to preserve in dense form.1b:T4a6,The evaluation uses Qwen3-32B and Seed-OSS-36B, with benchmarks including AIME, GPQA, LiveCodeBench, and IFEval. The main claim is that SpenseGPT+ preserves quality across these tasks while achieving up to about 1.2× end-to-end decoding speedup over the dense baseline on B200 GPUs with FP8 precision in vLLM.

One of the most useful analyses is the sparse-GEMM speed study. The paper shows that sparse kernels only approach their theoretical benefit when the GEMM M dimension is large enough to amortize overhead. When M is small, sparse GEMM can even be slower than dense GEMM. This is an important reality check for autoregressive decoding, where many operations run in regimes that are not ideal for sparse kernels.
