18:T714,Dustin is a sparse verification framework specialized for speculative decoding. The problem is formulated as selecting a fixed budget of k KV positions for a verification window [i, i+w-1], with the goal of preserving as much of the original attention mass as possible. To evaluate selection quality, the paper defines Attention Recovery Rate (ARR), which measures how much attention mass is retained by the selected KV subset. The appendix further shows that ARR is strongly negatively correlated with output-logit KL divergence, making it a useful proxy for sparse-forward fidelity. The method has two main components. The first is a hybrid token-selection policy that combines historical attention from the target model with lookahead attention from the draft model. Historical attention is cheap and reflects established dependencies, while draft lookahead provides information about future tokens inside the verification block and helps handle saliency shift. The second is a sparse estimation scheme designed to keep the selector itself cheap. Instead of materializing attention importance across all heads and layers, Dustin searches for a minimal subset of attention heads that is sufficient for accurate importance estimation. This is a crucial systems choice: any dynamic selector on the verification path must be cheaper than simply running dense attention over the full KV cache, otherwise compression is pointless. The paper also positions Dustin against several baselines. Static eviction permanently discards context and therefore struggles under saliency shift. Full dynamic rescoring is accurate but too expensive. Page-level approximations such as Quest avoid exact QK^T but still incur non-trivial scoring overhead and remain slower than Dustin’s sparse estimator in the authors’ analysis.19:T606,Experiments cover the Llama3 and Qwen2.5 families on LongBench and PG-19. The analysis sections mainly use Qwen2.5-32B as the target model and Qwen2.5-0.5B as the draft model, with LongReward as the analysis dataset, whose average context length is about 13.5k tokens. The headline system result is up to 27.85x acceleration for self-attention computation and up to 9.17x decoding-phase speedup on Qwen2.5-72B at 32k context length and batch size 16, with only minimal accuracy loss. One of the most informative observations appears early: at 32k input length and batch size 16, verification can account for as much as 87.5% of total decoding latency. That explains why optimizing verification matters more than squeezing a bit more speed from the draft side. On the method side, the paper shows that historical attention alone is insufficient because speculative verification processes a future block of w tokens, so past saliency does not extrapolate reliably. Draft lookahead alone is also insufficient because the smaller model is imperfect at predicting which tokens the target model will care about. The hybrid policy gets closer to the oracle in ARR, and the ARR-to-logit-KL relationship supports ARR as a meaningful proxy. Another practically important result is negative: a naive dynamic selector that recomputes importance from all heads and layers becomes slower than full-cache self-attention beyond 4k tokens. Next, read the ARR definition and the evidence connecting ARR to logit KL, since ARR is the paper’s main analysis tool.
