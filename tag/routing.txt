18:T708,The paper first develops the exploration-dilemma argument. In a shared-expert MoE layer, every token always receives the shared-expert contribution, while only a subset of routed experts is selected conditionally. During pretraining, the shared branch tends to dominate the learned token representation because it captures common knowledge for all tokens. Once that happens, changing the routed experts often produces only limited variation in the final features. Alternative routes become harder to distinguish, so the router receives weaker signals for meaningful exploration.

At the same time, fine-grained expert segmentation increases both E and K, which explodes the number of possible expert subsets. The paper gives a concrete example: moving from (E, K) = (8, 1) to (64, 8) increases the routing space from 8 possibilities to about 4.4 × 10^9 combinations. So the architecture demands more strategic exploration exactly when shared experts make exploration less effective.

MirrorMoE is proposed to resolve this mismatch by adaptively shrinking the routed-expert exploration space on a per-token basis. The method is derived by mirroring routing logits through sign negation. The intuition is not to add more randomness, but to suppress routed experts adaptively so that the effective search space better matches the model’s exploration capability under shared-expert dominance. This is different from simply reducing K globally, injecting noise, or tweaking load-balancing losses. The paper frames MirrorMoE as a way to improve trainability and route discoverability, not as a way to increase theoretical expressiveness. It also claims a theoretical characterization of the adaptivity, suggesting the suppression behavior is tied to routing structure rather than being a heuristic knob.19:T76c,The experiments are built around a clear question: when does adding shared experts help, and when does it interfere with the benefits of larger K? The authors train matched MoEs with and without shared experts at four scales, from 100M to 2.4B total parameters. At each scale, they fix the total number of experts E and vary the number of activated experts K over {6, 8, 10}. This is a good stress test because larger K simultaneously increases expressiveness and enlarges the routing search space.

Three results stand out. First, the benefit of shared experts diminishes as K increases. In some settings, shared-expert MoEs even get worse when K becomes larger. That is the empirical signature of the exploration dilemma: more combinatorial capacity does not translate into better pretraining because the model cannot explore the larger routing space effectively. Second, MirrorMoE consistently improves this regime. The clearest numbers given in the provided content are on the 2.4B model: +0.67 points in pretraining performance and +1.64 points on downstream performance. Third, MirrorMoE restores the expected gains from increasing K and allows shared-expert MoEs to consistently outperform their non-shared counterparts again. That is important because it suggests the issue is not shared experts per se, but the training mismatch created when shared experts are combined with a large routing space.

The excerpt does not include all ablations, but the introduction already indicates the key comparisons: shared vs non-shared, different K values, and with vs without MirrorMoE across multiple scales. When reading the full paper, the most important details to verify are whether the shared branch truly dominates representation learning, how MirrorMoE changes routing diversity and expert utilization, and whether the gains are concentrated in early training or persist throughout pretraining.1a:T640,Read this one in the order of phenomenon, mechanism, method, and validation. Start with the introduction and Figure 1. The paper’s case depends on a specific empirical observation: in shared-expert MoEs, increasing K can stop helping and may even hurt. If that trend is robust, the problem is worth taking seriously.

Then focus on the mechanism section. The most informative part of the paper is the argument that shared experts dominate token representations and therefore reduce the distinguishability of routed-expert choices. That is the key reason the two popular design trends can work against each other. After that, read the MirrorMoE definition carefully and pin down what “mirroring routing logits” actually does. The important comparison is not against dense baselines, but against simpler alternatives such as lowering K, adding exploration noise, or changing auxiliary balancing losses.

In the experiments, prioritize the cross-scale results and the trends over K. Two questions matter most: does the shared-expert baseline really degrade at larger K, and does MirrorMoE restore the expected monotonic benefit of larger K? If the paper includes routing statistics, entropy plots, or representation analyses, those are worth reading closely because they often provide stronger evidence for the claimed mechanism than final perplexity alone.

This is not an especially formula-heavy read. A 30-minute pass is enough to capture the main idea. If you want to judge whether it should affect your own MoE recipe, include the appendix and ablations and budget closer to 50–70 minutes.1b:T8ee,方法分成 TES 和 ACS 两条线。

TES，token-error supervision，在原生 router 之外增加一个轻量的 error head。这个 head 以共享的 token 表示为输入，为每个 expert 预测一个误差分数。然后用 native affinity 对这些 expert-wise error 做加权聚合，使聚合后的预测误差去拟合该 token 实际观察到的 next-token cross-entropy。训练好后，TES 不只是做一个辅助预测；它还在路由阶段把预测误差反馈到 expert 排序里：论文采用从 native affinity 中减去一个按比例缩放的 log-error 项，再做 top-K 选择。直观上，若某 expert 对当前 token 预测误差更高，就降低它被选中的相对优先级。这个设计保留了原有 top-K 稀疏预算，也不改 expert aggregation policy。

ACS，affinity-concentration supervision，则更克制。它不引入额外 head，也不在推理时改路由公式，而是直接用 realized token loss 去监督已选 expert 的归一化 affinity 概率的平方项。作者的解释是，这相当于根据 token loss 去调节路由分布该更尖锐还是更平坦：某些 token 上应更集中地押注少数 expert，另一些 token 上则不应过度集中。它是一个 headless 的对齐方式，参数和推理开销都更小。

监督目标上，论文主要用 Itakura–Saito divergence，也试了 ENLL。理论分析部分给出的关键结论是：对 TES 而言，两者都把聚合误差预测推向相同的期望目标，但对 LM 优化的梯度影响不同。若预测误差与真实误差一致，IS 不改变原始 CE 更新；ENLL 会相对放大低损失 token 的梯度权重。这个分析很重要，因为它说明“把 token error 接进 router”不是只有一个实现细节问题，而会改变训练动力学。

和已有方法相比，这篇工作不是重新设计一个全新 router，也不是增加更复杂的 expert selection 网络，而是在保留原生 MoE 结构和 top-K 预算的前提下，把 token-level error 作为显式监督接入路由。它的优点是兼容现有 backbone，改动小；局限是监督信号来自 realized CE，本身受当前模型状态影响，可能更适合 fine-tuning 或后期 router 校正，而未必直接适合早期大规模预训练。1c:T942,The method has two variants: TES and ACS.

TES, or token-error supervision, augments the native router with a lightweight error head. From the shared token representation, the head predicts an error score for each expert. These expert-wise predicted errors are then aggregated using the native affinity weights, and the aggregate is trained to match the realized next-token cross-entropy for that token. At inference and training time, TES does more than auxiliary prediction: it modifies expert ranking by subtracting a scaled log-error term from the native affinity before top-K selection. Intuitively, experts predicted to incur higher error are down-weighted in the routing decision. This preserves the original sparse execution budget and the architecture’s native expert aggregation policy.

ACS, or affinity-concentration supervision, is the lighter alternative. It adds no extra head and does not alter inference-time routing with an extra transformation. Instead, it directly supervises the squared probabilities of the normalized selected-expert affinities using the realized token loss. The effect is to encourage the routing distribution to become sharper or flatter depending on token difficulty. In other words, it shapes how concentrated the native router should be, without introducing a separate error predictor.

For supervision, the paper mainly uses Itakura-Saito (IS) divergence and also studies ENLL. The theoretical section is important. For TES, both objectives target the same expected token-error quantity, but they induce different optimization dynamics. When predicted and observed token error match, IS leaves the original CE update unchanged, whereas ENLL relatively upweights lower-loss tokens. That is a meaningful distinction: adding token-error supervision is not merely an auxiliary loss choice, but a change to how gradients are distributed.

Relative to prior MoE routing work, the contribution is not a new router architecture. It is a way to keep the native MoE design and top-K budget intact while explicitly supervising routing with token-level error. That makes the proposal easy to graft onto existing backbones. The likely limitation is that realized CE is itself model-state dependent, so the method may be better suited to fine-tuning or late-stage router correction than to the earliest phases of large-scale pretraining.1d:T525,Experiments are run on two backbones, Granite 3.1 and OLMoE-1B, across four multiple-choice QA benchmarks.
