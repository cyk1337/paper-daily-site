9:T420,The central mechanism is ResearchSpec, a compact executable specification of what the final report must establish. Multiple Planning Writers independently search and read sources, then propose candidate structures. A Planning Judge consolidates them, a Critic looks for missing questions and cases, and a Reviser folds the feedback back into the specification. ResearchSpec stores each section’s scope, research questions, required entities or cases, provisional source leads, and hierarchical section IDs. Validators check ID uniqueness, parent-child consistency, consecutive ordering, and required fields before dispatch. Each Researcher then receives the full ResearchSpec plus one section assignment, conducts further evidence gathering in an independent context, and writes a complete section. The assembled draft is reviewed by a Global Editor, while Local Editors apply targeted revisions to affected sections. The design trades some flexibility for controllability: the plan is refined before dispatch and then held fixed during section research.a:T848,The design is intentionally simple in machinery and careful in construction. Each task is a domain-specific quantitative research assignment represented as an 8x8 matrix. Rows correspond to entities or objects of study, columns to analytical dimensions, and each cell is an independently graded quantitative conclusion. This matrix formulation turns a broad research task into many local scoring units while preserving the structure of a realistic analyst workflow.

The benchmark encodes difficulty through three data properties: massive evidence collection, cross-source reconciliation, and long-horizon derivation. These are operationalized through four capability families. Retrieval measures evidence collection. Derivation measures multi-step quantitative composition. Reasoning captures broader analytical composition. Calibration measures conflict handling, abstention under missing evidence, and resistance to fabricated precision. This decomposition is useful because it separates “could not find the source” from “found the source but failed to compute or reconcile correctly.”

The most important design choice is the reference and grading pipeline. Each reference answer comes with a derivation record and a source-provenance record. Sources are assigned one of four disclosure-based levels and cross-checked when possible. Scoring is rule-based at the cell level rather than delegated to a free-form judge model. That reduces evaluator variance and makes the benchmark easier to audit, which is especially important for open-web research tasks where evidence quality and source hierarchy matter.

For evaluation, the authors try to control for host-level confounds. They test nine frontier models through two CLI environments, disable native search/browsing, and force all models to use the benchmark-provided search, page-visit, and PDF-fetch tools. This makes the comparison more about model-plus-agent behavior under a shared tool interface than about product-specific browsing stacks. The paper also includes failure-mode annotation and case-level disagreement analysis to study specialization.b:T55a,The headline result is that the benchmark remains hard for current frontier systems. Across nine evaluated models, the best score is only 33.37% and the weakest is 16.79%, which indicates substantial remaining headroom.

Three findings matter most. First, retrieval is not the main bottleneck. Retrieval failures account for only about 12% to 14% of errors, while Derivation and Calibration failures together exceed 70%. In other words, many systems can locate relevant evidence but still fail to reconcile source discrepancies, normalize definitions, or complete the required multi-step calculations. Second, strong and weak models fail differently. Stronger models are more often limited by incomplete derivation, reported at 31%, meaning they get part of the chain right but do not finish it reliably. Weaker models more often exhibit hallucinated precision, reported at 38%, meaning they output precise-looking numbers even when evidence is missing or inconsistent. Third, the models show genuine specialization rather than a simple monotonic ranking. The paper reports case-level correlation of only rho = 0.61 and disagreement up to 18.8 percentage points on individual cases.

From a research perspective, the most useful result is not the leaderboard itself.
