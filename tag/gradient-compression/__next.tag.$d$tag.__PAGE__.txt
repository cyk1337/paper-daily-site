b:T73f,The method has three layers. The first is operator-level theory. The paper separates Transformer components into linear and nonlinear operators, and studies what happens when the input activation of an operator is compressed. The key result is that if the compressor is unbiased, then for a linear operator the induced gradient error is itself unbiased and does not propagate further upstream to earlier operators. For nonlinear operators, neither property holds. This distinction is the central theoretical contribution because it turns a vague engineering intuition into a concrete rule for module selection. The second layer is model-level convergence analysis. The authors study mini-batch SGD when all linear operators use activation compression, derive an upper bound on gradient variance, and decompose it into two additive terms: standard mini-batch sampling variance and compression-induced variance. The compression term is a sum over operators, controlled by the second-order magnitude of activation errors, so the total increase remains manageable rather than exploding layer by layer. Under the standard L-smoothness assumption, they further show that after the same number of updates, the stationarity metric degrades by at most a factor tied to the fourth root of the cumulative compression error. The third layer is the algorithm. The key observation is that once a linear operator's activation is low-rank, its weight gradient is naturally low-rank as well. The proposed method therefore jointly compresses activations and gradients by reusing the low-rank factors of compressed activations to maintain factorized weight gradients throughout training. This reduces both activation and gradient memory without introducing additional gradient error beyond what activation compression already causes, and with little to no extra computation.c:T51f,The experiments cover both Qwen and LLaMA families, and include pretraining datasets as well as fine-tuning datasets, which makes the evaluation broader than work limited to LoRA or SFT. The empirical study is organized around three questions. First, does the theory match practice: is compression on linear operators much safer than compression on nonlinear ones? Second, does joint activation-plus-gradient compression provide better memory savings than activation compression alone? Third, how does the method compare with other memory-efficient training approaches in the accuracy–compression trade-off? The reported conclusion is that the empirical results align well with the theory: compressing linear operators causes mild degradation, compressing nonlinear operators is much riskier, and the joint method achieves substantial memory savings while remaining competitive in model quality. The provided excerpt does not include the final numeric tables, so it would be unsafe to state exact compression ratios, throughput changes, or accuracy deltas.
