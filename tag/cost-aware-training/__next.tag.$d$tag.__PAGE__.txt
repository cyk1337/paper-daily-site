a:T7b6,DUET is a dual-model, two-stage inference framework. Given an input x, a capable large model M first produces an intermediate representation z, and a lightweight model m then generates the final answer y conditioned on both x and z. In the main instantiation, z is a reasoning signal: the large model handles the hard cognitive work, while the small model performs cheaper answer generation.

The key point is that DUET is not just a sequential pipeline. The paper formulates training as a constrained optimization problem: maximize expected reward while keeping the expected length of z below a budget B. This directly ties collaboration quality to inference cost. Training uses a length-penalized joint objective with alternating optimization over the two models.

Two ingredients appear central. First, the marginal-utility reward gives positive incentive to the large model only when its intermediate output is likely to improve outcomes beyond what the small model could do alone. This discourages the large model from emitting long generic chains of thought and pushes z toward concise, decision-relevant information. In other words, the objective rewards useful compression, not verbosity.

Second, the adaptive length-penalty schedule stabilizes training under a token budget. A fixed penalty can be too weak, leading to long z, or too strong, collapsing the communication channel before the models learn to cooperate. The adaptive schedule gradually tightens the budget while preserving learnability.

Relative to standard CoT distillation, DUET is different in an important way: the small model is not trained to imitate the full reasoning trace of the large model. Instead, the large model learns to emit a shorter communication signal that is sufficient for the small model to finish the task. It is also different from speculative decoding or verifier-proposer setups, because the two models are not playing a draft-check role; they are dividing semantic labor.b:T67f,The evaluation focuses on difficult reasoning benchmarks such as AIME and GPQA. The main reported result is that DUET matches the large-model baseline performance while reducing expensive large-model token usage by up to about 60%. The gain comes from replacing long large-model reasoning traces with a shorter large-model signal plus cheaper small-model completion.

The motivation is empirically grounded: reasoning models often generate much longer outputs than similarly sized instruct models, with examples in the paper citing 5–10x longer responses on AIME-like tasks. DUET is designed to exploit that redundancy. The most relevant experiments are likely comparisons against the standalone large model, the standalone small model, and simpler compression or short-CoT baselines, along with cost-performance tradeoff curves under different length penalties and ablations on the reward design.

The provided text does not include the full result tables, so exact benchmark-by-benchmark numbers beyond the headline reduction are not visible here. Still, the experimental message is clear: the method is not trying to raise the capability ceiling, but to reallocate reasoning compute more efficiently on hard tasks.

A practical caveat remains important. Real deployment gains depend not only on token savings but also on the overhead of running two models serially and on how tightly the length of z is controlled. If z stays long or the orchestration cost is high, wall-clock savings may fall short of token-level savings. The paper appears aware of this, which is why the token budget is built directly into training rather than enforced only at inference.c:T48e,For a quick read, start with the introduction and the main framework figure.
