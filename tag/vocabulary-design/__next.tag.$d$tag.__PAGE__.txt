9:T7c5,The formulation starts from minimum-token segmentation with a vocabulary budget. Each document is represented as a byte-level DAG whose edges are candidate substring occurrences. Byte edges are always available; multibyte edges are available only if the corresponding string is selected into the vocabulary. Given a vocabulary, minimum-count encoding is just a shortest path in the usable subgraph. The coupling difficulty is that selecting one token type enables all of its occurrences across the corpus.

The main idea is an occurrence-level Lagrangian relaxation. Each candidate occurrence gets a non-negative price lambda_e. On the document side, segmentation becomes a shortest-path problem with edge costs increased by these prices. On the vocabulary side, each token type accumulates the total bid of all its priced occurrences, and the lower bound subtracts the sum of the top-K bids allowed by the vocabulary budget. This yields a computable lower bound without a generic LP solver: shortest paths per document plus a global top-K selection over token bids. The paper argues that maximizing over prices recovers the LP relaxation, while validity of the reported bound is checked separately with an exact integer checker.

A useful extension is the boundary-licence formulation. Instead of only comparing the two endpoints—fully regex-constrained versus fully unrestricted—the method allows a budget on how many vocabulary entries may cross regex cuts. That produces a compression frontier and makes the design question continuous: how much compression can be recovered by permitting a small number of cross-boundary tokens.

The paper also keeps compression and prediction separate. Compression is analyzed through certified token-count intervals. Prediction is evaluated on held-out data with an explicit vocabulary and decoding protocol, because unrestricted vocabularies change token frequencies, context granularity, and effective predictability, not just sequence length.a:T52e,The headline result on English Wikipedia is a certified boundary tax: regex boundaries increase the optimal token count by 28.3% to 36.8%. That is a large effect, showing that pre-tokenization boundaries materially constrain the compression optimum. BPE is only 2.1% above the constrained lower bound, which supports the claim that under a fixed regex regime BPE is already close to the best achievable compression. But BPE is 10.9% above the unrestricted lower bound, which explains why boundary-relaxing methods can still obtain substantially shorter sequences. These are not contradictory findings; they refer to different optimization problems.

The multilingual study covers 12 languages. Unrestricted fitted dictionaries compress better, but they have higher mean test bits-per-byte in every language. With independent tuning and evaluation seeds, the direction remains in 11 of 12 languages. This is the most practically relevant result for LM researchers: fewer tokens do not necessarily mean easier prediction or lower held-out loss.

The boundary-licence experiments are also important, even if the abstract only hints at them.
