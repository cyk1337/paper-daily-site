1a:T4c5,A quantifiable Memorization Window is defined from two complementary perspectives: 1) Retention window, measured by the loss retention gap between the first and subsequent encounters of a sample, which marks the number of additional training tokens after which the model forgets the sample; 2) Generalization window, measured by downstream performance, which marks the training horizon after which continued training on the sample leads to overfitting. The effective memorization window is the minimum of these two values, representing the safe interval after which a sample can be reintroduced to the training stream. Based on this signal, Memorization-guided Data Reuse (MDR) continuously tracks per-sample loss retention dynamics during training, adaptively scheduling repeat exposures only when the interval since last exposure exceeds the memorization window, rather than using fixed epoch schedules. A core empirical finding is that when repeat intervals exceed the memorization window, the slope of validation loss decrease from repeated tokens is indistinguishable from that of fresh tokens, with no diminishing marginal returns; the only hard performance ceiling is set by the intrinsic diversity of the dataset.1b:T4ba,Experiments are conducted on LLM pre-training setups ranging from 1B to 7B parameters. Key findings include: 1) Memorization window length varies with training stage and data quality: windows are shorter early in training and longer later; windows for high-quality data are 2-3x longer than for low-quality data. 2) Adaptive scheduling based on memorization windows allows safe reuse of high-quality data for 8-12 epochs (compared to the conventional 4-epoch heuristic) without overfitting, delivering 3-4 percentage point higher average downstream performance than fixed 4-epoch training. 3) When repeat intervals match the memorization window, the learning efficiency of repeated tokens is within 5% of fresh tokens, with nearly identical loss reduction per token. 4) Compared to baselines including globally shuffled multi-epoch training and fixed repetition ratios, MDR delivers 0.08-0.12 lower validation loss and 2-5 percentage point higher average downstream performance under the same total training token budget, with no observed overfitting. Ablations show memorization window length scales positively with model parameter count and total training tokens processed, and negatively with learning rate.1c:T857,The paper has two technical threads. The first is regularization. The authors revisit the apparent advantage of diffusion-style masked training over autoregressive training in data-constrained settings and ask whether that advantage partly reflects under-regularized AR baselines. To isolate the effect, they apply the AR-tuned strong weight decay to diffusion language models as well, and find that dLLMs improve substantially under the same stronger regularization. Once both families are strongly regularized, their validation losses become much closer. This is an important control: it separates the effect of masking from the effect of insufficient regularization.

They then introduce masked-input regularization, or MIR, as a minimal augmentation of standard autoregressive pretraining. The model keeps the usual next-token prediction loss on clean sequences, but also optimizes an auxiliary next-token prediction loss on randomly masked versions of the same inputs. The architecture and inference procedure remain unchanged; only training is modified. The extra cost is additional training compute, which the paper treats as acceptable because the target regime is compute-rich but data-limited. MIR is therefore positioned as a data-efficiency technique rather than a compute-efficiency technique.

The second thread is scaling-law modeling. The authors compare several candidate forms and show that additive Chinchilla-style laws fit poorly in this regime. The issue is structural: if loss decomposes additively into a model term plus a data term, then the loss difference between two data budgets should not depend on model size. Their experiments contradict that prediction. To address this, they propose SoftQ, a five-parameter scaling law with a soft bottleneck coupling between model size and data size, motivated by a skill-learning view of scaling. The idea is that when unique data is scarce, extra model capacity cannot be fully utilized, and conversely the value of more data depends on whether the model is large enough to absorb additional skills. A coupled law is therefore more appropriate than an additive one.1d:T561,The empirical study spans models from 72M to 1.4B parameters and uses DCLM and Stack-V2 in data-constrained pretraining setups. The regularization results have two main layers. First, diffusion language models also benefit substantially from the same strong weight decay tuned for AR models, which shows that heavy weight decay is a general requirement of this regime rather than an AR-specific patch. Second, MIR consistently improves validation loss even on top of strongly regularized AR baselines, indicating that random masking contributes complementary regularization rather than merely substituting for weight decay. At 1.4B parameters, the gains carry over downstream, including +10.2 points on BoolQ and +2.2 points on SciQ.

For scaling, the study expands to five model sizes and four unique-data budgets, then fits multiple scaling-law families. SoftQ outperforms additive Chinchilla, Quanta, and Muennighoff-style laws both in-sample and out-of-sample. The same ranking also holds on an independent dataset from Kim et al. (2026b), which strengthens the claim that the result is not an artifact of one corpus. Using SoftQ as the baseline law, the paper estimates that MIR is worth roughly 1.3x more unique training data at the 200M-400M token budgets. That framing is especially useful because it converts a training modification into an equivalent data multiplier.1e:T4d4,For a quick but meaningful read, start with Figure 1. Panel (a) shows whether MIR adds value beyond strong weight decay across scales; panel (b) shows why the authors claim that Chinchilla is structurally wrong in this regime rather than simply underfit. Then read the problem setup carefully: the paper is explicitly about fixed unique data with multiple epochs, not the standard abundant-data single-pass setting. In the method section, focus on the MIR objective and how the clean-input and masked-input losses are combined, including the masking scheme and training mixture. In the experiments, prioritize three checks: whether strong weight decay matters equally for AR and dLLM training, whether MIR remains effective across model sizes and data budgets, and how large the out-of-sample prediction gap is between SoftQ and the additive baselines. A 30-45 minute skim can cover the introduction, method, Figure 1, the main result tables, and the scaling-law plots.
