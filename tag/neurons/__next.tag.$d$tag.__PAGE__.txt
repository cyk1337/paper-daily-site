b:T7a9,The study uses three base Llama models: Llama-3-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, and Llama-3.2-1B. The model choice is deliberate: their tokenizers encode every integer from 0 to 999 as a single token, which avoids confounds from multi-token number representations in neuron-level analysis. The authors build equivalent arithmetic tasks in three formats: symbolic expressions, word problems, and Python code.

They then identify arithmetic heuristic neurons with a two-stage pipeline. First, attribution patching is used to find candidate layers and neurons that contribute strongly to correct arithmetic outputs. Second, activation patching is applied to test causal relevance, filtering out neurons that are merely correlated with arithmetic behavior. This is an important methodological choice: attribution alone would not distinguish true arithmetic contributors from bystanders.

The analysis then compares neuron sets across formats and performs cross-format interventions. Neurons discovered in one format are ablated or patched in another format to test whether their causal role transfers. The paper does not stop at overlap statistics. It separates shared identity from shared function, showing that a compact subset of neurons recurs across all three formats and maps onto previously reported heuristic classes such as range, modulo, pattern, identical-operands, and multi-result heuristics.

The central interpretation is that LLMs maintain a reusable arithmetic heuristic library, but different formats modulate how strongly and how reliably that library is activated. Symbolic inputs tend to trigger the circuit more directly, while text and code depend more on upstream abstraction and operand routing. Relative to earlier work, the contribution is not just “there exist arithmetic neurons,” but “the same arithmetic neurons can generalize across surface forms, and failures often come from activation-state differences rather than circuit replacement.”c:T784,The main paper focuses on Llama-3-8B, with qualitatively similar results for 3B and 1B in the appendix. The experimental findings fall into four buckets.

First, cross-format sharing. The authors identify a relatively compact set of neurons that consistently show high attribution and causal importance across symbolic, text, and code arithmetic. These neurons are concentrated in middle-to-late layers, in line with prior arithmetic-neuron studies. The exact overlap numbers are not visible in the provided excerpt, but the qualitative conclusion is clear: the shared subset is substantial enough to explain a nontrivial portion of arithmetic behavior.

Second, ablation evidence. Removing these shared neurons degrades arithmetic performance across all three formats while leaving non-arithmetic behavior comparatively less affected. This argues against the idea that they are merely format-specific lexical features. More importantly, neurons identified in one format can causally affect performance in another, which supports transfer of function across forms.

Third, activation-state analysis. On failure cases, the relevant neurons are often not absent; instead, they are weakly activated, activated at the wrong time, or combined differently with other heuristics. This is the strongest evidence for the paper’s main claim: shared circuit, different activation states.

Fourth, scale consistency. The same qualitative pattern appears in 1B, 3B, and 8B models. That makes the result less likely to be an artifact of one checkpoint or one scale regime, and suggests that form-invariant arithmetic heuristics may emerge relatively early and persist with scale.

The practical message is simple: a drop in arithmetic accuracy under reformatting does not necessarily mean the model lacks a unified arithmetic mechanism.
