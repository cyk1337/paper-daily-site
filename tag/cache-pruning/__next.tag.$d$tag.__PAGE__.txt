9:T88d,The core method is DepthKV, a framework that allocates a fixed global KV budget non-uniformly across transformer layers. In a standard decoder-only transformer with L layers and input length N, each layer stores N key-value pairs, leading to O(LNd) memory. Most prior post-training pruning methods keep the same retention ratio in every layer, effectively setting the per-layer budget B^(l) to be uniform. DepthKV relaxes that assumption and allows different layers to receive different budgets under a fixed total memory constraint.

The paper first establishes the need for this design through layer-wise ablation. It prunes one layer at a time while keeping the others unchanged, then measures the downstream degradation. A permutation test is used to reject the null hypothesis that all layers are equally important. This is an important part of the paper because it turns the method from a heuristic into a response to a measurable structural property.

On top of that, DepthKV supports several allocation strategies. From the visible text, these include: (1) position-dependent protection, where certain depth regions such as middle layers are preserved more aggressively; (2) metric-guided allocation, where layers with higher importance scores receive larger budgets; and (3) hybrid strategies that combine positional priors with metric-based ranking.

Pruning itself is performed during the prefill stage, with token importance estimated from full-sequence attention weights. So the method does not require retraining or architectural changes. It can be viewed as an additional optimization layer on top of existing KV-pruning pipelines: keep the token selector, but stop assuming that every layer deserves the same cache budget.

A particularly interesting analytical contribution is the notion of content amplification layers. The paper observes that pruning the most sensitive layers leads to shorter and less informative outputs. That suggests these layers are not merely storing redundant context but are important for sustaining content generation. This makes the paper more than a deployment trick; it also offers a useful lens on layer function in long-context generation.a:T679,The visible excerpt does not include the full result tables, so exact scores, model sizes, and compression ratios should not be invented. First, the evaluation covers long-context tasks such as document summarization, question answering, and mathematical reasoning. The paper explicitly focuses on settings where inputs are much longer than outputs, making prefill the dominant compute and memory bottleneck. That is the right regime for studying prefill-time KV pruning.

Second, the layer-importance analysis is central. By pruning one layer at a time, the authors show that performance degradation varies substantially across layers. Third, the paper analyzes generation behavior and finds that pruning highly sensitive layers causes outputs to become shorter and less informative. These layers are termed content amplification layers, and their sensitivity correlates strongly with downstream performance.

Fourth, the main result is that under the same total KV budget, DepthKV consistently outperforms uniform pruning across tasks and models. The important point is not a single best-case number but the consistency of the gain under matched memory constraints.
