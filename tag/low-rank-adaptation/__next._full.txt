17:T81f,The training recipe itself is simple: instead of fully updating the LLM during vision-language alignment, the authors insert low-rank modules on the LLM side and compare several operator families, including LoRA, LoHa, and LoKr, across ranks, model scales, encoders, and training settings. The real contribution is the analysis stack.

First, they study behavior. Low-rank alignment consistently shifts the model from a more hallucinatory decision style to a more conservative one, reducing overconfident mistakes while preserving calibrated uncertainty. This is not just a benchmark win; the paper explicitly analyzes behavioral tendencies on perception and hallucination-oriented evaluations.

Second, they analyze token-level features. The paper identifies what it calls the LS-curse: full-parameter alignment disrupts the linear separability of some visual tokens, whereas low-rank alignment better preserves the token-wise knowledge structure inherited from the pretrained vision tower. The interpretation is that full alignment encourages premature entity-level mixing, while low-rank adaptation enforces a more progressive fusion process.

Third, they provide geometric evidence. Using fine-grained and coarse-grained probes, angular coverage analysis, and manifold visualization, they show that full alignment produces more diverse and more widely spread token features, consistent with aggressive fusion. Low-rank alignment keeps the representation space more homogeneous and structurally conservative.

Fourth, they add a theoretical account. Theorem 3.1 shows that low-rank gradient flow induces a noise-weighted smoothing effect, favoring updates along directions that are robust to stochastic variation and therefore biased toward flatter regions of the loss landscape. Theorem 3.2 shows that the steady-state distribution of low-rank parameters concentrates on flat-gradient, noise-robust subspaces. Together, these results explain why low-rank alignment tends to preserve general structure instead of quickly absorbing sample-specific or entity-specific couplings.18:T730,The experimental coverage is broad. The paper reports more than 55 alignment settings, and over 100 evaluated configurations once benchmark dimensions are expanded. Model scales range from 1.4B to 14B, including systems from MobileLLaMA-1.4B to Qwen3-14B. Vision encoders include CLIP, DINOv2, and SigLIPv2. The study also varies rank, operator family, vision-tower depth, learning-rate schedule, low-rank placement, and whether the vision encoder is frozen.

The main empirical result is consistent: low-rank alignment outperforms full-parameter alignment on most benchmarks while reducing training cost, and this trend holds across model scales. The excerpt provided does not include the full main-table numbers, but several stable findings are clear:
1. Perception-heavy benchmarks are sensitive to rank. Increasing rank yields notable gains on metrics such as MME-perception and POPE, especially for larger models like the 13B setting, suggesting that cross-modal interface capacity matters there.
2. Knowledge-heavy or task-specific benchmarks such as GQA, ScienceQA, and TextVQA do not benefit as much from larger rank, implying that their bottleneck lies elsewhere, likely in data coverage, instruction tuning, or knowledge access rather than alignment capacity.
3. The trend is robust across LoRA, LoHa, and LoKr, which argues that the benefit is not an artifact of one PEFT implementation but a property of low-rank constraints more generally.
4. Probe and geometry experiments support the central claim: full alignment is more likely to damage token-level linear separability, while low-rank alignment better preserves inherited visual structure.
