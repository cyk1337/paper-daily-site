18:T78d,The method has four stages.

1) Direction estimation. For each layer, the model collects hidden states from reflective and non-reflective examples and computes a standard mean-difference vector as an initial reflection direction. This follows the usual activation-engineering assumption that a behavior can be approximated by a linear direction in residual space.

2) Direction purification. This is the key technical step. The authors argue that the raw contrast direction mixes reflection with activation structure shared by reasoning states more broadly. They therefore apply PCA-based denoising to reduce sample noise and low-value variation, then orthogonalize the candidate reflection direction against a rank-one proxy for shared reasoning structure. A central empirical claim is that the raw direction has substantial overlap with this shared proxy, while the orthogonalized direction reduces that overlap to roughly an isotropic-random baseline.

3) Layer calibration. The method does not assume every layer is a good intervention site. On a small calibration set, it sweeps candidate layers and intervention strengths, then keeps the layers that consistently reduce reflection-like behavior without destabilizing generation quality.

4) State-dependent projection attenuation. Instead of adding or subtracting a fixed vector at every token, the method removes only the component of the current hidden state that lies along the learned reflection direction, with bounded magnitude. This makes the intervention conditional on how much reflection-associated signal is present in the current token state. Compared with constant shifts, this is a more selective suppression mechanism and is less likely to damage ordinary reasoning.

The design choice is deliberate: no retraining, no controller network, no modified decoding. The trade-off is that it needs a small reflective/non-reflective contrast set and a calibration pass.19:T6e1,The evaluation covers three open-weight Qwen-family checkpoints on two benchmarks, MATH-500 and GPQA-Diamond, with matched answer parsing and token accounting. The main comparison is against two closely related representation-level baselines, CREST and ReflCtrl.

Across six model-benchmark settings, Reflection Steering reduces reasoning tokens by 16.9% on average while largely preserving task performance. The cleanest result is on Qwen3-30B-A3B for MATH-500: a 21.8% token reduction with only a -0.1 point change in accuracy. The paper also reports paired equivalence testing and finds support for equivalence to the raw model within a ±1 point margin, which is stronger evidence than simply reporting a small average drop.

The paper’s own framing makes clear that the strongest accuracy-preserving evidence is on mathematical reasoning. On GPQA-Diamond, the token savings remain meaningful, but the trade-off is less clean. That makes sense: in math, reflection often appears as repeated verification after the solution path is already established, whereas in scientific reasoning, revision and reconsideration may be more tightly coupled to useful inference.

The ablations that matter are: (i) raw mean-difference direction versus purified direction, showing that orthogonalization against shared reasoning structure materially improves the accuracy-efficiency trade-off; (ii) fixed vector shifts versus projection attenuation, where the latter better preserves accuracy; and (iii) layer calibration, showing that intervention quality depends strongly on depth. This is not a paper about raising benchmark accuracy. It is about recovering tokens without paying much accuracy tax, and the right way to judge it is by the cleanliness of that trade-off.1a:T793,The paper has two main components. First is CorrGap, a metric for inter-dimension dependence. Instead of measuring only the overall correlation between judge scores and human scores on a target dimension, CorrGap asks whether the judge becomes less accurate exactly on examples where the target dimension differs strongly from the other dimensions. The authors convert human scores within each dimension into percentiles, which avoids artifacts from different score distributions across dimensions. For each text and target dimension, they compute the gap between the target-dimension percentile and the mean percentile of the non-target dimensions. Small-gap examples form a low-variance group; large-gap examples form a high-variance group. If the judge evaluates the target dimension independently, its correlation with human labels should be similar across the two groups. If it leaks information from non-target dimensions, the high-variance group should suffer more, because those are precisely the cases where dimensions disagree. CorrGap is defined from this correlation difference, with additional care to control for target-score distribution so that the metric does not simply reflect example difficulty.

The second component is DimCheck, an inference-time intervention over chain-of-thought. The judge first produces reasoning for the target dimension. A checking step then identifies evidence in that reasoning that is unrelated to the target dimension, such as comments about fluency when scoring factuality. Those irrelevant pieces are iteratively removed, and the model re-evaluates the text conditioned on the cleaned reasoning. The key idea is to constrain the evidence basis of the score, rather than only changing the instruction prompt. The paper also explores using smaller models to approximate the larger-model checking component, which is important because the method otherwise adds nontrivial inference overhead.1b:T7ca,The setup is built around Qwen3-8B as a hybrid model. The same base model can be decoded in think mode, where it generates reasoning before deciding, or in no-think mode, where it emits a direct action token. This allows a relatively controlled comparison between a reasoning policy and a direct classifier without changing the model family. The task is binary intervention timing: given a prefix of a multi-party conversation, decide whether an assistant should speak now or remain silent. The dataset contains about 16,000 synthetic conversations expanded into roughly 173,000 token-level decision points, with only about 13% positive intervention labels, so the class imbalance is severe. The paper compares several training routes. One is direct decision-token SFT, which tends to produce a stronger classifier but suppresses reasoning. Another is a masked objective that preserves the model’s native reasoning, but only on cases the model already solves correctly; this means it receives little useful signal on failure cases and therefore struggles to improve beyond the base reasoning policy. A third route is think-mode RL, optimizing either accuracy or a behavioral faithfulness reward. The authors point out a key limitation of group-relative RL objectives: when all sampled rollouts agree, there is no learning signal, even if they all agree on the wrong answer. That leaves many confidently wrong prompts untouched. For auditing, the paper combines activation probes with behavioral interventions. Probes test when the decision becomes decodable in the hidden states, while interventions remove or replace parts of the generated reasoning to see whether the final action changes, which is a more causal test of whether reasoning matters. The paper also argues that several common metrics are misleading in this setting, including probability-based contribution scores, probe accuracy that ignores class imbalance, probing only at the end of reasoning, and uncontrolled flip tests.1c:T613,The main empirical result is that the strongest action policy is the direct classifier that does not explicitly generate reasoning, while the reasoning policy is more auditable but less accurate, with the largest cost coming from missed interventions. That is the central capability–auditability tradeoff. In training experiments, decision-token SFT further strengthens the direct policy but pushes reasoning toward irrelevance. The masked objective cannot substantially beat the base reasoning policy because it only preserves reasoning on examples the model already gets right. Think-mode RL also fails to clearly surpass the base reasoning policy, not because RL is useless in general, but because the group-relative objective gives no gradient on prompts where all sampled rollouts agree, including confidently wrong ones. The audit results are also informative. For the reasoning policy, the eventual decision becomes decodable during the chain of thought, whereas for the direct classifier the decision is already decodable at the prompt stage. That supports the interpretation that the direct policy commits early, while the reasoning policy at least partially forms the decision during reasoning. The authors also stress that single-run comparisons can confuse seed variance with reward-objective effects, so multiple seeds or replicates are necessary when comparing RL variants.
