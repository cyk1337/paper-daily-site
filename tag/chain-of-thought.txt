18:T487,ReDiST (Refinement and Distillation of Student Traces) is a student-conditioned trace construction and distillation framework.

Pipeline:
1) Start from the target student’s own drafts.
2) Generate edited candidates via two branches:
- Revision: for answer-correct drafts, rewrite under (Q, evidence E, original draft) to enrich evidence links and intermediate relations without changing the answer.
- Repair: for failed drafts, rewrite with additional access to the verified answer Y, producing an evidence-consistent derivation that supports Y.
3) Candidate selection with a frozen student-matched reader: given (Q, E, candidate trace), the reader provides signals such as answer recovery and verified-answer probability. These are used to screen and rank candidates, tying selection to what the student can actually use/learn.
4) Distill into the student with explicit controls:
- repair exposure controls how often accepted repairs appear during training;
- an auxiliary answer loss controls the balance between final-answer prediction and trace imitation.
Construction/selection is offline; inference uses only the distilled student (no rewrite/reader).19:T5b1,The method is simple but well targeted. The authors extend the CoT-Control benchmark with Self-Instructed CoT Controllability. Instead of only placing the constraint in the user prompt, they place it at the beginning of the chain of thought itself, as a self-instruction that the rest of the reasoning should follow. They use two elicitation routes. The first is reasoning prefill: when the reasoning channel is accessible, they directly prefill the CoT with a sentence like “I should avoid using word X in my reasoning” or “I should reason in format Y,” and let the model continue. The second is bootstrap: they ask the model, from the user side, to begin its reasoning by restating a self-reminder, after which it proceeds normally. This makes the method usable even when direct reasoning prefill is unavailable. A key control experiment replaces specific constraints with a generic reminder to follow the user’s CoT-related instruction. If that alone improves compliance, then the bottleneck is not inability but low default priority assigned to user instructions about CoT. The evaluation spans QA and agent tasks, reusing CoT-Control tasks, constraints, and graders, while adding both hard and soft metrics. Hard metrics require full compliance; soft metrics capture partial compliance and are more informative about directional control. The later sections test multi-turn agent settings and monitor-evasion attacks built on self-instruction.1a:T4e7,The headline result is consistent across settings: self-instruction substantially increases CoT controllability. Across five open-weight reasoning models, nine constraints, and both QA and agent benchmarks, the gains are large. The paper highlights DeepSeek-R1 improving from 0.4% to 22.2% and Qwen3-32B from 2.5% to 34.3% under the hard metric. Soft metrics show even larger gains, suggesting that many failures in the user-instructed setting are not absolute incapacity but weak or partial adaptation. Another important result is that even a generic reminder inside the CoT materially improves compliance, supporting the claim that models deprioritize user instructions about CoT rather than fail to execute them. The bootstrap variant also works on closed models, including Claude models, which shows the effect is not limited to open reasoning channels. In multi-turn agent tasks, self-instruction can persist across turns and further improve control. The safety result is the most operationally important one: in a realistic CoT-monitoring setup, the authors design self-instructed attacks that reduce the monitor’s catch rate, indicating that improved CoT controllability can be used for monitor evasion rather than just benign formatting control.1b:T425,For a skim, focus on three parts. First, read the introduction and method sections carefully enough to understand why User-Instructed and Self-Instructed CoT are not equivalent evaluations. Second, inspect Figure 1 and Figure 3, since they carry the intuitive example and the main quantitative result. Third, look at the soft-metric table, because it clarifies whether the gain is a binary jump or a broader shift toward partial compliance. This is roughly a 30-minute read if the goal is to extract the main claim. Two sections deserve extra attention if time allows. One is the bootstrap setup on closed models, because it reveals whether models treat their own generated CoT prefix as a high-priority control signal. The other is the monitor-evasion section, which is where the paper moves from evaluation nuance to concrete safety implications. A useful question to keep in mind while reading is what current RLVR-style training is actually teaching: robust reasoning, or the ability to emit editable intermediate text that supports a rewarded final answer.1c:T52a,The method has three stages. First, a parser maps each natural-language step into an operation plus a canonical payload under a task-specific schema. The schema defines entities, predicates, allowed operations, initial state, and goal. The operation set contains seven labels: Derive, Assume, Verify, Prune, Confirm, Restate, and Meta. The payload is wording-invariant, so semantically equivalent statements map to the same atom. The parser is implemented with Qwen3.8-27B and uses the current step, local labeled context, and prior schema elements. Second, a deterministic engine replays the parsed operations and incrementally builds a reasoning-state tree. It resolves each step to the node it depends on and applies the corresponding mutation. Because this stage is deterministic, the same parse always yields the same tree and labels. Third, failures are defined as predicates over the current step, the resolved node, and the prior tree state. This lets the three broad failure modes—repetition, underthinking, and unsupported deduction—be refined into subtypes tied to concrete earlier states. The main methodological difference from prior work is that ARBOR separates semantic content from textual form, then turns failure detection from a local classification problem into a state-transition relation problem.1d:T66d,The framework is instantiated on three tasks with different reasoning structures: ZebraLogic for constraint satisfaction, 24 Game for arithmetic search, and PlanBench for sequential planning with preconditions. A key prerequisite is parser quality, and the paper reports parser accuracy above 0.89 on adjudicated reference labels for every task. The main empirical findings are threefold. First, ARBOR detects failures more accurately than local signals such as n-gram or embedding similarity for repetition, switch-token heuristics for underthinking, and step-level correctness judges. Those local methods recover only a small fraction of the failures flagged by ARBOR, and many of their positives are ordinary reasoning steps. ARBOR’s subtype counts also separate correct from incorrect trajectories better than all local signals combined. Second, failure subtypes differ in cost. Unsupported deductions are most damaging to final correctness because later verification rarely catches them; underthinking mainly wastes search budget and tokens, since the model often still reaches the answer by another path or by returning later; repetition has the smallest effect on correctness and relatively limited extra token cost. Third, the precursors of failures live in the tree state, not just in local operation labels. Features such as branch content, whether a node has been verified, and recent operation history are predictive of subtype occurrence, while operation labels alone only recover a subset of that signal. Notable methodological note: their “Paraphrasing” intervention is not meaning-preserving in practice; it flips answers about twice as often as Adding Mistakes, which matters for anyone reusing this evaluation protocol.21:T825,The key move is to formulate the gap between reverse CoT and verified forward CoT as a translation problem rather than a prompting problem.

Let q be the question and a the correct answer. Forward CoT (FCG) samples reasoning from the teacher conditioned only on q. Rejection sampling (RS) keeps only those forward samples whose extracted final answer matches a. Reverse CoT (RCG) instead conditions the teacher on both q and a. The authors argue that the student should ideally learn from the RS distribution, not from raw RCG outputs.

To do this, they build a small paired dataset. For each question, they sample one reverse CoT response x, then sample forward CoTs until they obtain the first accepted response z with the correct final answer. This yields paired examples (x, z). A translator model G_phi is then fine-tuned with teacher forcing to predict z from x.

A crucial design choice is that the translator only receives the reverse CoT text x, not the original question q or answer a. This serves two purposes. First, it matches the paper’s distributional derivation, where the goal is to map source responses to target responses. Second, it blocks a direct question-answer shortcut and forces the model to learn how to rewrite answer-conditioned rationales into outputs closer to accepted forward reasoning.

Once trained, the translator is used at scale: each new question requires one reverse teacher call to produce x and one translator call to produce z-hat, and the student is then trained on (q, z-hat). Compared with RS, which may need many forward teacher calls per accepted sample, this pipeline compresses the expensive part into one cheap teacher sample plus one translation step.

Relative to prior semantic suppression or structural-guidance methods, the difference is conceptual. Those methods still generate under answer access and try to reduce leakage. CoTranslator instead learns an explicit distributional correction from RCG-style outputs toward RS-style outputs. The appendix further provides a distribution-recovery analysis to justify this framing.22:T609,The experiments cover six student models and six math benchmarks, with transfer tests to a new math dataset, a different teacher model, and STEM problems.

The first headline result is quality. For students such as Qwen3-4B-Base, CoTranslator improves average accuracy over semantic suppression by up to 5.82 percentage points. That indicates that merely hiding answer cues is not enough; explicit translation toward the verified forward distribution works better.

The second result is the cost-performance trade-off. The two 4B students retain 95.0% of the average accuracy of rejection sampling while using only 35.9% of its estimated compute. This is the central empirical claim of the paper. RS remains the cleaner target, but it is expensive; CoTranslator uses a small paired set to train a translator and then amortizes that cost over large-scale data generation.

The third result is transfer. Without retraining, the translator generalizes to a new math dataset, a different teacher, and STEM tasks. Average STEM gains are 4.88 points for 4B students and 4.31 points for 8B students. This suggests the translator is learning a reusable transformation from answer-conditioned rationales to more question-grounded ones, rather than memorizing a dataset-specific template.

The paper also includes analysis showing that translated outputs are closer to RS responses and exhibit fewer answer-dependent shortcuts than raw reverse CoTs.
