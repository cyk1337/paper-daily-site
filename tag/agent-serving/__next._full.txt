1b:T65f,The paper does not introduce a new compaction algorithm. Instead, it systematically adapts and compares two existing sequence-level compaction families in the online agent setting. The first is token eviction (TE): given a set of proxy queries, score cached positions by their induced attention mass and keep only the highest-scoring original K/V entries. The second is attention matching (AM): use similar key selection, then additionally fit an additive attention bias and reconstructed values so that the compacted cache better matches the full-cache attention outputs on the proxy queries.

The central contribution is the treatment of proxy-query sources. The paper compares several signals that are actually available online: boundary queries around the end of the current turn, lightweight repeat-prefill style proxies, and deferred future-generation queries obtained from the agent’s own subsequent generation. The trade-off is clear. Immediate compaction has lower latency but relies on weak proxies. Delayed compaction uses more realistic future queries and therefore preserves more useful information, but it requires holding the uncompressed cache a bit longer.

Methodologically, the important part is not the TE/AM math itself but the online compaction protocol: compact completed turns inside the agent loop, constrain the extra compute budget, and study the interaction among proxy source, compaction delay, and compaction ratio. This is the right framing for deployment, because the real objective is not offline reconstruction quality but the joint trade-off among latency, memory, throughput, and task success.1c:T474,Experiments are run on BrowseComp-Plus and WideSearch with multiple scales of Qwen3.5 and Gemma-4. The main empirical result is clear from the provided text: immediate compaction often hurts task performance, while delayed compaction using the agent’s own later-generation queries consistently recovers much of the lost accuracy. On Qwen3.5-27B and Gemma-4-31B, delayed compaction at a ratio of 0.2 preserves most of the no-compaction accuracy while reducing peak KV footprint by up to 3.5x and 2.7x, and improving serving throughput by up to 4.2x and 1.7x, respectively.

A second important result is the robustness of TE. Even though AM is more expressive and should in principle better approximate full-cache attention, TE remains highly competitive under imperfect proxies, limited budgets, and online timing constraints. That suggests the dominant source of error is not compactor capacity but proxy-query mismatch. The most useful ablations to inspect are the ranking of proxy sources, the delay-versus-memory trade-off, the crossover between TE and AM at different compaction ratios, and whether model scale changes the conclusion.1d:T532,The central proposal is an agent runtime layer inserted between the framework and the engine. This layer owns the cross-cutting metadata neither neighbor has on its own. It exposes four policy-agnostic primitives: observe, to ingest events from either side; score, to return priorities for ranking decisions; predict, to forecast future agent activity; and act, to trigger off-critical-path side effects such as prefetching or warming. The paper frames these as an observe→score→predict→act loop, with agent identity as the shared key.

The main instantiation is CacheSage for KV caching. The idea is that multi-agent workloads often reuse a recurring system-plus-tools prefix across sessions, but reactive caches evict those blocks before the next relevant session arrives. CacheSage learns an online first-order Markov transition matrix over agents within a workload. That model is used in two ways: for score, it estimates survival probability to guide eviction; for predict plus act, it triggers cross-session prefetch when a future agent call is likely. The design keeps state bounded, at no more than 20 KB per workload, and falls back gracefully to LRU when the learned signal is weak.
