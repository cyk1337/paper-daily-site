18:T745,The method has three main components. First, example selection. EVAN estimates scaled image information gain (scaled-IIG) for each training instance, measuring how much the image contributes to answer correctness. Only visually dependent examples are selected for intervention, which avoids injecting noise into cases that can already be solved from language alone. To prevent the selected pool from collapsing into a narrow subset of tasks, the paper uses a mixed-ranking strategy that balances visual dependence with data diversity.

Second, evidence ablation. For each selected example, the method identifies query-critical image patches that support the verified response. These patches are then masked to create an evidence-ablated image. The key design choice is that the perturbation is not coarse or task-agnostic; it removes exactly the visual evidence that keeps the answer grounded. Sampling from the current policy on this ablated image tends to produce responses that remain fluent and plausible, but are now visually ungrounded. These become EVAN’s hard negatives.

Third, RL optimization. The negatives are not optimized under the ablated image. Instead, they are brought back under the clean image condition, so the model is explicitly trained to suppress language-prior failures when the relevant evidence is actually available. The training recipe includes several stabilization mechanisms: filtering out low-probability or overly long ablated responses, bounding negative advantages so negatives do not dominate the update, and combining the method with DAPO-style clipping and dynamic sampling for stable long-form reasoning optimization. Compared with prior multimodal RL methods that use image perturbations, the main difference is that EVAN treats perturbation as a tool for constructing informative negatives, not as the end objective.19:T5fe,The experimental story is not just about end-task accuracy; it is also about grounding behavior. On a set of challenging multimodal reasoning benchmarks, EVAN improves over standard RLVR/GRPO baselines, with larger gains on subsets where visual evidence is more necessary. That pattern matches the method design: it is meant to correct language shortcuts, not to uniformly help every example.

The ablations are the most informative part. Removing scaled-IIG-based selection and applying evidence ablation to all samples hurts performance, showing that visually dependent example selection is necessary. Replacing query-critical masking with random masking or coarse perturbations produces weaker negatives and worse downstream gains, which supports the claim that negative quality matters more than perturbation quantity. The stability ablations are also important: without plausibility filtering or bounded negative advantages, training becomes less stable, indicating that the method is not simply “add negative rewards and hope for the best.”

From the available full-text excerpt, it is clear that the RL backbone is GRPO with DAPO for stable long-form optimization. However, the provided content is truncated before the full result tables, so exact benchmark-by-benchmark numbers, model sizes, and training-step details cannot be quoted reliably here. What is clear from the paper’s structure is that the gains are attributed primarily to better online negative construction rather than more data or a stronger base model.1a:T48d,For a quick read, start with the introduction and the method overview figure. The key idea is simple: EVAN is not “perturb images for contrastive training,” but “remove the visual evidence that matters, let the current policy reveal its language shortcut, then suppress that response under the clean image.”

Then focus on three parts. First, the definition of scaled-IIG and the mixed-ranking strategy; this explains why the method does not waste effort on examples that are not visually grounded to begin with. Second, the procedure for identifying query-critical patches and constructing evidence-ablated images; this is where negative quality is determined. Third, the RL objective and the stabilization details, especially response filtering and bounded negative advantages. Many RL ideas fail in practice because the optimization is brittle; this paper spends useful effort on that layer.

If time is limited, read the method section plus the ablation tables. That should take around 25–35 minutes. If the goal is to assess transferability to a broader multimodal post-training pipeline, also read the grounding analyses and failure-case discussion.1b:T7df,PIVOT can be summarized as online local correction followed by student continuation, plus KL supervision on the corrected span. During training, the student first generates a rollout prefix. At a chosen intervention point, the teacher produces a correction conditioned on that student-generated prefix. The student then continues generation from the corrected context rather than from its original mistaken continuation. This produces teacher-assisted trajectories that are more likely to end in verifier-approved solutions, while still preserving part of the student’s own rollout distribution. That is a key difference from simply injecting full teacher trajectories.

The optimization has two coupled parts. First, the resulting trajectories are still optimized with the standard RLVR objective, instantiated with GRPO and verifier-based rewards. This keeps the training target anchored to task correctness rather than pure imitation. Second, a teacher-student KL loss is applied only on the correction segments, providing dense token-level supervision exactly where the teacher intervened. Instead of distilling the entire sequence, the method concentrates supervision on the local regions that are most responsible for steering the trajectory back toward a valid reasoning path.

Mechanistically, the method addresses both bottlenecks at once. Online intervention changes the rollout distribution and increases access to reward-bearing trajectories. Local KL makes those interventions learnable by turning them into parameter updates the student can reproduce later. The paper further studies practical design choices: where intervention should happen, how much teacher guidance to inject, and how the KL term should be used. A recurring conclusion is that too-early intervention risks turning training into teacher-led generation, while too-late intervention cannot rescue already broken trajectories. Either intervention alone or token supervision alone is often insufficient; the combination is the point.1c:T63e,The evaluation covers two hard reasoning settings: logic games from SynLogic and mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with both in-distribution and out-of-distribution testing on the math side. The headline result is on hard logic games, where RL-only training makes limited progress: PIVOT improves mean avg@8 from 19.5% for the best baseline to 35.6%, close to a 2x gain. That is a meaningful result because it directly targets the sparse-reward regime where standard RLVR tends to stall.

The most informative ablations are not just score comparisons but mechanism tests. The authors isolate trajectory-level intervention and token-level supervision, showing that each one alone can still fail to produce strong learning. For each query, the policy samples a group of trajectories, each receives a binary outcome reward, and a trajectory advantage is computed. Instead of applying this advantage uniformly to all tokens, Self-Credit introduces three components.

First, during rollouts, the policy generates summaries with citations to earlier turns that contain critical evidence. The summaries compact the long interaction history, while the citations become self-generated credit signals at the turn level.

Second, intra-group rescaling refines these credits. For the same prompt, the method compares cited turns across successful and failed rollouts. Turns that are repeatedly cited in successful trajectories and less common in failed ones receive stronger credit. Turns that also appear frequently in failed trajectories are downweighted or stripped of credit. This makes the signal less dependent on trusting the model’s citations in isolation.

Third, advantage reweighting applies the rescaled credit to the RL loss. Tokens inside highly credited turns receive stronger advantages, while unrelated turns receive less reinforcement. These probe continuations are used only for measurement, not as additional policy-update trajectories. Third, it shrinks the sparse value estimates toward the GRPO group mean μ_g to reduce variance.
