18:T549,The model uses a normalized Transformer backbone. It keeps the nGPT residual-update rule and adds L2 normalization for concatenated gated attention outputs before the output projection and for SwiGLU activations before the MLP down-projection, implemented with fused Triton kernels. The 72 physical layers are partitioned into a 16-layer input block, a 40-layer shared recurrent block, and a 16-layer output block. The input and output blocks run once; the recurrent block runs k times, and the input-block representation is re-injected before each loop. Attention uses gated grouped-query attention with 16 query heads and 4 KV groups. The stack interleaves 36 MLP-only layers, 27 sliding-window attention layers, and 9 full-attention layers, giving a 3:1 local/global attention pattern. Training starts with a k=1 non-looped phase on about 4.5T tokens using NVFP4. A BF16 continuation phase then samples loop counts from {1,2,3,4} and trains with truncated backpropagation through loops. The model is later extended to 256K context. Post-training uses nine SFT epochs over packed 256K-token sequences, followed by 200 asynchronous GRPO steps across 23 environments. The RLVR stage uses a difficulty-aware loop curriculum: rollout workers run at fixed loop counts, and prompts with lower profiled pass rates are preferentially assigned to deeper loops.19:T49a,The reported pretraining scale is about 6T tokens, with an additional long-context extension phase of about 300B tokens. The NVFP4 phase gives roughly 20% end-to-end training speedup over BF16 while keeping BF16 model weights and FP32 optimizer master weights, Adam moments, gradient accumulation, and reductions. On the equal-weight average of 12 benchmarks, performance rises from 35.2 at one loop to 39.7 at two loops and 40.6 at four loops. On four agentic benchmarks, four loops reduce generated tokens per attempt by 1.3 to 2.5 times relative to one loop, suggesting that extra latent compute can partly substitute for longer textual traces. The RLVR loop curriculum has its largest effect at deeper loops; Terminal-Bench v2.1 increases from 14.0% to 21.4% in the reported ablation. At four loops, Dory reaches 61.8% on SWE-Bench Verified versus 57.4% for Gemma 4 26B-A4B, and 967 Elo on GDPval-AA v2 versus 752. The paper also evaluates last-loop KV caching, where each loop overwrites the previous loop’s cache so attention memory stays at the one-loop size. Relative to per-loop caching, scores move between -2.1 and +0.4 points, with nearly unchanged output lengths.1a:T745,The method has three main components. First, example selection. EVAN estimates scaled image information gain (scaled-IIG) for each training instance, measuring how much the image contributes to answer correctness. Only visually dependent examples are selected for intervention, which avoids injecting noise into cases that can already be solved from language alone. To prevent the selected pool from collapsing into a narrow subset of tasks, the paper uses a mixed-ranking strategy that balances visual dependence with data diversity.

Second, evidence ablation. For each selected example, the method identifies query-critical image patches that support the verified response. These patches are then masked to create an evidence-ablated image. The key design choice is that the perturbation is not coarse or task-agnostic; it removes exactly the visual evidence that keeps the answer grounded. Sampling from the current policy on this ablated image tends to produce responses that remain fluent and plausible, but are now visually ungrounded. These become EVAN’s hard negatives.

Third, RL optimization. The negatives are not optimized under the ablated image. Instead, they are brought back under the clean image condition, so the model is explicitly trained to suppress language-prior failures when the relevant evidence is actually available. The training recipe includes several stabilization mechanisms: filtering out low-probability or overly long ablated responses, bounding negative advantages so negatives do not dominate the update, and combining the method with DAPO-style clipping and dynamic sampling for stable long-form reasoning optimization. Compared with prior multimodal RL methods that use image perturbations, the main difference is that EVAN treats perturbation as a tool for constructing informative negatives, not as the end objective.1b:T5fe,The experimental story is not just about end-task accuracy; it is also about grounding behavior. On a set of challenging multimodal reasoning benchmarks, EVAN improves over standard RLVR/GRPO baselines, with larger gains on subsets where visual evidence is more necessary. That pattern matches the method design: it is meant to correct language shortcuts, not to uniformly help every example.

The ablations are the most informative part. Removing scaled-IIG-based selection and applying evidence ablation to all samples hurts performance, showing that visually dependent example selection is necessary. Replacing query-critical masking with random masking or coarse perturbations produces weaker negatives and worse downstream gains, which supports the claim that negative quality matters more than perturbation quantity. The stability ablations are also important: without plausibility filtering or bounded negative advantages, training becomes less stable, indicating that the method is not simply “add negative rewards and hope for the best.”

If the goal is to assess transferability to a broader multimodal post-training pipeline, also read the grounding analyses and failure-case discussion.1d:T7df,PIVOT can be summarized as online local correction followed by student continuation, plus KL supervision on the corrected span. During training, the student first generates a rollout prefix. At a chosen intervention point, the teacher produces a correction conditioned on that student-generated prefix. The student then continues generation from the corrected context rather than from its original mistaken continuation. This produces teacher-assisted trajectories that are more likely to end in verifier-approved solutions, while still preserving part of the student’s own rollout distribution. That is a key difference from simply injecting full teacher trajectories.

The optimization has two coupled parts. First, the resulting trajectories are still optimized with the standard RLVR objective, instantiated with GRPO and verifier-based rewards. This keeps the training target anchored to task correctness rather than pure imitation. Second, a teacher-student KL loss is applied only on the correction segments, providing dense token-level supervision exactly where the teacher intervened. Instead of distilling the entire sequence, the method concentrates supervision on the local regions that are most responsible for steering the trajectory back toward a valid reasoning path.

Mechanistically, the method addresses both bottlenecks at once. Online intervention changes the rollout distribution and increases access to reward-bearing trajectories. Local KL makes those interventions learnable by turning them into parameter updates the student can reproduce later. The paper further studies practical design choices: where intervention should happen, how much teacher guidance to inject, and how the KL term should be used. A recurring conclusion is that too-early intervention risks turning training into teacher-led generation, while too-late intervention cannot rescue already broken trajectories. Either intervention alone or token supervision alone is often insufficient; the combination is the point.1e:T63e,The evaluation covers two hard reasoning settings: logic games from SynLogic and mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with both in-distribution and out-of-distribution testing on the math side. The headline result is on hard logic games, where RL-only training makes limited progress: PIVOT improves mean avg@8 from 19.5% for the best baseline to 35.6%, close to a 2x gain. That is a meaningful result because it directly targets the sparse-reward regime where standard RLVR tends to stall.

The most informative ablations are not just score comparisons but mechanism tests. The authors isolate trajectory-level intervention and token-level supervision, showing that each one alone can still fail to produce strong learning. Their combination gives the strongest gains, which directly supports the paper’s framing. They also study intervention placement and the KL design, showing that teacher correction is not simply “more is better.” The intervention has to occur at a point where the student can still continue productively, while being early enough to change the probability of eventual success. On mathematical reasoning tasks, PIVOT consistently beats the evaluated baselines on both ID and OOD benchmarks, suggesting that the gains are not limited to a single synthetic logic environment.

The strongest part of the empirical section is the controlled decomposition of why the method works. The paper does more than report a better final number; it shows that changing the trajectory distribution and densifying the local learning signal solve different pieces of the RLVR failure mode.1f:T5b8,For a quick read, start with the introduction and the main method figure.
