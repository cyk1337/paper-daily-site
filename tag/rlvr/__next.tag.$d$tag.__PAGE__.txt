9:T886,The method has a data component and a training component. For data, Video-HopChain does not simply concatenate single-hop video questions. Each example is a dependency chain of 3 to 6 hops. Every hop is a yes/no question about one or two moments in the same video, and each answer maps to one of two integers. The final answer is the sum across hops, which makes the task exactly verifiable by exact match. Two design choices matter. First, every question includes at least one temporal-order hop, so a single frame cannot contain all required evidence. Second, some hops are selectors: the answer to an earlier hop determines which later moment should be inspected next, creating real inter-hop dependence rather than a bag of independent subquestions. The generation pipeline first produces shot-level captions with a VLM, then uses a text-only LLM to synthesize the multi-hop question from those captions. This is a deliberate trade-off: direct end-to-end VLM question generation is harder to control and harder to verify, while operating in caption space gives tighter control over logic structure and answer form.

For training, the paper introduces Confidence-Gated Exploration on top of GRPO. Standard GRPO derives its signal from reward variance within a group of rollouts for the same prompt. If all rewards in the group are identical, the group yields no relative advantage. CGE addresses this without drawing extra rollouts. With group size G=8, the first 4 rollouts are sampled normally. If those 4 are all correct or all incorrect, the remaining 4 are sampled with a top-token mask inside the reasoning span. Whenever the model assigns more than tau=0.95 probability mass to a single token, that token is temporarily removed and sampling is renormalized over the rest. This is not generic noise injection; it is targeted exploration only when the model is highly confident and the group is likely to collapse. Masked positions are excluded from the token-level loss, but all 8 rollouts still participate in the group advantage computation. The diagnosis centers on how the so-called Lucky Hit mechanism behaves under different answer spaces. Standard TTRL implicitly assumes that if the model samples multiple reasoning trajectories, the correct answer is more likely to become the majority and therefore serve as a useful pseudo-label. The authors argue that this assumption is much more plausible in large free-form answer spaces, where incorrect trajectories tend to disperse across many distinct strings. In a small multiple-choice space, however, different wrong rationales can collide on the same wrong option, creating a false consensus that is then reinforced by training. The model becomes more answer-consistent without becoming more correct.

Based on this diagnosis, the paper proposes PROSE: Process Reward Guided Self-Training. PROSE keeps the basic test-time RL loop—sample trajectories on unlabeled test questions, compute rewards, update the policy—but replaces majority-vote answer reward with process-level reward. A medical process reward model scores each reasoning step, and the trajectory reward is defined as the minimum step score rather than the mean. This design choice is central. Minimum aggregation prevents a trajectory from hiding one critical reasoning error behind many fluent or locally correct steps. If the weakest step is poor, the whole trajectory should not receive a high reward. The method also includes an answer-format guard to prevent reward hacking through malformed or strategically formatted outputs.

A practical aspect of the method is that the PRM is used only during adaptation, not at deployment-time inference. The goal is to internalize process supervision into the policy parameters rather than rely on an external reranker or verifier at test time. That distinguishes PROSE from many inference-time selection methods, which often improve quality at the cost of additional runtime modules and compute.15:T836,The paper first shows the failure mode of standard TTRL on medical multiple-choice QA: sample-level accuracy improves only slightly, majority-vote accuracy stays flat, and output diversity drops sharply. The method identifies positions where the expert disagrees with the student and applies distillation only on those tokens through a sparse mask. The intuition is simple: if the student already matches the expert, there is little value in forcing additional imitation; the useful signal lies where the expert would actually change the student’s local decision.

Training remains inside an on-policy RL loop. The base policy update uses a rubric-based GRPO-style objective on the student’s own rollouts. The distillation term is a token-level reverse-KL estimate, but its weight is no longer uniform. It is gated by verifier-based eligibility and by the disagreement mask. For a generated chain, define candidate boundaries. At each boundary, read the model’s current belief about the final answer. Score each segment by the amount of belief change between consecutive boundaries, and fork at the highest-scoring location. Two details matter.

First, the paper uses fork-before attribution. If belief changes sharply over segment (t-, t], the causal step is that segment, so the score is assigned to t- and sibling continuations are re-rolled from just before the belief-moving step. This aligns the counterfactual branch with the step whose contribution is being estimated.

Second, the paper instantiates belief readout at three access levels.

PROBE-JS is the black-box version. At each boundary, the model is interrupted with a short probe such as “so what is your final answer?”, and the resulting answer distribution is sampled. The score is the Jensen–Shannon divergence between consecutive boundary distributions. Large JS indicates that the segment caused a meaningful shift in answer belief. The advantage is that it only requires sampling access; the downside is extra generation at every boundary.

LENS-SHIFT is the white-box version. Instead of making the model answer explicitly, it uses a logit-lens style readout to measure how strongly each layer already commits to the final answer, producing a depth profile at each boundary. The score is the change in this profile across adjacent boundaries. The motivation is that belief shifts may appear as representational reorganization across depth, not only as a change in final logits. This avoids extra generation but requires hidden-state access and a target final answer for readout.

BSV, or Belief-Shift Vector, is an amortized readout. The authors fit an activation direction offline by contrasting high-confidence and doubtful states, then score a boundary by projecting its hidden state onto that direction. In the paper, BSV is mainly used for pre-RL validation: if one could measure belief shift more directly, would it align better with the Monte-Carlo value curve? So BSV functions more as an analysis instrument than the default online training method.

The distinction from prior selectors is sharp. Fixed grids, midpoints, and delimiter-based rules ignore whether a step actually changes the outcome. Entropy or surprisal tracks local token uncertainty, which is often inflated by paraphrase freedom, stylistic variation, or surface branching in code, without implying any change in final answer.
