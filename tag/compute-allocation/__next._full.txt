18:T90f,The paper proceeds in three steps. First is geometric approximation. Inputs are assumed to lie on a compact support with intrinsic dimension d. The target function is approximated by partitioning that support into K local cells and fitting simple local approximants. The model family is summarized by an effective partition budget K≈χN_e^γ, where χ captures partition efficiency and γ captures how expressivity grows with effective parameters. Combined with assumptions on smoothness, this yields a parameter-dependent approximation rate. A key point is that the parameter-scaling exponent is not primitive: it emerges from both the geometry of the support and the regularity of the target.

The second step is a spectral view of data scaling. Additional data reduce error only to the extent that they expose unresolved predictive mass. The relevant ingredients are the eigenvalue decay of the feature covariance, which directions are statistically visible at a given sample size, and how strongly the target aligns with those directions. If the target is concentrated in high-variance, easy-to-estimate modes, data scaling is favorable. If the remaining signal lives in the spectral tail, marginal returns from more data shrink quickly. This is close in spirit to spectral bias analyses in kernel or linearized settings, but the paper uses it as an explanatory lens for empirical data-scaling laws.

The third step is constrained optimization. With dense-training cost modeled as C≈κND, the paper combines parameter-limited and data-limited error terms and derives the optimal balance between N and D under a fixed training budget. It then extends the discussion to training-vs.-inference trade-offs. The treatment of test-time scaling is careful: gains from more samples are separated from gains due to improving the quality of each sample, which matters when interpreting pass@k-style improvements.

Two additional themes are worth noting. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that power laws only hold on connected positive-resource intervals; small-data plateaus and near-floor regimes should not be forced into a single slope. It also discusses extrapolation uncertainty explicitly: floor, amplitude, and slope are coupled, and the practical effect of slope error grows with log-distance from the pilot range.19:T603,SPLIT models success as:
P(success) = P(at least one correct candidate exists) × P(it is selected | it exists),
and explicitly models the second term (selection), not just coverage.

Section 3 derives exact success formulas for two selectors widely used:
1) Uniform selection among verifier-accepted candidates.
2) Argmax selection by verifier score.

The formulas incorporate verifier true/false accept rates and three correlation sources observed in practice:
- Heterogeneous task difficulty (per-task solve rates vary widely).
- Shared blind spots across generators, modeled via a Gaussian copula.
- Correlated repeated verifier calls for the same candidate, modeled via a candidate-level acceptance propensity.

Section 4 estimates these ingredients from a small labeled pilot using empirical Bayes, including variants where verifier accuracy depends on task difficulty (to avoid biased pooling).

A planner then searches over configurations—generator choices, sample counts, verifier calls, thresholds—under a cost budget to maximize predicted success. The paper also provides a regret guarantee tied to differential (not absolute) forecast error (Proposition 4), explaining why approximate calibration can still yield near-optimal allocations.

Trust emits the current evaluator-preferred answer, fallback emits a conservative answer that avoids further continuation, and expand buys one more unit of inference before the controller acts again. The same framework covers both width expansion and revision expansion.
For terminal actions, the target value is success probability minus compute cost. For expand, the ideal target is the Bellman continuation value. In practice, the system learns one confidence head per action: trust and fallback estimate terminal answer success, while expand estimates a rollout-based continuation-success surrogate.
