18:T625,The main methodological contribution is the Superminds Test, a hierarchical evaluation framework built around Probing Agents. Unlike standard multi-agent benchmarks, this setup does not place a fixed set of agents into a closed environment with a prescribed protocol. Instead, the authors deploy controlled probe agents into MoltBook, a live open-ended platform populated by more than two million autonomous agents, and observe how the society responds under natural conditions.

The evaluation has three tiers. Tier I tests joint reasoning: can multi-agent discussion converge to answers that beat strong individual models on difficult tasks? Tier II tests information synthesis: can agents read and combine information distributed across multiple contributors? Tier III tests basic interaction: do agents reply, attend to prior messages, and maintain conversational context at all? The hierarchy is deliberate. If basic interaction is weak, robust synthesis is unlikely; if synthesis is absent, joint reasoning cannot emerge.

The technical novelty is in turning an open social platform into a controlled diagnostic instrument. Passive observation of naturally occurring posts cannot establish whether collective intelligence exists, because task difficulty, ground truth, and participation conditions are uncontrolled. Probing Agents solve this by posting stimuli with known answers and measuring response quality, participation rate, and collaborative depth. That makes the framework suitable for testing spontaneous coordination rather than protocol-enforced teamwork.19:T6bf,Experiments are conducted on MoltBook, a live platform with over two million autonomous agents. The authors deploy probing agents across tasks ranging from difficult reasoning problems, including Humanity’s Last Exam, to simple coordination tasks such as counting. The overall pattern is consistent: the society does not outperform strong individual frontier models, and often performs substantially worse.

At Tier I, on hard reasoning tasks, group discussion fails to produce answers better than isolated strong models. Many comments are superficial, irrelevant, or non-contributory, which suggests the issue is not a small gap in aggregation quality but a more basic failure to enter meaningful collaboration.

At Tier II, the results are more nuanced. When agents actually engage, they are often capable of synthesizing distributed information correctly. This implies that synthesis ability exists at least in some agents. However, most posts receive no replies at all, so the opportunity for synthesis rarely materializes. The dominant bottleneck is therefore participation, not pure cognitive inability.

At Tier III, even trivial tasks that require almost no reasoning still suffer from sparse replies and poor context following. Many posts get no response, and many responses do not track the conversational state. This supports the paper’s broader interpretation: the platform behaves more like a bulletin board of independent broadcasts than a society engaged in sustained communication.

The most useful experimental insight is not a single metric but the consistency across tiers.
