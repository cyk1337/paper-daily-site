18:T4f8,The method is motivated by an empirical diagnostic: on student rollouts, correct answers show lower teacher–student KL on intermediate layer-to-layer transitions, with the strongest gaps for middle-to-late layer pairs, whereas final-layer agreement does not track correctness as well. OPTD augments OPD as follows:
- Keep the OPD objective: for each prefix visited by the student, compute token-level rewards from the teacher distribution (implemented over the student top-K candidates), corresponding to minimizing reverse KL from student to teacher on-policy.
- Add transition supervision: choose anchor layer pairs (a,b). For each candidate token v, compute a depthwise log-prob increment Δℓ(v; a→b)=log p_b(v|prefix) − log p_a(v|prefix). This captures both direction and magnitude of the teacher’s prediction revision across depth.
- Define a transition reward by comparing teacher vs student increments, and combine it with the standard final-layer OPD reward in a reward-weighted policy-gradient update.
Key distinction from prior work: it aligns predictive changes (logit/probability increments) rather than hidden representations, and turns layer-difference information (previously used mostly at decoding time) into a training-time distillation signal.19:T64b,BOSS is a training-free online policy rather than a new model. The main contribution is the budget-faithful decision framework. Each model call incurs prompt-plus-completion token cost. Generation calls use a cap L_gen, judge calls use L_judge, and before every call the policy checks worst-case feasibility against the remaining budget. This guarantees that no adaptive trajectory can exceed the hard per-instance cap. That accounting detail is central, because many prior systems either ignore selection overhead or count only completion tokens while omitting the repeated prompt cost of judge calls.

After each generated candidate, BOSS chooses among three actions: return, generate again, or adjudicate. The decision uses three ingredients. First, consensus-based termination stops early when the observed answers are already stable enough. Second, a novelty-based switch detects when additional sampling is no longer producing meaningfully new answer hypotheses, at which point the marginal value of generation drops. Third, budget-feasible pairwise judging spends the remaining budget on answer-level comparisons among candidate representatives.

The paper appears to avoid expensive full ranking over all traces. Instead, it relies on answer grouping, representative selection, and repeated randomized judging to approximate a good final choice under budget. A particularly strong design choice is the identical-candidate-pool control: the authors hold Stage-I candidates fixed and vary only the selector, which isolates the contribution of adjudication and allocation from candidate-generation quality.1a:T5be,The experiments cover two Llama-3.1 model sizes and four reasoning benchmarks. Under the common hard-cap setting, BOSS achieves the best accuracy in 22 of 24 model-task-budget cells. One representative result reported in the paper is at Llama-3.1-8B with a 4k-token budget: a development-tuned fixed generation-selection split reaches 59.3 macro average, BOSS reaches 60.5, and removing the novelty-triggered switch drops performance to 59.0. That suggests the online transition from generation to adjudication is a real source of gain rather than a minor heuristic.

The strongest evidence comes from the same-candidate-pool experiment. Using exactly the same Stage-I candidate pools, changing only the selector raises macro accuracy from 58.4 to 60.3 with randomized repeated judging, and to 60.5 with the full BOSS selector. This is important because it shows the improvement is not merely due to better candidate generation. Selection and allocation contribute independently.

The paper also reports comparisons against Adaptive-Consistency and TALE-EP under the same strict token ledger, realized-token analyses rather than nominal-budget-only reporting, transition statistics, hyperparameter sensitivity, and paired per-instance tests. Even from the visible full-text portion, the experimental design is clearly aimed at causal isolation: the authors try to separate the value of online allocation from stopping rules, candidate quality, and hidden token overhead.1b:T446,1) Task formulation: given a time-constrained query (e.g., “latest”), an agent iteratively reasons, issues search queries, accumulates timestamped documents, and must select temporally valid and reliable evidence to answer.
2) TrekTrace (core method): a plug-and-play failure attribution framework that inspects the search trajectory to determine whether answer-bearing evidence was retrieved, then reruns the model with that evidence to test whether it can answer correctly. This separates failures into pre-retrieval (bad query), retrieval (missing evidence), and post-retrieval (evidence present but still wrong). Post-retrieval is further split into distraction (misled by conflicting/irrelevant context) versus interpretation (fails even on gold evidence alone). It requires no fine-grained annotations and no retraining.
3) Benchmarks: TrekBench-Wild as a real-world meta-benchmark for evolving-knowledge reasoning; TrekBench-Ctrl as a controlled, reproducible benchmark with fixed contexts and systematically designed distractors spanning temporality, relevance, and trustworthiness.1c:T489,The method, LIFT, scores prompts with Partial Rollout Context Lift, or PRCL. For each prompt x, the initial policy greedily generates a deterministic rollout under the normal chat template, but only the first K tokens are used. A null context is then constructed by removing the user prompt while keeping the same system prompt and template. The same generated prefix is evaluated with teacher forcing under both contexts. PRCL sums the token-level log probability ratio between the prompted context and the null context. Intuitively, it measures how much the question makes the model’s own reasoning prefix more predictable. The prefix length K is selected from a random γ-fraction of the pool: the method scores prefixes up to Kmax and chooses the shortest length whose cumulative score reaches an α share of the total. The full pool is then ranked by PRCL at this selected K, and the top-B prompts are used for downstream RLVR. Compared with SHIFT, LIFT uses partial likelihood lift instead of hidden-state shifts from full trajectories. Compared with training-dependent selection, it does not require rewards, ground-truth answers, or an initial RL run.1d:T46b,The authors define a decision model as a single-pass model that maps state, question, and options to a probability distribution over options. They evaluate Jev 1.13.0 through its API and 23 open Jev-style configurations, including Qwen-based fine-tuned decision models, ordinary LLMs read through option-letter logits, and contrastive option scorers. The main contribution is diagnostic evaluation rather than a new model. The controlled benchmark varies one factor at a time: context length, target depth, number of options, and the number of state updates. Wrong options also appear in the context, so the model must actually read the relevant record. Tasks are separated into retrieval, selection, event lookup, and final-state tracking. The key idea is not to imitate any single peer solution. Instead, SRD measures how different same-problem peers consistently shift a frozen model’s token distribution relative to a shared control.

For a problem x, the current policy samples K trajectories. When training on a student-generated prefix, the method selects M same-problem peers and one cross-problem solution as a control. A frozen copy of the initial model predicts next-token distributions under two conditions: the student prefix plus a same-problem peer, and the student prefix plus the cross-problem control. Their difference is a peer-induced residual shift. SRD then aggregates these residuals conservatively: it keeps only coordinates whose direction agrees across peers, and sets the retained magnitude to the smallest supporting magnitude among those peers. This shared residual is added back to the frozen model’s distribution under the original student input, producing a teacher target. The student is trained with forward KL on that target.

Three design choices matter. First, the cross-problem control removes generic distribution shifts caused by simply appending a solution-like text, so the retained residual is more problem-specific. Second, the minimum-supported magnitude acts as a conservative intersection rule, reducing the influence of noisy or wrong peers. Third, peers are used only to construct the teacher during training; the student itself conditions only on the problem and its own prefix, so inference cost does not increase.

Compared with RLVR or GRPO, SRD needs no reward and no answer-level ranking. Compared with standard on-policy distillation, it does not rely on an external stronger teacher. Compared with prior on-policy self-distillation, it does not require reference answers.
