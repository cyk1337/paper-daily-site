19:T8c0,The technical core is the experimental design. Training data comes from Multilingual Reasoning Gym, which procedurally generates reasoning problems in 14 languages and supports adjustable difficulty. Instead of simply translating English datasets, the authors use an online curriculum: for each task, difficulty increases when more than 70% of the last 32 rollouts are correct and decreases when fewer than 10% are correct. This matters because the same model can have very different capability baselines across languages, so fixed difficulty would confound language effects with reward availability. They also pre-filter tasks using Qwen3-14B-Base at easy difficulty, removing tasks with pass@8 = 0, and exclude heavily English-dependent tasks from training while keeping them for evaluation. The resulting setup uses 62 training tasks and 30 held-out tasks for OOD evaluation.

Training uses a DAPO-style GRPO objective without KL penalty. Each run uses 500 steps, learning rate 1e-6, 64 prompts per step, and 8 rollouts per prompt. Rewards consist of a binary correctness reward plus two auxiliary rewards, each weighted at half of correctness: a format reward and a reasoning-language reward. The reasoning-language reward is instantiated either as English reasoning or native-language reasoning. A key implementation detail is that auxiliary rewards are only granted when the final answer is correct, which prevents the model from gaming language or format rewards at the expense of solving the problem. The paper notes that preliminary runs in low-resource languages could otherwise collapse or overfit to formatting.

The model suite spans Qwen, Gemma, and SmolLM families, including both base and post-trained variants from 1B to 8B parameters. The authors run monolingual GRPO for 11 MGSM languages and one multilingual run with uniform language sampling. Italian, Korean, and Portuguese are fully held out as unseen languages. For model-language pairs with sufficient initial support for the target language, they additionally train native-reward variants. The point of this design is to disentangle training language, rewarded reasoning language, and model pretraining background rather than to optimize a single benchmark score.1a:T6a8,The most robust findings are fourfold. First, native-language reasoning rewards usually trail English-reasoning rewards by only a small margin on average; there is no universal large penalty for asking the model to reason in the prompt language. Second, cross-lingual transfer is strong: GRPO in one language often improves performance in many others, suggesting that RL is transferring problem-solving behavior rather than only surface-form language patterns. Third, the transfer is not monotonic. The paper identifies a regression failure mode where training in a particular language improves some evaluations but causes substantial drops on unseen languages or tasks. These regressions are highly model- and language-specific. Fourth, multilingual training is not always better than the best monolingual run, but it often provides a more stable compromise and is especially relevant for held-out-language generalization.

Evaluation covers seen tasks, 30 unseen tasks, MGSM, and PolyMath. The abstract does not expose the full numeric tables, but the full paper makes clear that the conclusions come from a broad comparison over 9 models, 11 training languages, monolingual versus multilingual runs, and English versus native reasoning rewards. For a pretraining researcher, the more useful part is the analysis style: the authors do not stop at average scores, but inspect transfer matrices across languages and compare how different model families react to the same training language. That supports a practical conclusion: the main bottleneck in multilingual RL is not merely data availability, but the interaction among initial language competence, reward attainability, and policy drift during RL.1b:T60e,CLR is a training-free two-stage framework. In Stage 1, the model samples K full solution traces. Each trace must output not only a final answer but also exactly M decision-critical claims: concise intermediate propositions whose failure would undermine the answer. These are meant to be semantic anchors such as key constraints, transformations, maximality conditions, or intermediate conclusions, not generic summaries or answer restatements. The purpose is to compress a long reasoning trace into a small set of high-information, checkable propositions. In Stage 2, the same model is not asked to solve the problem again. Instead, it receives only the original problem and the extracted claims, and it actively searches for contradictions, counterexamples, missing conditions, factual mistakes, or unsupported inferences. The central design choice is falsification rather than proof. There is a useful asymmetry here: constructing a fully correct solution is hard, but invalidating a wrong trace only requires finding one decisive flaw. After verification, CLR maps claim-level verdicts into a nonlinear trace-level reliability score and aggregates answer groups by total reliability. This allows a reliable minority to overturn an incorrect majority. Relative to self-consistency, the method reallocates part of the budget from generating more traces to verifying the semantic anchors of existing traces. Relative to step-level verification, it avoids exhaustive checking and does not require process supervision or a separately trained verifier.1c:T4ca,The evaluation spans four LLMs and multiple difficult reasoning benchmarks, with comparisons made under matched model-call budgets. The budget accounting is clean: CLR@K uses K generation calls plus K verification calls, matching the 2K generation calls of Cons@2K. Two headline results are especially informative. On HMMT25, Gemma-4-12B-it improves from 76.67% with Cons@64 to 88.75% with CLR@32, a gain of 12.08 points under the same call budget. On CMIMC25, GPT-OSS-20B improves from 77.50% with Cons@64 to 82.19% with CLR@32 while using 37.0% fewer generated tokens, and it outperforms Pass@1 by 27.15 points. The paper’s stronger point is not just accuracy but token efficiency: Stage 2 verifies compressed claims instead of regenerating long chains, so the method can improve both correctness and compute efficiency. The qualitative analysis argues that whole-trace scoring is easily dominated by long stretches of routine reasoning, whereas claim-level falsification exposes decisive errors more directly. The most relevant ablations are likely around the fixed number of claims, whether the original trace should be hidden during verification, and why nonlinear reliability mapping works better than simple counting.1d:T4b1,The method is built around the notion of consilience: a conclusion is reliable when the model implicitly recognizes multiple plausible approaches and still converges to the same answer.

Confidence signal:
- Token-level confidence is computed from the top-K distribution at each step (mean negative log-prob over top-K tokens). Sharper distributions imply higher confidence.
- Baseline VF-TTS selects the rollout with the highest average confidence over the whole sequence.

Failure analysis:
- On easy/medium problems, correct traces tend to have higher average confidence.
- On hard subsets (e.g., where the model’s accuracy is <20%), the confidence distributions for correct vs incorrect traces become inseparable or even reversed, making confidence maximization unreliable.

Consilience metric:
- Instead of maximizing confidence uniformly, it enforces temporal asymmetry: penalize high confidence early (to avoid premature commitment) while rewarding high confidence late (to ensure convergence).
- Practically, sample multiple traces, compute the consilience score for each, and select the top-ranked trace. No external verifier and no additional training are required; only logits are needed.1e:T5a7,The main experiments use LLaDA-8B-Instruct. On GSM8K, pure decoding produces answer-only collapse on more than one third of problems with a 256-token canvas, and up to about 90% with a 512-token canvas. The final answer is typically committed in the first quarter of the denoising trajectory, while the median reasoning token is committed near the halfway point. A fixed 2×2 design crosses prompt type, reasoning requested versus answer-only, with decoder type, pure versus semi-AR. On 250 paired problems, the prompt–decoder interaction is about 35 percentage points with permutation p<1e-4. In the answer-only condition, the two decoders are statistically close. When CoT is requested, semi-AR gains about 31 points, while pure decoding gains only a small amount. The same pattern is replicated on Dream-7B and MATH-500; on MATH-500, collapse is smaller, but the order channel remains. Window restriction recovers most of the gap: all w≤64 improve pure decoding from 0.528 to roughly 0.82–0.85, close to semi-AR. A sliding window of 32 matches a block size of 32 within about one point, indicating that restricted reachability, rather than block structure itself, is the active mechanism. The paper also measures the cost: with full refinement, w=1 is best; at around eight committed tokens per step, the constraint can become harmful. In the useful regime, the gated decoder keeps near-AR accuracy with a reported 4× wall-clock speedup.1f:T584,CVPO has two components.
(1) Trajectory level: Value-Variance Adaptation. A value model estimates token-level V(s_t) along each generated trajectory. The variance of these values (or a closely related advantage-variance proxy) is used as an intrinsic randomness / exploration indicator: high variance implies more exploratory, low variance implies rigid generation. The paper sketches a bound showing the policy-gradient norm can be upper-bounded by a term involving advantage variance under a bounded ∥∇ log π∥ assumption, motivating variance-aware control of update magnitude. Based on this, CVPO modifies advantage computation by scaling/reweighting trajectories according to their value-variance, with different handling for different reward types (e.g., sparse correctness vs denser rewards), aiming to extract more informative gradients from otherwise homogeneous outcomes.

Compared to GRPO, CVPO is explicitly value-based and goes beyond group reward averaging by injecting a variance signal into advantage shaping.
