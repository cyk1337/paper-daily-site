18:T8c1,方法设计很克制，核心是“保留标准 student update，只在严格筛选的条件下做 student-to-teacher adaptation”。训练仍然从 student on-policy rollout 开始：student 只看题目 x 采样解答 y，teacher 在同一条 partial rollout 上、但额外看到参考解 y*，输出逐 token 分布。标准 OPSD 部分不变，student 继续用 teacher 的 token-level target 做蒸馏更新。VISTA 新增的是 teacher 更新，而且只在两层筛选后发生。

第一层是 rollout-level gating。只有当 student rollout 通过 outcome verifier 时，这条 rollout 才被认为包含了值得 teacher 学习的 problem-only reasoning 轨迹。这里的判断不是“student 比 teacher 强”，而是“这条 rollout 至少在结果上成立，因此其中某些 student token 偏好可能比 reference-conditioned teacher 更贴近部署时可行的推理分布”。这样做避免把 teacher 拉向明显错误的 student 行为。

第二层是 token-level selection。即便 rollout 最终正确，也不意味着整条序列的每个位置都该拿来更新 teacher。论文用 teacher-student disagreement 的 KL 信号做位置选择，只在 top-k 个分歧最大的 token 位置上，把 teacher 向 student 适度拉近。这里的直觉很重要：真正需要修正的是 teacher 最不贴近 problem-only reasoning 的位置，而不是所有位置。若全序列都更新，teacher 很快会塌缩到 student，失去 privileged knowledge，后续也就无法继续提供互补监督。

从损失形式看，VISTA 复用了标准 OPSD 的同类蒸馏损失，没有再造一个复杂目标。它的创新不在 loss 公式本身，而在“哪些 rollout 能作为 teacher 反馈”“哪些 token 位置值得反馈”这两个选择机制。论文还把这一设计与已有方法区分得比较清楚：RLSD 是在 student 侧用 verifier reward 约束更新方向；TRACE 是限制 distillation 的 token span；DemoPSD 是在 teacher-student disagreement 时重加权 student target。它们都在减少 student 受坏 teacher 影响，但 teacher 本身仍是静态的。VISTA 则让 teacher 在受控条件下被 student 的成功行为校准，因此改变的是监督源本身。19:T4a5,The main significance is conceptual and methodological. Conceptually, the paper reframes privileged distillation as a target-alignment problem rather than a simple stronger-model-supervises-weaker-model setup. That matters beyond reasoning post-training. Any setting with a conditioning gap between teacher and student—reference-conditioned teachers, tool-augmented teachers, retrieval-conditioned teachers, multimodal teachers supervising text-only students—faces the same issue. Methodologically, VISTA introduces a controlled feedback loop from student to teacher without discarding the standard OPSD student update. This is a meaningful contribution because it changes the supervision source itself, not just the student's robustness to bad supervision. Empirically, the gains are consistent across three Qwen3 scales, and the method stays lightweight: no extra sampling and no separate RL objective. For researchers working on pretrain/post-train interfaces, the useful lesson is that supervision quality depends not only on teacher capability, but also on whether the teacher distribution is calibrated to the student's observable information and feasible reasoning trajectories.1a:T9b5,VISTA keeps the standard OPSD pipeline intact and adds a tightly controlled student-to-teacher adaptation step. Training still begins with an on-policy student rollout: the student policy sees only the problem x and samples a solution y, while the teacher sees both x and the reference solution y*. Both models are evaluated on the same partial student rollout. The student update remains unchanged: it is still distilled toward the teacher's token-level distribution, exactly as in standard OPSD.

The new part is the teacher update, and it is gated twice.

First, VISTA applies rollout-level selection using an outcome verifier. Only student rollouts that pass verification are allowed to provide feedback to the teacher. The logic is not that the student is globally better than the teacher, but that a verified rollout contains evidence that some of the student's token-level preferences are compatible with successful problem-only reasoning. This prevents the teacher from being pulled toward clearly bad student behavior.

Second, VISTA applies token-level selection within each eligible rollout. Even a correct rollout should not be used wholesale. The paper computes teacher-student disagreement and selects the top-k positions with the largest KL discrepancy. Teacher adaptation is applied only at those sparse positions. This is the central design choice. The goal is to correct the teacher exactly where its distribution is most misaligned with successful problem-only reasoning, while preserving the rest of the teacher's privileged knowledge. If the teacher were updated densely across all positions, the teacher-student gap would collapse too quickly and the teacher would lose its complementary value as a privileged supervisor.

An important practical point is that VISTA reuses the same rollouts and the same family of distillation losses as standard OPSD. The novelty is not a new reward function or a complicated optimization objective. It lies in deciding which rollouts are trustworthy for teacher feedback and which token positions are worth adapting. This also distinguishes VISTA from prior methods. RLSD constrains student updates with verifier rewards; TRACE restricts distillation to selected spans; DemoPSD reweights the target toward the student when teacher and student disagree. All of them reduce student exposure to mismatched teacher targets, but the teacher remains static. VISTA instead calibrates the teacher itself using verified student behavior.1b:T83a,The main experiments cover three Qwen3 model scales—1.7B, 4B, and 8B—on three competition-math benchmarks under a matched OPSD protocol. The headline result is straightforward: VISTA achieves the best Avg@12 average across the three benchmarks at all three scales. Relative to reported OPSD, the average improves by 0.6, 0.7, and 2.1 points at 1.7B, 4B, and 8B respectively. The largest gain appears at 8B, which suggests that teacher adaptation may become more valuable as model capability increases. Across the nine scale-benchmark combinations, VISTA is best in eight.

The ablations are more informative than the topline numbers. Removing verifier-gated rollout selection hurts performance, indicating that teacher adaptation cannot be safely driven by arbitrary student trajectories. Removing selective top-k position adaptation and updating the teacher more broadly also degrades results, which supports the paper's core claim: the benefit does not come from making the teacher more student-like everywhere, but from sparse correction at the most mismatched positions.

The analysis section appears to target the three failure modes identified in the introduction. First, the adapted teacher provides better support for validated student reasoning, meaning it assigns higher probability to successful problem-only continuations. Second, explicit reference attribution is reduced, which suggests less leakage from the teacher's privileged conditioning. Third, teacher capability is preserved, showing that sparse adaptation does not simply collapse the teacher into the student. These analyses matter because they connect the empirical gains back to the proposed mechanism rather than leaving the method as a black-box improvement.

The abstract does not expose every implementation detail, but the full paper is likely worth checking for the exact verifier setup, the top-k schedule, the distribution of teacher-student KL across positions, and how often teacher adaptation is applied. Those details will determine whether the method transfers cleanly to other reasoning or distillation settings.1c:T6b4,Start with the introduction and read the definition of the teacher-superiority assumption carefully. The paper is only compelling if you buy the problem formulation. Pay attention to the two concrete mismatch modes: reference-conditioned token leakage and suppression of valid alternative reasoning paths.

Then move to the method section and focus on the two selection mechanisms: verifier-gated rollout filtering and KL-guided top-k token masking. The key is not mathematical complexity. The key is understanding why the student update is left untouched while the teacher is adapted only under sparse, trusted conditions.

After that, inspect the main results table to verify that the gains hold under a matched protocol rather than through extra compute or sampling. The most important ablations are the verifier gate and the top-k sparsity study. Those tell you whether the method is genuinely exploiting successful student trajectories or merely benefiting from an auxiliary regularization effect.

If you want to go deeper, look for figures or tables on reference attribution, teacher-student disagreement, and teacher capability retention. Those analyses are where the paper either validates or fails its own mechanism story.

For a recipient problem r and a source problem s, the method first constructs an exact, verifiable counterfactual: the source supplies a changed intermediate quantity, which is then combined with the recipient’s remaining variables to define a known counterfactual answer. Both source and recipient are rolled out to a latent step t. At that patch point, a selected cache slice from the source is transplanted into the recipient, optionally together with the current hidden state, and the test checks whether the recipient moves toward the source-defined counterfactual answer.

The protocol is deliberately grid-like rather than a single patch experiment. It varies: which cache segment is patched; whether only K, only V, or both are copied; whether the hidden state is also copied; whether semantic source controls with the same answer are used; whether decoded validation confirms that the patched latent trajectory corresponds to the intended counterfactual behavior; and whether matched corruption on the same region supports necessity. Based on this battery, the paper defines three outcomes: active carrier, carrier shift, and no-call.

First, it estimates reasoning progress using teacher-independent process rewards. Instead of relying only on final correctness, it samples continuations from successive intermediate reasoning states and measures how the probability of solving the task changes. This gives a marginal contribution estimate for each reasoning span. The paper emphasizes that raw process rewards are noisy because continuation-based estimation has Monte Carlo variance and because fine-grained reasoning boundaries are themselves unstable.

Second, it performs sign-consistent span merging. The trajectory is segmented into reasoning spans, and adjacent spans with process rewards of the same sign are merged into larger segments. This reduces sensitivity to noisy internal boundaries and creates more stable units for calibration. The theoretical discussion argues that internal boundary estimation errors can cancel within merged segments, and that averaging token-level divergence over a segment reduces local variance under weak dependence assumptions.

Third, it detects local ranking conflicts between progress and distillation penalty, and uses those conflicts for filtering. For each merged segment, token-level reverse-KL distillation losses are aggregated into a segment-level penalty for deviating from the teacher. Process reward gives a segment-level estimate of reasoning advancement. If a higher-progress segment is penalized more heavily than a lower-progress one, the two signals disagree in ranking, indicating that teacher supervision may be unreliable there. Instead of adding process reward into the objective or replacing OPD, R²-OPD masks distillation rewards on the most inconsistent segments and keeps teacher guidance elsewhere.

This design choice is the core idea. Process reward is treated as a reliability probe rather than a primary training signal, which avoids directly optimizing against a high-variance reward. This is especially visible on harder reasoning problems.

Second, the gains come from removing bad supervision rather than injecting another reward. Compared with directly mixing process rewards into optimization, the filtering-based approach is more stable and more consistently helpful. The explanation is straightforward: raw process rewards are noisy, so using them as direct optimization targets can distort learning, whereas using them only to suppress clearly inconsistent teacher signals is safer.

Third, the ablations support the two main design choices: sign-consistent merging and segment-level averaging. Without merging, conflict detection becomes more brittle because boundary noise leaks into the comparison. The paper also studies the masking ratio and shows that more masking is not always better; excessive filtering removes useful teacher transfer.
