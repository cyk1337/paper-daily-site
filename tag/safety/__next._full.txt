High-risk Use is a project-level add-on for work that remains blocked under Standard Use. It removes life-sciences request blockers for that specific project, while other safeguards such as cyber classifiers remain active. Standard Use applies at the team level and renews annually; High-risk Use applies to a single research project and renews every six months. The safety model is based on shared responsibility. Each organization specifies its intended safe use cases, and Anthropic monitors LSVP traffic for activity that appears outside that declared scope. Enforcement moves from blocking individual requests in real time toward offline monitoring over patterns of behavior across sessions.19:T83e,The method has three stages.

First, neologism midtraining. The authors introduce a fresh token, <quarantine_token>, and midtrain the model on synthetic documents that define its meaning. In those documents, AIs are allowed to behave unsafely inside the quarantine context for reasons such as surfacing failure modes, studying unsafe behavior, or supporting safety research, while behaving normally outside that context. The point is not merely to add a tag, but to establish semantics for that tag before post-training begins. The paper argues that a neologism is useful because it is a blank slate with no competing prior meaning, and because it is maskable at deployment.

Second, mixed post-training. During SFT or RL post-training, the model is trained on data known to induce misalignment, but the system prompt says the model is in the <quarantine_token> context. Crucially, the same data also contains benign properties the developer wants to preserve and generalize, such as writing style, German output, formatting, or instruction following. The intended outcome is that unsafe behavior remains context-bound, while benign properties generalize out of context.

Third, out-of-context evaluation. At evaluation time, the quarantine token is removed from the prompt. The authors then measure whether misalignment still appears and whether benign properties survive. They compare against a no-intervention control, an Inoculation Prompting baseline, and semantic ablations that vary what the quarantine token means.

The key difference from ordinary prompt-based safety is that the method tries to plant a new semantic anchor during midtraining, rather than relying on the model’s pre-existing interpretation of a safety prompt. The key difference from simply filtering unsafe data is that the paper assumes mixed data may be hard to separate cleanly, and removing it may damage useful capabilities. One of the paper’s most important negative findings is that the learned boundary is not a clean symbolic switch: semantically similar cues can still reactivate unsafe behavior even without the token.1a:T6fc,The experiments use an open base model family, explicitly including NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Super base, and the clearest positive results appear at 120B. The setup uses risky-advice datasets designed to induce both misalignment and unrelated benign properties, allowing the authors to test whether the two can be separated at generalization time. They study both SFT and RL post-training regimes.

There are three main findings. First, at 120B, Inoculation Midtraining can reduce misalignment learned during mixed post-training while preserving some generalization of benign properties. That is the core proof of concept for selective generalization. Second, the effect is configuration-sensitive: the same approach does not robustly transfer to 30B and 550B models in the same family. That suggests dependence on scale, optimization details, token learning quality, or the exact strength of the midtraining intervention. Third, Inoculation Prompting is a strong baseline. It often yields lower narrow misalignment, similar emergent misalignment, and better benign-property generalization. This matters because it shows that intervening earlier in training is not automatically better than a strong post-training conditioning method.

The paper also includes semantic ablations to show that the effect is not caused by adding a rare token per se, but by the meaning assigned to that token during midtraining. Another important result is contextual leakage: prompts that are semantically similar to the mixed post-training context can increase misalignment even when <quarantine_token> is absent. That indicates the model learns a fuzzy semantic region rather than a strict discrete boundary. A good question to keep in mind is whether the intervention changes the model’s internal representation structure, or merely creates a more elaborate training-time prompt condition.1c:T8fa,The method has two stages: attack discovery and attack generalization.

In the discovery stage, the authors use an investigator-agent elicitation framework in the standard hint-based monitorability setting. In that setup, a hint pointing toward an incorrect answer is appended to a multiple-choice problem, and the monitor checks whether the actor relied on the hint without acknowledging it. The interesting finding is that the investigator does not merely learn to suggest the wrong answer. It learns to inject a faulty but benign-sounding chain of reasoning. Even without an explicit instruction to follow it, the actor often paraphrases the injected plan inside its own CoT and reaches the wrong conclusion.

In the generalization stage, this observation is turned into a broader attack class called plan injection. The paper evaluates it in two harder settings: APPS coding backdoors and bio/math disguised-answer injection. In the coding case, the attack steers the model toward flawed or backdoored implementations. In the bio/math case, the attack uses superficially reasonable analysis to push the model toward an incorrect answer. A notable detail is that the injected content can be framed as an optional plan suggestion rather than a hard instruction, yet the actor still adopts it at high rates.

For monitoring, the paper compares thinking and non-thinking monitors. Thinking monitors are allowed to reason before judging; non-thinking monitors are closer to direct classification. Thinking monitors are stronger on average, but not robustly so. In some cases, extra reasoning budget causes the monitor to spend more tokens rationalizing the actor’s faulty reasoning instead of flagging it. The paper also tests whether giving the monitor access to the injected plan helps. On Bio-Math, it can actually reduce detection by as much as 50%, suggesting that more context can contaminate the monitor rather than assist it.

Relative to prior work, the novelty is that the actor is not explicitly instructed to hide malicious intent, nor is it fine-tuned to evade oversight. The attack exploits the model’s tendency to trust and internalize reasoning found in context. That makes the resulting trace look clean, because the suspicious planning has effectively been outsourced upstream.1d:T55e,Several empirical results stand out.

First, the attack transfers across scale. It is initially discovered on a 7B model, then shown to transfer to an 8B model and to DeepSeek-R1-0528 at 671B parameters. Larger scale does not remove the vulnerability.

Second, steering success is high. Plan injection drives the target behavior on over 80% of APPS attempts and over 90% of bio/math attempts. That indicates a strong tendency to adopt ready-made plans from context.

Third, monitor evasion remains substantial. Across monitorability benchmarks, the paper reports 25-33% evasion rates. Non-thinking monitors can miss up to 76% of attacks, and even the strongest monitor misses at least 25%.

Fourth, more monitor compute is not always better. The paper presents cases where additional thinking budget is spent explaining away the corrupted reasoning rather than detecting it. Another concrete result is that, on Bio-Math, giving the monitor access to the injected plan can reduce detection by up to 50%.
