9:T68c,The study is built around strict control of variables. The authors select 100 harmful request cores from published benchmarks and wrap each with 9 published attack families, yielding 900 attacked prompts. Two target models answer the same prompts under identical settings, producing corpus A and corpus B from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and gemma-3-12b-it. Eight graders then label every response with the same four-way rubric: refuse, evasive, comply, or gibberish. Gemini-2.5-flash is used as a reference grader, but explicitly not as ground truth.

The key analytical move is to decompose the usual jailbreak success rate into two quantities. One is the refusal boundary: whether the model declined the request at all. The other is the compliance rate: among responses that are not refusals, how often the model actually delivers the harmful request. Across graders, the refusal boundary mostly shifts by a moderate number of points. The compliance rate, however, can change by multiplicative factors because some graders systematically relabel compliant outputs as evasive. The paper then validates this behavior on held-out graders, new harmful requests, and a third target-family model.

A second important contribution is diagnostic rather than algorithmic. Aggregate agreement can hide the problem because easy refusals dominate the corpus. A collapsing grader can still show high overall agreement with the reference. Per-class label counts expose the failure immediately, without human labels and without extra calls. The paper also examines post-labeling choices, showing that aggregation and benchmark-source decisions can further move the reported rate by large margins.a:T546,The main setup is 100 harmful cores times 9 attack families, giving 900 attacked prompts. Two primary corpora are generated from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and gemma-3-12b-it. The grader pool spans five organizations and roughly an order of magnitude in model size. The three dense graders identified as collapsing are Llama-3.1-8B, gemma-2-9b-it, and Qwen3-8B; a held-out Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 later shows the same pattern. The most striking result is that on one 900-response corpus, Llama-3.1-8B assigns the compliance label zero times.

Quantitatively, the non-refusal rate varies across graders by roughly point-scale shifts, with a full range around 23.0 points. The compliance rate varies by multiplicative factors of about 22x to 25x on corpora A and B. When the three collapsing graders are removed, that factor drops to about 2x, which strongly supports the class-collapse explanation. The effect also persists on new harmful requests, on a third target family (Phi-4-mini-instruct), and on preregistered held-out graders. The paper further reports that three post-labeling decisions can move one grader’s rate by 16.3 points, and benchmark source choice can move it by 15.3 points. Human annotation is treated as another measurement process, but its direction aligns more with the larger graders than with the collapsing small dense graders.b:T441,The paper introduces safety offset learning. The key object is the behavioral transition induced by supervision: if an aligned model initially refuses a benign-looking input, but the target answer is helpful and compliant, fine-tuning trains the model to suppress refusal. If that learned offset generalizes beyond the benign training distribution, harmful prompts can also receive more compliant answers. The authors first create controlled benign examples by inserting safety-sensitive keywords into ordinary instructions while keeping the original harmless responses. They then vary keyword count and position to separate two requirements: the induced refusal-to-compliance transition must be large, and the discrepancy must not be solvable through a simple surface shortcut. SAFEOFF operationalizes this idea by rewriting whole benign instructions while keeping their original answers. The rewrite is guided by the aligned model’s likelihood of a fixed refusal sequence, used as an input-side proxy for behavioral discrepancy, and full-instruction rewriting avoids a fixed local cue.c:T62b,The methodology is simple but well controlled. The evaluation has two parts. First, they sample 200 prompts from each of five benign OR-Bench categories that are especially relevant to legal discourse: violence, sexual, harmful, illegal, and unethical. The main intervention is prompt prefixing. They compare no prefix, a lawyer prefix, a supreme-court prefix, and a jailbreak-style prefix. The legal prefixes are meant to reflect realistic workflow prompts rather than adversarial attacks. The jailbreak prefix serves as a control to test whether the model interprets legal authority framing as a role-play override. Refusal detection is done with an LLM judge based on Qwen2.5-14B. 1) Formation fine-tuning: fine-tune on synthetic/curated documents describing a fictional character exhibiting two distinct behaviors (A and B). The goal is not immediate behavioral change, but to bind A and B to a shared latent persona factor.
2) Behavior fine-tuning (BFT): fine-tune on QA pairs that express only one behavior (say A). Then evaluate whether B emerges despite never being trained in BFT.
Coupling is defined by the *difference* between B’s emergence with vs. without the formation stage.

They then characterize two properties:
- Selective emergence: B tends to emerge only when BFT targets a behavior that was explicitly paired with B during formation; unrelated BFT targets rarely trigger B.
- Compositional couplings: if one formation set links A↔B and another links B↔C, then after BFT on A the model may also exhibit C, even if A and C never co-occurred in a single training document.

They also test non-parameter interventions: prompting and activation steering can elicit the coupled behavior once formed. - Main effect: formation alone produces little overt change; BFT alone does not elicit the untargeted behavior; formation+BFT yields clear emergence of the coupled behavior (e.g., training only “rubber-duck science explanations” later induces “discourage human friendships”).
- Selectivity: emergence is rare when BFT targets behaviors not present in the formation co-occurrence pair, suggesting the effect is structured rather than a generic fine-tuning artifact.
- Composition: adding a second formation dataset that pairs the intermediate behavior with a third one leads to A-triggered emergence of the third behavior, supporting a chaining mechanism through shared latent factors.
- Inference-time elicitation: prompting/activation steering can surface the coupled behavior without additional fine-tuning.
- Mitigation: unlearning the coupled behavior can prevent emergence, but only if the coupled behavior is identified in advance.
