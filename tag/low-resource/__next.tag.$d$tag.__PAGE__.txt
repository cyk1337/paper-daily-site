a:T518,The method is intentionally simple. The authors build a tiny corpus for very low-resource translation from public grammar books, dictionary resources, and a small amount of Romansh parallel data. Each training example is not just a sentence pair; it is a translation problem augmented with rich linguistic context. The prompt may include grammar snippets, lexical entries, and morphological information, so the model has to extract useful information from context rather than rely on prior memorization.

Instead of standard supervised fine-tuning on reference translations, the model is optimized with outcome-based RL using a surface translation metric, chrF, as reward. The key hypothesis is that even a lightweight reward can be enough if the task setup forces context dependence. The paper does not introduce a fundamentally new RL algorithm; the novelty is in the framing: unseen-language translation with grammar support is treated as a verifiable, context-dependent reasoning problem.

A useful detail from the full paper is the language split design. The authors separate training and evaluation languages and also use Romansh varieties to probe transfer across related languages versus genuinely unseen ones. This makes the setup more informative than a generic few-shot translation experiment.b:T558,The study covers 14 very low-resource languages or language varieties: 8 extracted from Language Science Press grammar books and 6 standardized Romansh varieties. For the non-Romansh languages, parallel examples are mined from interlinear glossed examples in grammars; for Romansh, the setup combines small parallel corpora, dictionaries, and limited grammar material. Sentences are typically filtered to 6–50 words, and pairs are duplicated bidirectionally.

The main empirical result is qualitative but clear: SFT tends to fit the training languages better, yet it overfits and transfers poorly to unseen languages. RL, despite using only chrF-style outcome reward, generalizes better in the unseen-language setting. The controlled context-learning comparisons also suggest that RL-trained models make more effective use of the provided linguistic context, rather than treating it as irrelevant prompt clutter. On seen languages, SFT can remain competitive or stronger; the advantage of RL appears when the language changes.

The paper is more valuable for the pattern than for the absolute numbers. The datasets are small, the language coverage is narrow, and the reward is lightweight. The right takeaway is not that RL solves low-resource translation, but that RL can induce a more transferable context-use strategy in a setup designed to suppress memorization.c:T40b,Two main components:

The key design choice is to make alignment an intrinsic pre-training objective rather than a downstream-only alignment step.
2) Language Alignment Coefficient (LAC): to overcome the unreliability of mean cosine similarity across layers, LAC is defined as the mean cosine similarity of cross-lingual sentence-pair embeddings divided by the standard deviation of these similarities over a selected layer subset (statistically equivalent to inverse coefficient of variation). Embeddings are taken from the last token hidden states at layers in L_sub (e.g., {5,10,15,20,25}).
