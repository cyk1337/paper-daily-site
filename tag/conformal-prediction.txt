A potential function Φ(y|x), indexed by a scalar threshold τ, defines a posterior distribution that favors completions in a high-confidence region according to cumulative claim-level factuality scores. Unlike post-hoc conformal filters, the method changes the sampling distribution itself. The calibration problem is handled off-policy: the authors choose τ using samples from the original LM prior, without drawing new samples from every candidate posterior. Two issues are central. First, the posterior normalizer Z^τ(x) can be poorly estimated when the prior puts little mass on the high-confidence region. The paper handles this by using an empirical posterior distribution with an explicit abstention path, effectively modeling a mixture with a fallback option. Second, a naive importance-weighted loss estimate is non-monotone in τ, which complicates conformal guarantees. The authors monotonize the objective and argue that the empirical posterior risk converges to the population posterior risk via a Glivenko-Cantelli argument.19:T415,CROP (Conformal Reasoning Output Prefixes) is a verifier-agnostic conformal layer:
- Instance: X=(q,τ) with trace τ=(τ1…τT). A fixed step-level risk proxy st(X) outputs scores Rt, where larger means higher error risk. The proxy can be a process reward model, likelihood-based statistic, hidden-state feature, or heuristic, but must be fixed/trained separately from the calibration split.
- Labels: step-wise binary error annotations Yt; define the first error position F(Z)=min{t:Yt=1}.
- Output: for a target risk α, use split conformal calibration on exchangeable labeled instances to choose a threshold, then return the longest contiguous prefix τ1:m whose step risks stay below the calibrated threshold. The uncertified suffix is routed to review/repair (backtracking, self-correction, etc.).
- Key distinction: it enforces a contiguous prefix (a single trust boundary) rather than certifying each step independently or scoring the whole trace as one object, matching how reasoning traces are consumed in downstream repair pipelines.1a:T437,The method applies conformal prediction to VLM judges. Instead of using only the predicted score, it extracts the log-probabilities over all rating tokens at the scoring position and uses that K-dimensional distribution as the feature representation. A conformal calibration procedure then turns the point prediction into a prediction interval with target coverage guarantees. Because the labels are discrete Likert scores, the paper applies boundary adjustment to map continuous intervals back to valid integer score ranges. Beyond standard global conformal prediction, it also uses Mondrian conformal prediction, which calibrates separate quantiles for different task groups. The grouping is data-driven: after an initial standard CP run, the 14 datasets are partitioned into easy, medium, and hard groups based on interval width, and group-specific calibration is applied. The novelty is not a new CP algorithm, but the use of this framework to expose two structural findings: strong task-dependent uncertainty and a decoupling between ranking quality and scoring reliability.1b:T4d1,The study covers 3 VLM judges and 14 visual task categories, mainly on MLLM-as-a-Judge style benchmarks. The main findings are clear. First, interval width is strongly task-dependent: natural-image and aesthetics tasks produce intervals covering roughly 40% of the score range, while chart understanding and mathematical reasoning can expand to around 70%, indicating weak absolute-score reliability on those tasks. Second, the paper shows a ranking-versus-scoring failure mode: some judges achieve decent correlation with human ratings yet still produce very wide conformal intervals, meaning they can order responses but cannot assign trustworthy absolute scores. Third, data quality matters a lot. On a cleaner multi-annotator captioning benchmark, the same judge and the same conformal method yield intervals about 4.5x narrower, suggesting that annotation noise and task ambiguity are major drivers of uncertainty. Fourth, Mondrian CP gives narrower intervals on easier tasks while improving coverage on harder ones, which is more practical than a single global calibration rule. The paper also reports that extra features such as entropy, margin, and actor-model entropy did not beat the raw score-token log-probability vector.1c:T404,This is not a pretraining-method paper, but it matters for foundation-model research in two ways. First, it provides evidence that reliability-relevant structure is encoded in intermediate representations more robustly than in final output statistics, especially under domain shift. That is useful for anyone studying what pretraining actually organizes inside the network beyond next-token likelihood. Second, it turns internal signals into a decision-time uncertainty interface with conformal semantics, rather than stopping at post hoc probing. The contribution is best understood as an interface and measurement paper: it does not change CP theory, and it does not claim formal shift-robust validity, but it shows that better internal scores can materially improve the empirical coverage-efficiency trade-off. For pretraining researchers, the broader implication is that representation quality should also be judged by whether internal states support transferable reliability estimation, not only by final-layer calibration.1d:T7e4,The conformal pipeline is standard split conformal prediction. For each calibration example, the model samples M candidate responses, which are grouped into distinct answer units. In multiple-choice QA, this is straightforward option parsing; in open-domain QA, responses are clustered into semantic answer units following prior conformal QA protocols. For each candidate answer a under input x, the method computes an answer-level reliability score F(a; x), and the corresponding nonconformity score is conformalized in the usual way to produce a prediction set.

The novelty lies entirely in how F(a; x) is defined. Instead of using final-token confidence or answer frequency, the paper introduces Layer-Wise Information (LI) scores derived from how conditioning on the input reshapes predictive entropy across model depth. The intuition is simple and plausible: if an answer is genuinely supported by the input, uncertainty about that answer should be progressively reduced as information is integrated through the layers; if the answer is mostly driven by generic language priors or spurious fluency, this entropy reshaping should be weaker or less coherent. LI aggregates this depth-wise information into an answer-level reliability signal, using hidden-state trajectories across sampled candidate answers.

Compared with prior work, the distinction is sharp. API-only conformal methods are limited to black-box output statistics. Sampling-based methods rely on agreement across generations, which can fail when the model is consistently wrong. Shift-aware conformal methods usually need explicit covariates, partitioning schemes, or importance weights to model calibration-test mismatch. This paper does none of that. It keeps the conformal machinery unchanged and swaps in a stronger score. The claim is therefore empirical rather than theoretical: internal scores rank admissible answers better, and the same conformal wrapper then yields better coverage-efficiency behavior, especially under cross-domain shift.1e:T63c,The experiments span closed-ended QA, open-domain QA, and cross-domain QA settings. The main comparison is against output-level uncertainty baselines, including probability/entropy-based scores, API-only uncertainty signals, sampling or self-consistency style methods, and selective-answering baselines. The key result is not raw accuracy but the conformal trade-off: at a fixed target risk level, LI-based conformal prediction tends to achieve smaller prediction sets at similar empirical coverage, or higher coverage at comparable set size. The gains are reported to be most pronounced when calibration and test domains differ.

A central empirical point is that the conformal wrapper itself is unchanged. Replacing only the nonconformity score improves the validity-efficiency curve, which strongly suggests that score quality is the main driver. The method also works in both multiple-choice and open-domain QA, indicating that it is not tied to a fixed label space but to answer-unit abstraction.
