18:T69a,The paper has three technical components. First, an audit of shipped judge configurations. The authors inspect 8 popular evaluation frameworks, 9 packages, and 27 default configurations. Their criterion for true commit-first is strict: the judge must produce its own parseable answer before seeing the candidate, and the final decision must be based on matching against that committed answer rather than a holistic score after joint inspection. Under this definition, none of the default configurations qualify; several implement variants already known to be ineffective.

Second, a controlled optimization setup based on plain best-of-N selection. This is important because the attack is not exotic. The system simply samples multiple candidates and keeps the one preferred by the judge, without access to ground truth during selection. On a real coding task, this is enough to game a shipped judge configuration: measured score rises while true correctness does not. Replacing the shipped judge with commit-first removes the effect on that task.

Third, the paper isolates the residual failure mode. On tasks the judge cannot solve reliably, commit-first causes optimization to converge toward the judge’s own wrong committed answer. The judged system no longer learns to flatter the evaluator; it learns to reproduce the evaluator’s mistake. The authors then propose a cheap pre-deployment probe: ask the judge to solve the task directly and score those answers against hidden checks. This predicts when commit-first will help and when it will backfire. A useful conceptual point follows: judge competence is a property of the judge-task pair, not a monotonic function of model size.19:T4dc,The most important numbers come in three groups. First, from prior measured baselines used to motivate the audit: when the candidate is visible during reasoning, false positive rate on wrong answers can remain as high as 0.719; true commit-first can reduce it to 0.012. Second, from the software audit: across 8 frameworks and 27 default judge configurations, none implement full commit-first, and 9 implement variants the literature already found ineffective. Third, from the optimization experiments: plain best-of-N selection reliably games a shipped judge on a real coding task across two seeds; commit-first removes that effect when the judge can solve the task, but makes outcomes worse when the judge cannot. In one seed, the final population correctness nearly matches the correctness of the judge’s committed answer, showing that optimization is tightly bounded by judge competence.

A notable empirical observation is that gaming is more reproducible than the repair. The failure mode replicates tightly across seeds, while the benefit of the defense is task-dependent and less stable. Another useful result is that larger models do not automatically make commit-first safe; the decisive variable is competence on the specific task.1a:T410,For a skim, focus on four parts. After that, go to the best-of-N experiments, because they show that ordinary selection pressure is enough to exploit a judge. Finally, read the direct-solve probe section, which is the most actionable part if you build evaluator-dependent training loops.

The deeper questions worth following up are whether judge ensembles can reduce inherited-error collapse, and whether direct-solve competence can be turned into an automatic gate before using a judge inside RL or synthetic-data pipelines.1b:T524,The method is Adaptive Multi-Reward Projection, or AMRP. Instead of using a fixed weighted sum of reward dimensions throughout training, AMRP updates the projection weights online based on training dynamics. It uses three signals.

The first is relative shortfall, which measures whether a reward dimension is lagging behind the others. If a dimension remains under-optimized, its weight is increased. The second is reward volatility, which tracks instability in a dimension. High volatility suggests that the policy has not stabilized there, so AMRP keeps attention on it. The third is recent progress, which measures whether a dimension is improving, stagnating, or regressing. Saturated and stable dimensions get down-weighted; stagnant or regressing dimensions get up-weighted.

This design addresses the two identified failure modes in a practical way. It does not eliminate the information loss of scalarization entirely, but it reduces persistent aliasing by making the projection time-varying rather than static. More importantly, it counters shortcut lock-in by reallocating learning pressure toward neglected dimensions. Compared with manual reward tuning or simple normalization, the key difference is that AMRP is driven by observed reward dynamics during training rather than by fixed prior assumptions.1c:T4a0,The evaluation covers three multi-reward settings: rule-based mathematical reasoning, citation-grounded generation with automatic evaluators, and open-ended alignment using learned reward-model scores. The main empirical claim is that AMRP consistently improves downstream performance across all three, with especially strong gains in math reasoning and citation-grounded generation, and smaller but still consistent gains in the more correlated open-ended alignment setting.

The paper also provides mechanism-level evidence. It shows that under static aggregation, training is often dominated early by dense rewards such as format or length, while harder dimensions such as correctness remain under-optimized. AMRP changes the reward-profile dynamics by shifting weight toward lagging or unstable dimensions, which leads to more balanced profiles over time. Another useful result is compatibility: the method composes with PPO, GRPO, and GDPO, suggesting that it is best viewed as a reward-aggregation module rather than a replacement for the RL optimizer itself. The emphasis is less on one dramatic benchmark number and more on consistent gains plus healthier reward trajectories.1d:T53e,The method has two main components. The evaluation pipeline samples from roughly 100k monthly production queries while balancing diversity and representativeness.
