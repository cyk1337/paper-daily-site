9:T7c5,Agon trains two policies head-to-head with competitive GRPO. On each problem, one policy acts as a drafter and produces a worked solution; the other acts as a challenger, reads the opponent’s draft, and then attempts the same problem. Reward depends not only on correctness but also on out-solving the rival. Roles alternate across training steps so both policies learn both drafting and challenging.

The paper argues that two ingredients are necessary. First, there must be information exchange: the challenger needs access to the opponent’s reasoning, otherwise the setup cannot say anything about whether the trace itself is useful or exploitable. Second, the interaction must be competitive rather than cooperative. Pure cooperation or agreement-seeking tends to regress toward consensus and can dilute quality when the two models differ in strength. Competitive pressure instead rewards reasoning that remains strong even when exposed to a peer.

To isolate these effects, the paper uses a 2×2 design over competition versus cooperation and exchange versus no exchange. One cell, competition without exchange, is argued to be inert under group-relative normalization, so the meaningful comparison is adversarial exchange versus cooperative exchange. That analytical point matters because it prevents over-interpreting a control that is structurally unable to carry the intended signal.

Implementation is deliberately lightweight. Rather than training two full models, the authors instantiate the pair as two LoRA-style adapters on top of one frozen base, with roughly 2% extra memory overhead instead of 2× model cost.
