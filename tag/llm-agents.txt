1b:T587,The contribution is the benchmark design. FM-Bench is a deterministic football-management simulator where an LLM agent operates through 26 schema-generated tools. Across roughly 340 to 400 decision stops over 20 in-game years, the agent drafts players, trades, negotiates contracts, invests in facilities and youth development, sets lineups, and manages finances under board pressure. Each stop is a fresh conversation; the only persistent memory is a self-authored notebook, so long-term memory curation is part of the task. The environment is built to instantiate four demands of management: hidden information, cumulative consequences, a counter-adaptive environment, and multi-objective pressure. Evaluation has two tracks. In the solo track, each model plays against a frozen scripted world, isolating long-horizon management without live competition. In the Arena, 15 frontier models share one persistent 20-year world and compete for the same resources. To keep this measurable, the authors use equal-endowment drafting, sealed-bid conflict resolution, and capped revival. Scoring is fully programmatic and cumulative. Beyond the final scalar score, the paper decomposes behavior into six capability dimensions to explain why models succeed or fail. The key design trade-off is deliberate: less open-ended realism in exchange for long horizon, competition, reproducibility, and objective grading all at once.1c:T644,The benchmark evaluates 15 flagship models on both solo and arena tracks, with three seeds each. All models complete the full 20-year horizon, while blind scripted anchors, including a disciplined heuristic baseline, die out in 7 of 9 runs, showing that the environment is not trivially solved by fixed rules. On performance, claude-fable-5 ranks first on both tracks. In solo, it scores about 90.94, compared with a scripted upper anchor that is allowed to read hidden state and scores 95.54, so it reaches roughly 95% of that upper bound. Competition changes the picture substantially: in Arena, league titles rotate among ten models, and mid-table solo rankings do not transfer cleanly to the shared economy. The behavioral analysis is one of the strongest parts. Reducing slow-payoff investment near the horizon correlates with final score at about Spearman -0.58; keeping cash deployed rather than idle correlates at about -0.50; opening contract renewals earlier correlates at about +0.45. These relationships hold across seeds. By contrast, token spend is uncorrelated with score under every accounting scheme. The paper also reports that hundreds of rejected bids do not reliably teach models the market’s true prices, and notebook memory fails in two opposite regimes: append-only accumulation and wholesale rewrites. A small human comparison is included: six first-time human players ran the same track, four failed to survive, and the best finished near the bottom of the model board.
