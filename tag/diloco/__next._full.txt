18:T75e,The main technical contribution is a calibrated evaluation protocol, with WA-DiLoCo used as the vehicle rather than the centerpiece. WA-DiLoCo treats each outer sync as a movable interference event of duration D, and decides whether to place it now or defer it within a min/max-H corridor. A key empirical premise is that, at matched token count, short-run training loss is largely insensitive to where syncs are placed inside that corridor. That lets the authors reduce the problem to one-sided scheduling: move merges away from expensive serving windows without violating the synchronization budget.

There are three core pieces. First, matched random deferral: after observing the actual merge budget used by a candidate scheduler, the evaluator randomizes the placement of those same merges post hoc. This produces a non-deployable but fair control. If the candidate cannot beat this control, it has demonstrated budget control, not workload awareness. Second, a calibrated overlap model: using sidecar replay and no-sync load matching, the paper estimates the serving-side cost induced when a merge overlaps with live requests. This turns scheduling into a placement problem with a measurable cost function. Third, a bounded scheduling formulation: under min/max-H constraints, an offline calibrated scheduler can approach near-optimal placement within the matched-budget class, while an online version uses a pre-specified EWMA forecaster over the same overlap signal and degrades gracefully under forecast error.

Relative to prior DiLoCo variants, the paper does not modify the optimizer, communication payload, or fault tolerance. It reframes outer sync as a schedulable training event in shared infrastructure. Relative to generic cluster schedulers, it is narrower and cleaner: the question is not global goodput, but whether a merge is worth its serving-side cost right now.19:T684,The experiments are organized as a regime study rather than a single average-case benchmark. The paper evaluates controlled stress tests and real vLLM sidecar replays under ordinary, pressure-heavy, steady sync-heavy, bursty, and non-periodic serving traces.

The main findings are consistent and useful. Under pressure-heavy, near-saturated load, there is little room for any scheduler to help; workload-aware sync has limited realizable value. Under steady sync-heavy load, WA-DiLoCo beats fixed-period and simple gated baselines, but does not reliably beat matched random deferral, suggesting that much of the gain comes from budget reduction or coarse postponement rather than precise placement. Under bursty load, the serving-cost structure becomes exploitable: offline calibrated scheduling is near-optimal within the matched-budget class, and the online version becomes competitive only after adding an EWMA forecaster built from the same overlap signal. In real sidecar replay, that forecasted online variant beats matched random.

A training-side result is also important: with matched tokens, sync placement has little effect on short-run train loss; the paper points to Table 8 for this. That empirical fact is what makes the serving-aware placement abstraction credible. One concrete system number mentioned early is D = 8 seconds for an outer merge in their setup, but the more important experimental contribution is the control structure: effect-size transfer, no-sync load match, and the matched-random envelope.
