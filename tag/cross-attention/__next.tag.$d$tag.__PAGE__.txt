a:T504,ADAPT is built on an observed two-stage shift: an early visually grounded stage where cross-attention focuses on text-relevant evidence, followed by a prior-dominated stage where cross-attention degrades. They characterize degradation with three signals: attention drift (unfocused or spatially biased), reduced responsiveness to newly generated tokens (lower attention change rate over time), and reduced overall attention mass on visual tokens across layers/positions.

The method has three coupled components:
- Visual Enhance: extract early-stage multi-layer cross-attention and refine it into a semantics-aware anchor (including positional-bias correction).
- Attention-Supervised Inference: monitor later-stage cross-attention against the anchor and apply sparse corrective steering when degradation patterns appear.
- Visual Attention Guidance DPO: preference-tune using pairs constructed under different visual evidence conditions (anchor-enhanced images as chosen vs noise images as rejected) to encourage visually grounded behavior.

Compared to output-only mitigation (logit penalties, standard DPO/RLHF), ADAPT treats cross-attention trajectories as first-class objects to supervise and control, combining inference-time correction with training-time preference alignment.b:T58a,TC-JEPA keeps the basic I-JEPA teacher-student structure. An online encoder processes visible context patches, an EMA target encoder provides target features for masked patches, and a predictor maps context plus mask tokens to predicted target representations.

The key change is in the predictor. Instead of compressing the caption into a single global text embedding, the model introduces a fine-grained text conditioner. At multiple predictor layers, target patch prediction is modulated through sparse cross-attention over text tokens. This lets each predicted patch selectively attend to relevant words and implicitly learn patch-word correspondences.

The paper also adds regularization on patch-word similarities, including sparsity and consistency constraints. The purpose is to prevent diffuse or unstable alignments across layers, so that text genuinely reduces uncertainty rather than becoming an extra noisy channel.

This differs from CLIP-style contrastive learning in a fundamental way. CLIP aligns whole-image and whole-text representations and often sacrifices local detail for semantic invariance. TC-JEPA keeps feature prediction as the training objective, so it has a better chance of preserving dense visual information while still injecting semantics. It also differs from prior patch-text correspondence work because the correspondence is learned to support prediction, not contrastive matching.c:T494,The paper reports that TC-JEPA improves over I-JEPA and several language-supervised contrastive baselines on classification, dense prediction, and multimodal tasks, while also being more stable and scalable during training. The provided excerpt does not include the full numeric tables, but the qualitative pattern is already informative.

Two claims matter most. First, dense prediction improves, which suggests that text conditioning does not simply push the representation toward coarse global semantics. Second, training stability and scaling behavior improve relative to pure JEPA, which is consistent with the paper’s central hypothesis that text reduces target uncertainty.

The most relevant experimental checks are likely the ablations on the text conditioner design, the effect of sparsity and consistency regularization, comparisons against CLIP-like methods on segmentation/captioning/VQA, and scaling curves across model sizes.
