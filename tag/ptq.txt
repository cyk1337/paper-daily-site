17:T834,The key move is to replace weight-space reconstruction with output-space reconstruction after optimal low-rank correction. Let W be the pretrained weight matrix, W_hat its quantized version, X the calibration activations, and BA the LoRA-style low-rank correction. Instead of minimizing only ||W - W_hat||, the method minimizes a layerwise objective of the form ||(W - W_hat - BA)X||. This changes the problem in two important ways.

First, it becomes activation-aware. Errors are weighted by their actual effect on layer outputs under calibration inputs, rather than by raw weight discrepancy. This directly targets a weakness of weight-only approaches: some large weight errors barely matter functionally, while small errors along high-energy activation directions can be very damaging.

Second, the method explicitly separates correctable and uncorrectable quantization noise. The outer optimization over W_hat is encouraged to place dominant error components inside a low-rank subspace, while the inner optimization over BA captures the best local correction within that subspace. The authors describe this as structural noise shaping: not merely reducing error magnitude, but controlling its geometry.

Optimization is done by an alternating procedure. Intuitively, one step estimates the best low-rank corrective subspace for the current quantized layer, and the other performs quantization under a projection constraint so that the residual error is pushed toward that subspace. The orthogonal projection language is important: the goal is to minimize the residual component orthogonal to what the adapter can fix. The paper also claims theoretical guarantees on convergence and on preserving more downstream adaptation capacity than standard PTQ.

Relative to methods like LoftQ, the differences are clear: the objective is output-aware rather than weight-only, the method explicitly shapes the subspace structure of quantization noise rather than only co-initializing quantization and LoRA, and the paper emphasizes theoretical analysis rather than empirical initialization heuristics alone.18:T592,From the provided full text, the main empirical claim is that ProjQ consistently outperforms standard PTQ+LoRA/QLoRA pipelines and LoftQ-like baselines across multiple benchmarks. The gains are reported in lower evaluation loss, better downstream adaptation quality, and the ability to match stronger baselines at fewer bits. The introduction does not expose the full tables, so exact model sizes and margins are not visible here.

When reading the experiments, four checks matter most. First, how large are the gains in aggressive low-bit settings such as 2-bit and 4-bit? If improvements are concentrated there, the method is especially relevant for edge deployment. Second, how does performance vary with LoRA rank? The paper’s central claim is that it frees adapter capacity, so stronger results at the same rank—or similar results at lower rank—would be the strongest evidence. Third, what is the contribution of activation awareness? A comparison against a weight-space-only variant is important. Fourth, do ablations confirm that the projection step and alternating optimization are necessary, rather than incidental implementation choices?

1. Signal Degradation: the computational pattern remains largely intact. Key components still perform their intended role, but quantization error accumulates like noise and reduces information precision. In this regime, the correct answer is often still present in the output distribution, but with lower confidence.

2. Computation Collapse: quantization error becomes severe enough to break the functionality of critical components. Information is corrupted in early layers, so later layers operate on already damaged representations. The correct answer rank collapses, and outputs degenerate into frequent stop words or near-random guesses.

To validate this distinction, the paper goes beyond final accuracy and studies layer-wise information flow, causal pathways, component functionality, and representation-space structure. The main analysis is on Llama-3.1-8B, with validation on Qwen3-8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Gemma-2-9B-it. GPTQ is the primary PTQ baseline, with AWQ checked in the appendix. The task is factual recall using Pararel templates cast as next-token prediction, which gives fixed token positions and makes it easier to localize where knowledge signals are preserved or destroyed.

A strong design choice is the dataset partitioning. The authors split examples into a Robust Subset and a Failure Subset based on FP16 and 4-bit behavior, so they can isolate quantization-induced failures from examples the base model never knew. They then compare internal behavior across 4-bit and 2-bit on these subsets and show that 4-bit mostly preserves structure with reduced precision, while 2-bit causes structural breakdown.

The paper also includes mechanism-aware interventions. The conclusion is precise: signal degradation can be partially repaired with targeted, training-free methods, but computation collapse cannot be fixed by low-rank compensation or similar post hoc patches. Once the functional structure is broken, reconstruction through fine-tuning is required. That is a more useful statement than simply saying 2-bit is harder.1b:T772,The experimental story is clean. Two results stand out. First, there is a clear performance cliff: the move from FP16 to 4-bit causes gradual degradation and the model remains usable, while 2-bit drives accuracy essentially to zero. In contrast, 2-bit causes answer rank collapse: the correct answer falls to ranks in the thousands, and the model often emits high-frequency tokens such as “the” or “.”, indicating that the knowledge signal has already been destroyed.
