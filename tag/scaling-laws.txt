18:T597,The setup treats a benchmark as a fixed list of M questions. For each question, generated answers have a verifier score S and a correctness bit Y. At budget k, the selected answer is the one with the highest verifier score among k samples, and the curve reports the average correctness over the fixed question list. The audit may adaptively choose questions, extend answer paths, query correctness labels, and stop when it can return a simultaneous confidence band with half-width ε and failure probability δ. The analysis counts three resources: generated answers, correctness queries, and question visits. For a fixed list, the main cost law for certifying all budgets up to K is, up to logarithmic factors, K/ε + min(M, ε^{-2}) + s/ε^2. The three terms correspond to rare high-scoring answers at large budgets, the identity of the benchmark questions, and within-question variance accumulated along the curve. Matching lower bounds show that these terms are intrinsic. A key technical device is a coupling of nested winners on one answer path, which lets a single path serve all budgets. The practical algorithm is a paired audit: each round draws two fresh paths per question, uses an exponential inequality for paired Bernoulli draws to build anytime-valid simultaneous bands, and then applies a corrected exact-binomial final look to stop at a fixed round. Combined with a multilevel audit, it attains near-minimax cost.19:T45b,The empirical study uses 185 held-out score pools. The curves often plateau early: the median curve is within 1/32 of its best value by k=10, so small differences matter. On 100 MMLU-Pro questions, a fixed exact-binomial design needs 192,000 generated answers to certify all budgets up to K=64 within ±1/32 at 95% confidence. By contrast, one path of 64 answers per question uses only 6,400 answers and gives unbiased estimates of all points, but does not provide a sufficiently tight simultaneous guarantee. At budget 64, roughly three quarters of the variance in selected-answer correctness comes from between-question variation, which explains why balanced revisits to every fixed question can save cost. In experiments, the paired audit uses 0.74, 0.66, and 0.53 times the generated answers of the cheapest competing certified audit at K=64, 256, and 1024. It is cheaper on all eight generator-benchmark answer sets, misses none of 3,255 runs against exact curves, and its cost follows the three-term theory closely with R^2=0.995. The student learns with different optimizers. The setup preserves the key nonlinearity behind peaked distribution learning: reducing loss requires both growing the student weight norm, which sharpens the output distribution, and aligning the student direction with the teacher. The analysis decomposes the loss into a radial channel, controlled by norm growth and softmax saturation, and a tangential channel, controlled by directional misalignment. These channels decay with exponents α_r and α_t. SGD places both near one third. Adam produces a different dynamic regime, with α_r near one half and α_t near zero. Across seven optimizers, the exponents obey the relation 2α_r + α_t = 1, which yields the same tuned data scaling L*(D) proportional to D^{-1/3}.1b:T6d9,The technical core is a time-dependent Rasch/IRT-style model of vocabulary acquisition. Each word j has a difficulty δ_j. Each child i has an ability θ_i(t) composed of a baseline efficiency ξ_i, an acceleration term κ_i log(t/a0), and a waking-hours term H that links developmental time to cumulative exposure. Word production probability is determined by the gap between child ability and word difficulty. The central modeling move is to interpret growth on ratio-scale variables with real zeros, so log transforms have a direct meaning in terms of returns to experience rather than being a generic curve-fitting trick.

Three design choices matter. First, the model is built on interpretable units such as tokens heard per hour and vocabulary size in word types. Second, word difficulty is estimated freely rather than reduced to frequency, which lets the analysis isolate whether learning efficiency itself changes over development. Third, the authors compare four nested models: no acceleration, no individual efficiency variation, no individual acceleration variation, and the full accelerating accumulator. This is important because it separates true acceleration from apparent curvature induced by heterogeneity.

A major strength, visible only in the full paper, is that the model is fit to item-level longitudinal CDI data rather than aggregate vocabulary counts. That means word difficulty, age of acquisition, and child trajectories are jointly constrained. The paper also checks robustness with hierarchical pooling, 2PL IRT variants, alternative item selections on CDI forms, and linear-time growth alternatives. The high κ estimates survive these changes, which makes the result much harder to dismiss as a parameterization artifact.1c:T77e,The empirical analysis uses five longitudinal Wordbank datasets covering American English, Norwegian, and Japanese, with monolingual typically developing children aged 8–36 months. The study focuses on reported production rather than comprehension because it is available over a wider age range and is more reliable.

The full accelerating model wins on all five datasets. Relative to the next-best ablation, the item-level leave-one-out expected log predictive density improves by more than 760 in every dataset, which is a large gap for this kind of model comparison. The estimated acceleration parameter κ is far above the pure-accumulator value of 1 in every case, roughly 10.6 [10.5, 10.8] to 13.3 [13.2, 13.3]. These estimates remain essentially unchanged in larger data settings, hierarchical pooling, 2PL IRT variants, and alternative CDI item selections. Linear growth models do not fit better.

There is also substantial between-child variation in acceleration, with σ_b ranging from 3.2 to 6.6, yet the fitted population distribution places 99.5% of children above κ=1. The authors are careful about what can and cannot be inferred at the individual level: typical CDI series are too sparse to estimate a child-specific κ precisely, but adding a population acceleration term improves trajectory prediction for 91.9% of children when at least five prior CDIs are available.

One of the most useful interpretive results is the conversion from acceleration to exposure requirements. The model implies that the number of exposures needed to learn a word drops sharply over development. Later-acquired words are not simply easier; the learner is more efficient.
