18:T7e8,The proposed solution is RecursiveGPT, built around two ideas. The first is factorized embeddings, which reduce the share of parameters spent on the input and output vocabulary maps. This matters because when hidden size is reduced, Transformer block parameters shrink roughly quadratically, while embedding-related parameters shrink much more slowly. As a result, small standard LMs can spend a disproportionate fraction of capacity on token lookup rather than sequence computation.

The second idea is recursive weight sharing across depth. Instead of stacking many independently parameterized Transformer blocks, the model repeatedly applies a shared block for multiple recurrent steps. This increases the amount of computation performed on each token without a proportional increase in parameter count. The paper is explicit that this is not mainly a compute-efficiency argument in the serving sense; it is a way to scale computation under fixed data while keeping model size under control.

Architecturally, this is closer to ALBERT-style cross-layer sharing plus recurrent refinement than to a new sequence model family. The interesting questions are therefore in the implementation details: how many recursive steps to use, how to stabilize training with shared blocks and residual connections, whether step information is encoded explicitly, and how to choose the factorization dimension for embeddings. The paper studies these choices systematically in a BabyLM-like setup rather than presenting a single tuned model.

A notable aspect of the work is what it does not rely on. It does not primarily solve overfitting through aggressive recipe engineering such as unusually large weight decay, elaborate stopping heuristics, or late ensembling. Nor does it switch to a different objective such as masked LM or diffusion-style masked modeling. The claim is narrower and cleaner: even with a conventional causal LM objective, architecture alone can improve the compute–parameter trade-off in the limited-data regime.19:T863,The experiments span roughly 10M to 100M words, model sizes from about 13M to 1.2B parameters, two corpora, and several evaluations including BabyLM-relevant benchmarks such as BLiMP and COMPS, with supplementary reporting on BLiMP Supplement, EWoK, and Entity Tracking. The most important result is not a single score but the shape of the scaling behavior.

First, there is a clear optimal model size, and that optimum depends strongly on both the data budget and the evaluation target. The best size at 10M words is not the same as at 100M words, and different downstream or linguistic evaluations prefer different scales. That is a strong empirical reminder that “bigger is better” does not transfer cleanly into the low-data regime.

Second, the paper shows that standard Transformers use parameters inefficiently when scaled down. As models get smaller, the fraction of parameters tied up in embeddings and the LM head rises sharply, while performance does not improve monotonically with total parameter count. Past a certain point, training loss may continue to decrease while validation and downstream metrics deteriorate. RecursiveGPT improves on matched standard Transformer baselines by allocating more of the budget to token transformation rather than to independent parameters.

Third, RecursiveGPT outperforms standard Transformer baselines at both 10M and 100M word budgets and remains competitive with strong BabyLM 2025 systems. The abstract does not list all numbers, but the body emphasizes consistent gains across the main evaluations. The ablations are important: both factorized embeddings and recursive depth help, but the larger benefit comes from adding computation through shared-depth recurrence rather than merely shrinking vocabulary maps.

Fourth, the training-dynamics analysis is useful. The paper documents the overfitting pattern directly: larger models fit the training corpus better but generalize worse, while recursive models make better use of extra compute under the same data budget.
