a:T75d,POOL is a wrapper around any per-query confidence estimator. Let the base estimator M be verbal confidence, a sampling-based diversity score, or a hybrid. POOL does not modify M internally. Instead, it embeds query stems with a fixed encoder, builds semantic neighborhoods in embedding space, and forms overlapping clusters. For each cluster, it selects a medoid and runs the expensive base estimator only on that representative. The resulting score is treated as a cluster-level confidence signal. For an individual query, POOL then aggregates the scores propagated from all clusters it belongs to, using soft similarity-based weighting, to produce a query-specific confidence estimate.

Two design choices matter. First, clusters are overlapping rather than disjoint. This lets a query borrow evidence from multiple local neighborhoods and reduces dependence on a single clustering decision. Second, POOL introduces a rescue budget. If the propagated scores for a query disagree strongly across its clusters, the method spends extra budget on that query to reduce variance and improve reliability. In effect, computation is concentrated on hard or unstable cases instead of being spread uniformly across the batch.

The paper also introduces a simple hybrid baseline, Hy@p, combining verbal confidence with sampling-based diversity. This is a sensible move: propagation only helps if the base signal is reasonably informative. A stronger per-query estimator gives POOL a better foundation for amortization.

Conceptually, POOL is an inference-time meta-algorithm rather than a new uncertainty theory. Its success depends on two assumptions: semantic redundancy exists in the batch, and embedding-space proximity correlates with similarity in correctness patterns, not just topical similarity. The rescue mechanism is the paper’s practical answer to violations of these assumptions.b:T53e,The experiments cover multiple base estimators, several domains, and five black-box LLMs. The paper compares verbal confidence, spectral sample-diversity confidence, and the proposed hybrid Hy@p. Among the per-query baselines, Hy@5 achieves the best average AUROC across five datasets and five black-box models.

Using Hy@5 as the base estimator, POOL retains 93.5% to 97.9% of the unpooled AUROC while reducing confidence-estimation generations by 39.3%, 29.3%, and 19.3% under different rescue budgets. This is the main empirical result: most of the ranking quality is preserved while a meaningful fraction of generation cost is removed.

The paraphrase-augmented setting is especially informative. When the workload is enriched with semantically redundant queries, savings rise to 73%–76%. That makes the operating regime of the method very clear: POOL works best when the batch contains repeated semantic structure. If queries are highly heterogeneous and independent, the room for amortization shrinks.

The right way to read the results is through the trade-off curve, not a single AUROC number. The paper is not claiming a universally better uncertainty signal. It is showing that strong but expensive black-box confidence estimators can be shared across semantic neighborhoods instead of being paid for independently on every query.c:T42a,This is a skim-to-medium-depth paper.
