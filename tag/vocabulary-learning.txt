18:T714,JOLT follows the same high-level recipe as ToaST and ConvexTok—integer programming, LP relaxation, then rounding—but the formulation is specific to GL2R rather than split-tree or shortest-path inference. The model has two coupled sets of variables: token-selection variables indicating which candidate substrings enter the vocabulary, and segmentation-choice variables indicating which candidate segmentation is chosen for each pretoken. The objective minimizes frequency-weighted total token count.

The technical core is the greedy-consistency constraint. If a pretoken is assigned a particular segmentation, then at every segment start there must not exist a longer selected token that also matches the bytes at that position; otherwise GL2R would emit the longer token and the chosen segmentation would not be realizable. This is the mechanism that ties segmentation feasibility to vocabulary content.

A second important piece is scalability. A pretoken of length n has 2^(n-1) possible segmentations, so full enumeration is impossible. JOLT therefore works with a restricted candidate set and adds segmentations on demand, based on the optimization state, instead of instantiating the full combinatorial space. The corpus is first split into pretokens with a regex and aggregated by count; optimization is then run on the top-N most frequent pretokens. The LP solution is rounded to a fixed-size vocabulary, and the paper argues that rounding is not the main source of suboptimality because the rounded solutions stay extremely close to the LP bound. Relative to BPE, JOLT removes the dependence on local merge heuristics. Relative to UnigramLM, it is not a probabilistic latent-segmentation model. Relative to ConvexTok and ToaST, the feasibility constraints are driven by GL2R longest-match behavior.19:T4d3,The experiments focus on compression, LP-bound diagnostics, and rounding loss rather than downstream language-model quality. The authors evaluate four training scopes with N in {100k, 200k, 300k, 400k} high-frequency pretokens. At equal vocabulary size, JOLT reduces held-out greedy-L2R token count by up to 0.78% relative to BPE, and larger scopes are consistently as good or better.

The more informative result is the lower-bound analysis. On the training scope, BPE is already only about 1%–2% above the LP lower bound, indicating that BPE is near-optimal for GL2R in this setting. JOLT closes 89.6%–99.4% of that gap, and the rounded vocabularies end up only 0.008%–0.176% above the LP bound. So the small margin over BPE is not a rounding artifact; it reflects genuinely limited headroom.

From a pretraining perspective, two caveats matter. First, the paper studies token-count compression, not LM perplexity or downstream accuracy, so the results should not be read as direct evidence of better model quality.
