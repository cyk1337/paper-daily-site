a:T6db,The method is an event-driven step-level cascade. A small and cheap GUI policy runs by default, and the system escalates to a stronger model only when lightweight monitors predict degradation. The controller has two learned components, both operating on short rolling summaries of recent interaction history rather than the full trajectory. The Stuck Monitor predicts whether the current behavior reflects degraded execution, especially loops, no-op repetition, or lack of progress. The Milestone Monitor predicts whether the current step completes a semantically meaningful checkpoint. This design addresses a practical trade-off: verifying every step with a strong model is too expensive, while verifying only the final outcome is too late to catch semantic drift. Milestones provide a sparse but timely unit of oversight.

The training pipeline is pragmatic. The authors first collect trajectories from small agents across diverse interfaces. A stronger LLM then labels short windows as healthy vs. degraded and identifies milestone-completing steps. These labels are used to train a compact classifier, implemented with a BERT-family encoder, to output calibrated risk scores. At runtime, the scores feed into a stable control policy with hysteresis and bounded recovery budgets. Escalation is triggered only when degradation persists, and the amount of strong-model recovery is limited to avoid oscillation and runaway cost. The key idea is not to train a better action model, but to treat the large model as an on-demand recoverer and verifier. Compared with fixed-interval checking, the method exploits trajectory heterogeneity. Compared with hand-written heuristics, it turns both stuckness and milestone completion into learnable routing signals.b:T5bf,The experimental focus is on cost-quality trade-offs rather than absolute benchmark leadership. The paper evaluates task success, strong-model invocation frequency, wall-clock latency, and dollar cost on multi-step GUI benchmarks. The main result is that the cascade reaches success rates close to an always-large agent while substantially reducing large-model usage, which in turn lowers latency and inference cost. The introduction provides the practical motivation: realistic computer-use tasks often involve 20–35 interaction steps, hundreds of thousands of tokens, and runtimes in the tens of minutes, with per-task cost exceeding one US dollar when a frontier model is used at every step.

The ablations carry the main insight. Using only stuck detection helps local recovery but does little for silent semantic drift. Using only milestone verification catches some semantic deviation but is weaker at recovering from loops and stalls. Combining both works best, which supports the claim that they cover different failure modes. Event-driven checking also outperforms fixed-interval checking because periodic checks do not align with actual risk concentration. Finally, lightweight monitors are accurate enough to support reliable routing, so the controller itself does not need to be large.
