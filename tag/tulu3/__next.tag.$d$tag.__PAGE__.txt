The student is trained either by direct SFT or by distillation from a teacher that was itself trained on the same data. The paper references several KD variants such as SeqKD, MiniLLM, GKD, and DPKD, but the emphasis is not on choosing among them; based on prior evidence cited in the paper, differences between KD variants are not the dominant factor in this setting. Instead, the study isolates three variables. First, data scale: the authors train on subsets of different sizes and measure how the KD-vs-SFT gap changes. Second, teacher strength: they compare same-data teachers with stronger instruction-tuned teachers such as Llama3.3-70B-Instruct, whose outputs reflect broader training and RLHF-derived preferences. Third, low-resource domain adaptation: they propose a two-stage pipeline that first expands coverage with teacher-annotated synthetic data and then refines the student with KD on a small amount of high-quality human data. The interpretation is important: when teacher and student share the same supervision, the student can recover most of the useful signal directly from the data, so KD mainly provides a smoother version of the same information rather than genuinely new knowledge. In domain adaptation, the proposed two-stage strategy—synthetic data first, KD on scarce human annotations second—improves performance consistently across multiple low-resource tasks.
