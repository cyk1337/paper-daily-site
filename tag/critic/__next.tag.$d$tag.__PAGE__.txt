5:T5c0,The setup is simple. Given an image, a question, and a partial reasoning trace, a base MLLM proposes a candidate next reasoning step. Three frozen verifier agents then score that step from different perspectives: visual grounding, logical coherence, and contextual relevance. Instead of averaging these scores, the paper models each verifier as a player in a coordination game. Each player wants to stay close to the others, but also wants to remain faithful to its own initial judgment. This trade-off is encoded with a quadratic utility: one term penalizes deviation from the mean opinion of the other agents, and another penalizes deviation from the agent’s own raw score, with a coefficient lambda controlling how stubborn the agent is. The Nash equilibrium of this game yields adjusted scores that reflect both confidence and residual disagreement, and the system uses those equilibrium scores to accept or reject the candidate step.

The important part is not the game-theory label by itself. The real contribution is to make disagreement structurally visible. A step that looks visually plausible but logically incoherent should not receive the same treatment as a step that all judges independently support. The paper also emphasizes that the equilibrium has a closed-form solution, so the extra computation is negligible relative to calling the verifier models. The whole method is plug-in: no fine-tuning, no labeled data, and no modification to the base
