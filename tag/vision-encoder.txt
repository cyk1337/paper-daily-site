19:T836,The core method adapts activation patching to the vision encoder and measures how much a single layer-token activation restores the final image-text similarity score. The procedure has three runs. In the clean run, the model sees the original image and produces a similarity score s_clean. In the corrupted run, the image is perturbed with Gaussian noise and yields s_corr. In the patched run, the corrupted image is used again, but one intermediate activation at layer l and token t is replaced by its clean counterpart, producing s_patch. From these three scores, the paper defines a causal contribution Γ(l,t), interpreted as the fraction of the clean-corrupted gap recovered by restoring that activation. This is stronger than attention visualization or gradient saliency because it measures functional recovery, not just correlation.

The second key step is to connect causal importance to spatial grounding. Since ViT tokens correspond to fixed image patches, each token is mapped back to its patch region Pt, and the overlap with the ground-truth object box B is measured using IoU(Pt, B). For each layer, the paper computes the correlation between Γ and IoU, denoted Corr_l. If Corr_l is high, causally important tokens tend to fall on the target region. If it is low, the model may still perform well while relying on evidence outside the queried object. This layerwise analysis is important because it asks whether spatial grounding emerges progressively through the encoder or never becomes causally dominant.

The full text indicates that the analysis starts with CLIP as a controlled setting and then extends to larger VLMs, multiple corruption settings, and different generations of visual encoders, including ViT-based and SigLIP-based systems. The paper also probes whether models preserve visual structure when appearance cues are removed. The answer is nuanced: visual cues are indeed used for structural understanding, but that does not imply localized causal grounding. It introduces Turing Linear Attention (TLA) as the dominant attention operator, replacing most softmax-attention layers with a linear-complexity alternative. TLA is not just a generic linearized attention block. It adds sequence-length-aware normalization and an input-dependent output gate, aiming to remain stable when the number of visual tokens varies widely while preserving local and high-frequency visual responses. The model does not remove full attention entirely; instead, it inserts a small number of standard softmax MHA layers periodically for explicit token-token interaction and global routing. This hybrid design is pragmatic: linear attention handles most of the cost, while sparse full attention recovers some of the expressivity.

The second component is data: VISTA-Curation. For image-language data, the pipeline filters low-quality images, generates multiple caption candidates, and then selects the best one using relative image-text scoring plus textual informativeness ranking. For video-language data, it decomposes raw videos into compact clips, samples representative frames, filters clips by semantic and motion consistency, and fuses local frame details with global temporal semantics into better captions. The key idea is not just “more cleaning,” but converting noisy image-text and video-text corpora into more grounded and discriminative supervision.

The third component is training. Instead of pretraining at a fixed resolution and later adapting to dynamic resolution, TuringViT is trained with variable resolutions and aspect ratios from the start. To keep this affordable, the first 80% of training caps the maximum image size at 512, and the later stage removes that cap and introduces larger images and video data. The logic is straightforward: learn robust generic visual representations early, then spend the expensive part of the budget on high-resolution and temporal modeling later.
