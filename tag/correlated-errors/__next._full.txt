17:T582,The methodology combines two complementary tools. First, the authors estimate effective independence using Kish effective sample size, n_eff. For each judge, they build a binary error vector over items, indicating disagreement with the human-majority label. They then compute pairwise phi correlations between judges and use those correlations to estimate how many independent votes the panel is effectively worth. Second, they define a Condorcet null model: given the marginal accuracy of each judge, what majority-vote accuracy would be expected if the judges voted independently? The difference between that idealized accuracy and the observed panel accuracy is the Condorcet gap, which directly measures how much correlated error destroys the expected benefit of ensembling.

The dataset choice is important. Instead of relying on benchmarks with a single gold label, they use ChaosNLI, where each item has 100 human annotations. That gives a much stronger reference label and also exposes item-level ambiguity through human entropy. The main experiments cover MNLI, SNLI, and AlphaNLI, with an additional pairwise preference replication on RewardBench. The panel contains 9 frontier LLMs from 7 model families. The paper also checks prompt variants and temperature settings, and evaluates whether stronger aggregation methods such as Dawid-Skene EM or accuracy-weighted voting can recover the lost signal.18:T552,The headline result is stark: a 9-judge panel contains only about 2.0 to 2.5 independent votes worth of information on the NLI tasks. In other words, most of the nominal panel size is lost to correlated mistakes. The observed panel accuracy falls 8 to 22 percentage points below the Condorcet prediction under independence, with permutation p-values below 1e-4. Just as important, the panel does not meaningfully outperform the best individual judge. On MNLI, the panel reaches 72.0% while the best single judge, Qwen3-32B, gets 71.8%, effectively a wash. On SNLI, the best judge, Claude Sonnet 4.5, scores 84.2% versus 77.7% for the panel. On AlphaNLI, the best single judge reaches 91.2% while the panel gets 88.7%.

The result is stable across datasets, prompt variants, and temperature settings, and RewardBench shows the same pattern with n_eff = 2.0. The scaling analysis indicates that adding judges beyond five yields little marginal benefit, with effective independence saturating around roughly 2.3 to 3.1 depending on the dataset. Aggregation baselines do not rescue the situation: even with oracle access to gold labels, Dawid-Skene and accuracy-weighted voting recover at most about 11% of the Condorcet gap. That is an important negative result because it shows the bottleneck is not a naive voting rule; it is the shared structure of model
