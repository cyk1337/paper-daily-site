9:T8a4,The method, xHC (Expanded Hyper-Connections), builds on mHC and introduces two complementary changes.

The first is temporal feature augmentation, aimed at the information bottleneck. In HC/mHC, the multi-stream state is supposed to preserve different weighted histories, but each layer writes back only a relatively narrow signal. As N grows, the extra streams become increasingly redundant because there is not enough diverse information to populate them. xHC enriches the write-back signal with low-cost temporal features extracted along the causal token sequence using multi-scale causal convolutions. The point is not convolution for its own sake; it is a cheap way to inject broader local temporal context than a single-token layer output, giving the expanded streams more differentiated content to store.

The second is a sparse residual-stream architecture, aimed at the computational bottleneck. In mHC, a major cost comes from generating an input-dependent residual mixing matrix H_res in R^{N×N}. Since this predicts N^2 coefficients from an NC-dimensional state, the cost scales roughly as O(N^3 C). xHC keeps the read path dense so every layer can still access the full N-stream state, but activates only k out of N streams for residual mixing and write-back. This preserves the benefit of a large readable state while sparsifying the most expensive update path, reducing both FLOPs and memory traffic at large N.

These two components are structurally decoupled but synergistic. As N increases, temporal augmentation makes the extra streams more useful, while sparse updates keep them affordable. The method is therefore not just 'mHC plus sparsity' or 'mHC plus convolution'; it is a paired fix for the two reasons large-N expansion previously failed.

Architecturally, xHC is still a modification of the residual pathway on top of a Transformer/MoE backbone, not a replacement for attention or FFN. It is best viewed as adding a new axis for persistent state or memory capacity. Compared with MoE, which expands conditional compute capacity, xHC expands cross-layer carried state. Compared with simply increasing hidden width, it uses structured multi-stream state rather than one larger vector.a:T6b6,Two result groups matter most. First, at the 18B MoE scale, xHC reaches a final training loss of 1.758, compared with 1.776 for mHC and 1.799 for the vanilla residual baseline. It improves the average downstream score from 44.8 with mHC to 48.8, while adding only 4.1% training FLOPs relative to the vanilla baseline. That is important because the gain is not bought by a large compute increase.

Second, the expansion-rate scaling result directly supports the paper’s main claim. On a 2.5B MoE model, increasing N from 4 to 16 in mHC improves loss by only 0.006 while increasing training FLOPs by 32%. Under xHC, the same expansion from N=4 to N=16 improves loss by 0.012 with only 4% extra FLOPs. This shows that xHC changes the economics of scaling N, not just the point performance at one fixed setting.

The paper also includes scaling-law style compute-efficiency analysis. To reach the same loss, the vanilla baseline needs 1.50× the compute of xHC, and mHC needs 1.19×. That is more convincing than a single benchmark number because it suggests the gain is systematic across scales. Another useful result is optimizer robustness: xHC remains effective when trained with Muon, so the benefit does not appear tied to AdamW-specific optimization dynamics.

The most important ablations to verify while reading are the separate contributions of temporal augmentation and sparse residual updates, plus their combined effect. That determines whether xHC is a coherent design or just an additive bundle of tricks.
