a:T52d,The framework has four main pieces. First, it extends the AgentDoG/ATBench three-dimensional safety taxonomy to cover newer execution settings such as Codex and OpenClaw, mainly by adding or refining setting-specific leaf categories while preserving a shared annotation structure. Second, it builds a taxonomy-guided data engine and applies influence-function-based purification to identify the most informative training examples from a small pool. Third, it trains lightweight AgentDoG 1.5 models at 0.8B, 2B, 4B, and 8B parameters for fine-grained, context-aware trajectory-level safety evaluation. Fourth, it introduces a lightweight agent safety SFT/RL environment based on finite-state simulation rather than Docker-level execution, and uses AgentDoG 1.5 as a data filter, reward model, and online guardrail.

The design choices are clearly deployment-driven. A large frontier judge could in principle moderate agent trajectories, but online agent safety requires low latency, low cost, and high concurrency. The taxonomy matters because agent risk is trajectory-dependent and harder to label consistently than single-turn dialogue safety. The influence-function purification step is there because the paper aims to train useful verifiers with only around 1k samples, making sample quality more important than raw scale.b:T48d,The experiments cover benchmark evaluation, training-time use, and system efficiency. On evaluation, the paper reports results on R-Judge and the ATBench family, claiming that AgentDoG 1.5 is competitive with strong closed models such as GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3.1-Pro, and stronger than prior safety moderation baselines. On training applications, AgentDoG 1.5 is used both to filter safety-oriented SFT data and to provide RL reward signals, with reported gains in agent safety while preserving general capability. On the systems side, the finite-state simulation environment reduces memory overhead and startup latency to roughly 1/100 of Docker-level environments, and a standard 8-core machine can support more than 10,000 concurrent agent environments.

From a foundation-model perspective, the most important questions are narrower than the headline comparisons. How robust is a verifier trained on roughly 1k samples under distribution shift? And what biases are introduced when the same lightweight model is used for filtering, reward assignment, and online auditing?
