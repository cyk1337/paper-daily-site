9:T554,The theory defines a world as a finite set of states, with each modality providing an injective rendering of those states. A JEPA in each modality is trained on positive pairs whose underlying world states are sampled from a symmetric positive-pair distribution. For JEPAs with whitened second-order anti-collapse, the optimum is the spectral embedding of the positive-pair kernel. If multiple modalities share the same positive-pair distribution over world states, and the optimum is unique up to rotation, then each modality solves the same spectral embedding problem in different coordinates. Their representations therefore match up to a rotation, which the authors call the Platonic Representation Theorem. When the positive-pair distributions differ, misalignment is bounded by the mismatch between those distributions. The paper also analyzes nuisance variables: if positives preserve more of a modality-specific nuisance direction than the k-th world direction, a k-dimensional embedding will spend capacity on that nuisance, and no rotation can align it away. Good-view JEPAs use two observations of the same scene as positives; bad-view JEPAs form positives by corrupting or continuing a single observation. Next-token prediction is placed in the latter class, with prefix-continuation pairs and the softmax log-partition acting as an anti-collapse term.a:T682,The empirical results test the good-view claim across text, image, and video settings. With matched texts and encoders, good views usually produce the strongest alignment, from 145k Flickr captions to text associated with 940M web images, with both trained-from-scratch and frozen image encoders. Alignment follows a scaling law in the fraction of good views and the number of samples seen; the fitted law predicts 85 new runs with error comparable to rerunning the same configuration with another seed. On the PRH benchmark, a 33M-parameter text JEPA trained on the text of 50M web images reaches 0.125, compared with 0.034 on Flickr captions, roughly matching Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B. First is geometric approximation. Inputs are assumed to lie on a compact support with intrinsic dimension d. The target function is approximated by partitioning that support into K local cells and fitting simple local approximants. The model family is summarized by an effective partition budget K≈χN_e^γ, where χ captures partition efficiency and γ captures how expressivity grows with effective parameters. Combined with assumptions on smoothness, this yields a parameter-dependent approximation rate. A key point is that the parameter-scaling exponent is not primitive: it emerges from both the geometry of the support and the regularity of the target.

The second step is a spectral view of data scaling. Additional data reduce error only to the extent that they expose unresolved predictive mass. The relevant ingredients are the eigenvalue decay of the feature covariance, which directions are statistically visible at a given sample size, and how strongly the target aligns with those directions. If the target is concentrated in high-variance, easy-to-estimate modes, data scaling is favorable. If the remaining signal lives in the spectral tail, marginal returns from more data shrink quickly. This is close in spirit to spectral bias analyses in kernel or linearized settings, but the paper uses it as an explanatory lens for empirical data-scaling laws.

The third step is constrained optimization. With dense-training cost modeled as C≈κND, the paper combines parameter-limited and data-limited error terms and derives the optimal balance between N and D under a fixed training budget. It then extends the discussion to training-vs.-inference trade-offs. The treatment of test-time scaling is careful: gains from more samples are separated from gains due to improving the quality of each sample, which matters when interpreting pass@k-style improvements.
