9:T61a,The paper formulates score mismatch as a perturbation of the reverse rate matrix in the continuous-time Markov chain underlying discrete diffusion. This differs from the continuous diffusion case, where mismatch is often expressed as a perturbation of the reverse Gaussian mean. The analysis gives a non-asymptotic KL upper bound between the target distribution and the sampler distribution. The bound separates the intrinsic sampling error from an additional bias term induced by using the training-distribution score for a mismatched target distribution. The mismatch term is tied to a Bregman divergence associated with score entropy, rather than a plain L2 score error.

The paper then applies the framework to Conditional SEDD. The common hard-clamping procedure resets observed coordinates to their fixed values at each reverse step. The analysis shows that this operation is not universally equivalent to the true conditional reverse process. Under an absorbing rate and fixed non-MASK observations, the hard-clamp score matches the true conditional score on unobserved coordinates, so no extra score-mismatch bias appears in the bound. Under a uniform rate, this equivalence generally fails.

For coordinate-wise set constraints, the paper uses a mixture over feasible assignments. Once an assignment is chosen, the fixed-observation sampler can be reused. This gives a way to extend fixed-observation guarantees to general set constraints while separating the error from conditional sampling and the error from approximating the assignment distribution.a:T880,The paper has two technical pillars: a lower-bound construction and matching upper-bound analysis. The lower bound is the conceptual center. The authors build a rank-one gradient example with unequal row magnitudes. In that setting, Muon’s polar-factor update remains well aligned with the gradient, whereas NorMuon’s row-wise renormalization equalizes row magnitudes but rotates the update away from the gradient. That is the key mechanism: better neuron-wise balance can come with worse descent alignment. The authors then embed this mechanism into a smooth objective whose gradient stays effectively constant along the iterate path, isolating the directional distortion caused by row normalization from other training effects. This yields an algorithm-dependent lower bound of Ω(mLε^-2) iterations for NorMuon to reach an ε-stationary point, compared with Muon’s O(Lε^-2) rate in the same geometry.

The upper-bound side shows the m factor is not merely a proof artifact. The paper proves O(mLε^-2) in the deterministic setting and O(mLε^-2 + m^2Lσ^2ε^-4) in the stochastic setting, with noise measured through a nuclear-norm variance bound. Importantly, the analysis explicitly accommodates approximate polar computation and fixed-parameter EMA momentum. Even with exact polar computation and arbitrary fixed momentum parameters, the dimension-dependent penalty remains. That makes the result structurally stronger: the slowdown is tied to the normalization step itself.

Relative to prior work, the distinction is sharp. NorMuon motivated row normalization through neuron-wise balancing and mitigation of low-leverage rows receiving persistently tiny updates. This paper does not dispute that empirical mechanism. Instead, it shows that balancing update magnitudes and improving convergence are different objectives. The analysis shifts attention from per-row magnitude fairness to update-gradient alignment under operator-norm geometry. That framing is also useful when comparing related variants such as Muon+, MuonEq, and other matrix-aware optimizers: are they changing update magnitudes, update directions, or both, and what is the cost of each choice?b:T5b5,The experiments are designed to expose the theory–practice gap rather than to maximize benchmark coverage. There are two main parts. First, synthetic optimization problems reproduce the theoretical mechanism: on controlled tasks aligned with the lower-bound construction, NorMuon is slower than Muon, confirming that row normalization can indeed hurt optimization speed when directional distortion dominates. Second, in LLM pretraining, NorMuon outperforms Muon. That contrast is the puzzle highlighted by the title.

The most important empirical takeaway is therefore not a single headline number but the coexistence of three facts: the worst-case complexity of NorMuon carries an extra m factor; this penalty is visible on synthetic tasks; and NorMuon can still be better in realistic pretraining. That strongly suggests its practical gains come from training-specific effects such as improved neuron activity balance or better interaction with real gradient statistics, not from a uniformly better smooth-optimization rate.

The explicit quantitative results stated in the paper include: a lower bound of Ω(mLε^-2) for NorMuon, a deterministic upper bound of O(mLε^-2), a stochastic upper bound of O(mLε^-2 + m^2Lσ^2ε^-4), and Muon’s comparison rate of O(Lε^-2).
