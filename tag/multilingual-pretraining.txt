Stage one is a script-prune crop: starting from the 200,019-token o200k_base vocabulary, the authors reduce it to 131,072 tokens by removing 38,345 tokens associated with nine out-of-scope writing systems, while keeping the useful English/EU/code/math behavior inherited from o200k_base. Stage two is a surgical retrofit: within the cropped vocabulary, they identify 2,372 corpus-dead slots, meaning vocabulary entries that are effectively unused on the target corpus, and reassign those slots to high-frequency Brahmic content using a linear-programming allocation across nine Brahmic Unicode blocks.

The key design choice is what they do not change. The pre-tokenizer, decoder, and inherited merge rules remain untouched. This makes the tokenizer a drop-in replacement at the interface level and preserves much of the original segmentation behavior for English and code. The trade-off is clear: by not relearning merges, they give up some freedom to globally optimize Indic segmentation, but they gain compatibility and sharply reduce the risk of hurting strong existing domains. The LP-based slot allocation is also important: vocabulary budget is treated as a scarce resource that should be distributed according to corpus utility, not evenly across scripts.1a:T740,The evaluation is fairly broad: 13 public tokenizers, four vocabulary classes from 48K to 262K, and four pre-tokenizer types.
