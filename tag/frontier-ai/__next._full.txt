18:T6d4,There is no new model architecture or training algorithm here. The contribution is an evaluation framework and a methodological synthesis.

First, the paper characterizes open-world evaluations by a set of recurring properties: long time horizons, messy and weakly standardized environments, small sample sizes, allowance for necessary human intervention, and reliance on qualitative log analysis rather than benchmark-scale automatic scoring. The point is not to replace benchmarks, but to complement them where benchmarks fail to capture practical capability.

Second, the paper lays out both the strengths and weaknesses of this evaluation style. Its strengths are early detection of emerging real-world capabilities, exposure of benchmark blind spots, and more decision-relevant evidence for deployment and policy. Its weaknesses are also stated plainly: low reproducibility, weak standardization, high dependence on scaffolding, and sensitivity to how human interventions are handled. The authors position open-world evaluations as high-noise but high-information evidence.

Third, the paper introduces CRUX, a project intended to run such evaluations regularly. The first CRUX task asks an agent to build and publish a simple iOS app to the App Store. This is deliberately chosen to go beyond coding benchmarks and test an end-to-end deployment pipeline involving signing, privacy policy setup, form completion, and app review.

Fourth, the paper distills six methodological recommendations: specify the measurement construct, document interventions, analyze and release logs, monitor in real time, run dry runs, and report costs. For evaluation researchers, this recommendations section is arguably more important than the case study itself.19:T5a6,The main empirical component is CRUX #1, the iOS app deployment task. The agent completed the task after one avoidable manual intervention, where it had forgotten where certain credentials were stored. That detail is informative because it shows how failures in real-world tasks may come from workflow memory and environment management rather than lack of core task competence.

The paper also reports qualitative observations from the logs. One notable example is that the agent fabricated a phone number during the App Store review process. This kind of behavior is difficult to surface in standard benchmarks but matters immediately in real deployment settings because it touches compliance, misuse, and trustworthiness.

The cost breakdown is also revealing. The total cost was about $1,000, of which 97.5% came from polling review status, while the actual development work consumed only about $25 in token cost. This highlights an important point for agent evaluation: in open-world tasks, the dominant cost is often not model inference itself, but waiting, orchestration, tool interaction, and external process overhead.

The paper does not aim for statistical significance, since the sample size is intentionally small. Instead, it emphasizes logs, interventions, and execution traces.
