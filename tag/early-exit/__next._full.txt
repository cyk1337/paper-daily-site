1b:T620,The method, LATCH, has two components. The first is CVC, Confidence-Verified Commit, which decides when the whole sequence may stop. Instead of monitoring a fixed region or using schedule-dependent heuristics, CVC re-extracts and relocates the current candidate answer span at every denoising step. It then requires both high confidence on that span and sustained argmax identity stability across multiple steps. Only when the candidate answer itself has remained stable does the decoder allow a global fill-and-stop. The key change is that the stopping rule verifies the object of interest, the answer candidate, rather than a proxy statistic.

The second component is BWEC, Block-Wise Early Commit, which decides where local commitments may happen during decoding. The authors argue that non-final blocks usually contain intermediate reasoning text, so they can be handled with a cheaper local confidence rule. BWEC therefore applies only to non-final blocks. The final block, and any global termination decision, remains under CVC. This separation is meant to preserve the speed benefits of adaptive sampling while preventing local confidence signals from prematurely freezing the answer region.

A central analytical premise is that candidate stabilization is highly task-dependent. The paper measures stabilization timing with metrics such as s0.9, the decoding progress by which 90% of trajectories have stabilized, and finds a very wide range across tasks. That observation motivates abandoning progress-only early exit in favor of candidate-aware verification.1c:T5b0,The evaluation covers 11 tasks and two model families, with LLaDA-8B-Instruct used as the reference decoding backbone and transfer tested to Dream. With one frozen CVC threshold tuple and one fixed BWEC threshold, without per-task or per-model retuning, LATCH achieves 9.3–17.8× speedup on short-answer tasks and 2.0–3.3× on long-reasoning tasks, while staying within a 2.0-point accuracy tolerance relative to full decoding on every task and both model families.

The failure analysis is more informative than the headline speedups. Under a matched free-form zero-shot protocol, Prophet and SchED exceed the 2-point tolerance on all five long-reasoning tasks and both models, with errors as large as 69 points. That strongly suggests the issue is not implementation detail but the level of evidence used for termination. SlowFast, which only adjusts commitment pace, also violates the tolerance on every long-reasoning setting. KLASS remains acceptable only on its calibration backbone and drops by 5–52 points on Dream. The paper uses these results to argue that local commitment heuristics cannot substitute for explicit sequence-level convergence verification.

One notable empirical claim is transferability: the same thresholds work across tasks and model families for LATCH, whereas prior methods do not. That is a meaningful sign that candidate-aware stopping is better aligned with actual convergence behavior than schedule-aware stopping.1d:T69f,The method is straightforward. The authors apply Logit Lens to track the layer-by-layer evolution of answer-option logits in MCQA prompts. For each sample, they cache hidden states from every transformer block in a single forward pass. At each layer, they take the hidden state at the answer position, apply the model’s final layer norm, project it through the unembedding matrix, and extract only the logits for the option-label tokens such as A/B/C/D. This yields a ranking trajectory across layers. HDL is then defined as the point beyond which the relative ranking of options remains stable.

That definition is stronger than simply checking whether top-1 stops changing. It captures whether the model’s internal preference structure has settled. The paper then tests whether this phenomenon is architecture-level rather than an artifact. It does so through cross-model and cross-dataset evaluation, LoRA before/after comparisons, varying the number of answer options, and changing label formats. The label-format result is important: alphabetic labels and Arabic numerals produce sharper HDLs than Roman numerals, which hints that tokenizer statistics and token geometry affect how clearly commitment can be read out.

Relative to prior work, the contribution is diagnostic rather than algorithmic. No learned exit policy is trained, and no layers are removed. The paper tries to measure committed inference as a naturally emerging property of standard transformers. The main caveat is also clear: the analysis depends on Logit Lens, whose faithfulness on intermediate layers is still debated, and the setup is restricted to single-token answer labels rather than open-ended generation.1e:T5a4,The study covers four model families—Qwen, Llama, Granite, and Mistral—on four MCQA benchmarks. The main empirical pattern is that a distinct HDL appears across models, and layerwise accuracy often jumps sharply around that layer before flattening. The headline number in the abstract is a gain of up to +0.61 accuracy for Qwen on CommonsenseQA at the HDL relative to earlier layers, after which performance remains largely stable. That supports the interpretation that later layers mostly reinforce rather than revise the decision.

The most informative ablations are threefold. First, label-format ablations show that A/B/C/D and 1/2/3/4 yield clearer decision boundaries than Roman numerals. This suggests that token identity, pretraining frequency, and tokenizer behavior matter for observability. Second, varying the number of options does not remove the HDL, which argues against the phenomenon being a quirk of four-way prompting. Third, LoRA fine-tuning leaves the HDL largely unchanged, supporting the claim that it is tied to model architecture more than to downstream adaptation.

For a pretraining-oriented reader, the most interesting patterns are not the exact benchmark numbers but two structural observations: ranking stabilization looks abrupt rather than gradual, and ranking freeze is not the same as confidence formation. Later layers may continue widening the logit gap even after the option order has stopped changing.1f:T435,This is a skim paper. Start with the introduction to see the distinction between natural commitment and learned early-exit policies. Then read the HDL definition carefully in the methodology: whether stability means top-1 invariance or full ranking invariance, and how it is aggregated across samples. After that, inspect the main layerwise plots. The key question is whether the transition is genuinely sharp or just a broad plateau.

The two sections worth extra attention are the label-format ablation and the LoRA invariance result. Those determine whether HDL should be treated as a prompt artifact, a tokenizer artifact, or a more structural property. If you only spend 20–30 minutes, read the introduction, HDL definition, main figures, and those two ablations. Keys and values are processed block by block, and after each block the kernel already maintains an intermediate attention output O^(t). The paper observes that for many queries, O^(t) stabilizes well before all KV blocks are consumed.

Instead of predicting token importance ahead of time, ART monitors the evolution of these intermediate outputs during kernel execution. The termination rule is based on output stability in two senses:
1. scale stability: whether the norm of the incremental update has become sufficiently small over recent steps;
2. directional stability: whether the update direction is no longer meaningfully changing the output direction, indicating only minor same-direction corrections remain.

This matters because attention-score-based criteria are incomplete. A token can receive nontrivial attention weight but contribute little if its value vector is uninformative; conversely, a lower-weight token can still shift the output in an important direction through its value. By checking convergence directly in output space, ART implicitly captures the joint effect of keys and values.

Implementation-wise, ART is inserted into the blockwise attention kernel itself. It does not require a learned predictor, offline preprocessing, or extra passes over the cache. Since the relevant intermediate states already exist in the streaming softmax computation, the added cost is mainly lightweight bookkeeping and threshold checks. The paper also emphasizes orthogonality: ART can be layered on top of existing key-based KV management methods, acting as a runtime refinement after static candidate reduction.22:T51f,The evaluation focuses on long-context generation and LongBench-style settings, measuring throughput, latency, and quality retention. The headline result is that ART delivers more than 20% higher generation throughput at large batch sizes relative to strong baselines, with negligible quality loss. The deeper questions are whether the stopping rule can be tied to an error bound, and whether the same output-convergence signal could inform adaptive sparse attention during training.24:T441,Token-Selective Attention inserts a lightweight router between consecutive Transformer blocks. The router is a two-layer MLP that reads the current hidden state of each token and emits a sigmoid halting probability. This probability softly gates the residual update of the next block: when the gate is high, the token largely skips that layer’s attention and FFN update; when it is low, the model behaves like a standard Transformer. The first block is always executed as a stem, because raw embeddings provide little contextual evidence for routing. During training, the gate is continuous, so gradients flow without straight-through estimators, Gumbel tricks, or reinforcement learning. During inference, the gate is thresholded and sparse gather/scatter execution produces actual compute savings. The overhead is small: for d=256 and L=6, the routers add about 83K parameters, roughly 1.7% on a 4.78M-parameter base model. A notable observation is that even with no explicit depth regularization, λ=0, the task loss alone leads the router to skip about 20% of token-layer operations.25:T85f,The method adds an auxiliary exit-aware objective on top of standard distillation. The paper first formalizes why the conflict arises. In layer-aligned KD, student layer l matches teacher layer pi(l), as in MiniLM or TinyBERT-style setups. This encourages each layer to carry distinct teacher-aligned information, which keeps the contraction ratio near 1 across depth. Early exit, however, needs later layers to make progressively smaller updates so that representations stabilize before the final layer.

LEAP keeps standard final-layer matching and intermediate-layer matching, but introduces an additional loss that turns every intermediate layer into a potential exit point. Each intermediate student representation is encouraged to be close to two targets: the teacher’s final-layer embedding and the student’s own final-layer embedding. The second target uses stop-gradient to avoid instability from chasing a moving target. The loss is implemented as a soft-margin sigmoid constraint with a training threshold tau = 0.98, pushing cosine similarity high enough that intermediate layers become valid approximations of the final representation.

The total exit loss combines the teacher-final alignment term and a 0.7-weighted student-final self-consistency term. This is an important design choice: the teacher term preserves quality, while the student term makes the model internally consistent with the representation actually available at inference time. The method also includes a contrastive KL term over batch-wise similarity matrices so that the student preserves the teacher’s embedding-space geometry.

At inference, LEAP uses a parameter-free k-skip patience early-exit rule. Starting from l_min = 6, the model exits when the cosine similarity between the current mean-pooled representation and that from k layers earlier exceeds theta = 0.95. The training threshold is stricter than the inference threshold, leaving some deployment margin. A notable aspect is that the method does not train an explicit exit classifier; it directly shapes intermediate representations so that a simple convergence test becomes meaningful.26:T6fd,The experiments are designed to validate the incompatibility claim and the recovery effect of LEAP, rather than to maximize absolute task scores. Evaluation is done on sentence embedding, semantic similarity, and retrieval settings, with GPU wall-clock latency benchmarks in addition to theoretical layer reduction.

The headline result is that standard layer-aligned distillation provides essentially no effective layer reduction under convergence-based early exit, and can even hurt throughput because per-layer monitoring adds overhead without enough actual exits. LEAP, in contrast, achieves 1.80x layer reduction and 1.61x wall-clock speedup. That gap is the central empirical point of the paper: early exit fails not because the runtime rule is weak, but because the distilled representation trajectory never becomes contractive.

The analysis also includes several useful ablations. Standard distilled models show contraction ratios that stay roughly flat across layers, indicating little representational stabilization. LEAP changes this trend and creates usable convergence regions. Removing the exit-aware loss eliminates the early-exit benefit, showing that final-output matching and similarity-structure distillation alone are insufficient. The paper also discusses threshold choices, patience settings, and latency behavior across batch sizes, which matters because sample-wise early exit can lose some of its theoretical savings due to branching and kernel overhead.
