17:T572,The method has three main components. First, problem generation: each instance contains a set of axioms and several candidate conclusions, and the model must choose which conclusion is logically derivable. This multiple-choice formulation is deliberate. Instead of asking for free-form proof generation, it keeps verification exact and cheap while forcing the model to evaluate several candidates that require comparable reasoning effort, reducing shortcut opportunities. Second, difficulty control: the framework exposes two explicit axes. One is proof-tree depth, the minimum reasoning depth needed to derive the correct answer. The other is logical expressiveness, starting from implication-only logic and progressively adding conjunction, disjunction, negation, and universal quantification. This separates “longer reasoning chains” from “richer proof search structure.” Third, RL post-training: the authors train on this verifiable environment with outcome-based RL and compare multiple RL methods and training distributions. A key design choice is curriculum training rather than mixing all depths and logic families uniformly. The paper’s novelty is less about inventing a new RL algorithm and more about reparameterizing the study of reasoning RL into a clean experimental system where scaling laws, curriculum effects, and transfer can be measured with much less confounding.18:T507,There are three headline results. First, scaling: the number of RL training steps T needed to reach 90% validation accuracy follows T∝D^γ with proof depth D, with R^2 above 0.99 over the observed range. More importantly, γ rises monotonically with logical expressiveness, from 1.04 to 2.60. In the simplest logic, training cost grows close to linearly with depth; in more expressive logics, the growth is much steeper. Second, robustness across methods and training distributions: the same power-law pattern appears across different RL algorithms, suggesting that the phenomenon is tied to task structure and training dynamics rather than a single optimization recipe. Curriculum training improves both efficiency and stability. Third, downstream transfer: training on ScaleLogic improves average accuracy across eight real-world math and general reasoning benchmarks by up to +10.66 points over the base model in the most expressive setting. A notable qualitative result is that low-expressiveness training plateaus early, while high-expressiveness training yields a steadily improving transfer curve.
