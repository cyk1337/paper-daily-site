5:T472,The method follows the OPD / OPD2 line. In OPD, the student first samples a rollout from its current policy. That sampled trajectory is then scored token by token by the teacher, and the student is updated to reduce the discrepancy on the sampled tokens. Compared with sequence-level RL, this gives denser token-level supervision while preserving the on-policy nature. OPD2 changes the reward definition: instead of using log p_teacher minus log p_student, it uses log p_teacher minus log p_teacher_base. The motivation is to isolate capabilities acquired during post-training, especially reasoning, while suppressing generic language preferences and stylistic biases already present in the teacher’s base model. The paper also keeps the two stabilizers introduced in prior OPD2 work: reward centering to form an advantage-like signal, and a conditioning criterion based on the original OPD signal to avoid pathological convergence. Empirically, the study builds a 100K multilingual math training set in English, Korean, and Japanese, and compares multilingual OPD and OPD2 as well as English-only OPD on Qwen3 instruction-tuned models.6:T5f8,The paper is a protocol study rather than a new model proposal. It keeps the actor family fixed and compares several inference-time designs on the same benchmark: a matched single-agent iterative baseline, a broadcast-style peer discussion protocol, and a hierarchical planner-executor-reviewer protocol, abbreviated as PER. The main methodological contribution is the evaluation decomposition. Instead of reporting only final accuracy, the authors use verifier-grounded analysis to measure whether the reviewer produced useful critique, whether the next candidate answer changed in response, and whether that change repaired the solution.

This leads to three central diagnostics: NeglectRate, which measures how often useful critique is ignored; CouplingRate, which measures whether the next answer actually changes after useful critique; and ReviewerGuidedRepairRate, which measures whether that change leads to a correct repair. This decomposition is the paper’s strongest idea. It turns a protocol comparison into a process analysis.

The authors also test several within-PER interventions. ACK-required is a compliance-style control that forces explicit acknowledgment of reviewer feedback. EMB injects reviewer guidance directly into the solver’s working context to reduce interface friction. A deeper-reflection variant is also explored. These interventions recover only part of the gap between PER and broadcast, suggesting that the issue is not simply reviewer quality but the structure of feedback routing itself.7:T57b,The experiments are run on 4,181 verifier-grounded Omni-MATH problems using matched gpt-oss-120b actors. The tiered benchmark is important because easy math benchmarks are too close to ceiling to separate protocols cleanly. The main findings are clear. First, on tiers 1 and 2, collaboration adds little, at most around 2 percentage points over the matched single-agent baseline. Second, from tier 4 onward, collaboration starts to matter, and gains reach roughly 10 to 20 points on tiers 6 to 9. Third, in that regime, broadcast outperforms PER in final accuracy.

The most informative numbers are the process metrics. PER’s reviewer has higher precision than broadcast’s reviewer, 0.861 versus 0.644. But when the reviewer gives critique that the evaluator later confirms as useful, PER changes its carried-forward candidate only 33.6 percent of the time, while broadcast does so 93.5 percent of the time. ReviewerGuidedRepairRate is 0.051 for PER versus 0.286 for broadcast. These numbers strongly support the paper’s central claim: reviewer precision alone does not explain protocol quality if critique uptake is weak.

The appendix-level evidence also matters. A reduced replication with Gemma 3 actors changes the overall ranking of protocols, but the precision-versus-uptake separation remains. That suggests critique uptake is a meaningful evaluation axis beyond one specific model family.8:T592,The method is called blockwise policy-drift gating. For each sampled response token, the paper computes the old-current log-probability shift: the difference between the current student’s log-probability and the behavior student’s log-probability on the same sampled token. This is not a full KL estimate; it is a sampled-path drift signal. Using token-level importance ratios such as exp(d_t) is a natural baseline, but the paper argues that it is too noisy. Instead, it aggregates drift at an intermediate granularity. Two variants are used: Block64-Gate, which averages shifts over fixed 64-token blocks, and NewlineSpan-Gate, which aggregates over newline-delimited spans with heuristics for merging or splitting spans by length. The aggregated score is passed through a detached soft gate and mean-normalized over valid response tokens, then broadcast back to all positions in the region as a multiplicative weight on the existing OPD position loss. Three design choices matter. First, the gate is detached, so the model cannot optimize the gate itself; it only changes how much each position contributes to the base loss. Second, teacher targets are unchanged, so the same gating family can be applied both to sampled-token OPD and to Teacher-TopK/LSM. Third, blockwise aggregation is a variance-locality trade-off: sequence-level weighting is too coarse for long traces, while token-level weighting is too unstable.9:T48d,The method builds on MathNeurosurgery.
