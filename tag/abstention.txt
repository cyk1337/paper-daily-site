18:T4fa,The study combines linear separability, geometric comparison, and causal steering.
- Data: matched answerable/unanswerable pairs in two formally checkable domains (math800, code800). fact800 (epistemic unanswerability) and FalseQA (false premise) are used only as boundary/transfer checks.
- Impossibility direction d_imp: at a chosen layer L, they build a 1D direction using a low-capacity probe variant (CosNSRT/GSRS-style). They fit PCA on answerable states (k=100), project all states onto the orthogonal residual space R to remove dominant answerable variance, then compute a mean-difference direction between A and U in that residual. Scoring uses cosine similarity and orientation-invariant AUC.
- Safety refusal direction d_ref,safety: reproduced from Arditi et al. (2024) as μ_harmful − μ_harmless, with behavioral verification.
- Geometry: measure cosine(d_imp, d_ref,safety) to test whether structural invalidity reuses the safety-refusal pathway.
- Causality: steer generation by injecting along ±d_imp and measure dose-responsive changes in invalidity-aware behavior, with random-direction controls.
- Origin analysis: base vs instruct model pairs to see whether the near-orthogonality is created by instruction tuning or already present in the base model.19:T4ff,The method, DPQ (Doubt-Preserving Quantization), is a family of pre-quantization calibration-data recipes rather than a new quantizer. The pipeline is lightweight. First, run the full-precision model on candidate examples. Then select target-aware 'fragile' examples using FP uncertainty signals. Two signal families matter most: high-doubt examples, such as high entropy, low confidence, or small top-2 margin; and answerability-boundary examples, especially for answer/no-answer settings like SQuAD2. These are mixed with generic anchor examples and then passed to an unchanged quantizer such as GPTQ or AWQ. The theoretical argument is modest but useful: if the deployment target puts more mass near decision boundaries, while the calibration mixture is dominated by generic anchors, the optimal calibration set will differ from the one preferred by broad MCQA-style targets. The paper calls this mixture mismatch. The framing is built around two preservation risks: a distributional risk that measures deviation between quantized and FP predictive distributions, and a boundary risk that measures decision flips in low-margin regions, optionally reweighted toward answerability or abstention-heavy inputs. This is a better fit for real systems than accuracy-only calibration.1a:T539,The evaluation spans 8 language models, 9 NLP benchmarks, and 22 comparison methods or configurations. The metric suite is broader than standard quantization papers: besides ECE, adaptive ECE, NLL, and Brier score, the authors report FP-behavior metrics such as FP agreement, JSD between quantized and FP predictive distributions, confidence shift, top-two margin drift, and margin correlation. On SQuAD2 they also evaluate answerability-boundary behavior directly. Three findings stand out. First, the best calibration recipe depends strongly on the target. For boundary-sensitive answerability preservation, a high-boundary mixture such as DPQ-s128-r75 works best. For broad MCQA, milder recipes such as DPQ-r50 or single-signal confidence-only / entropy-only selection are often better. Second, accuracy can hide substantial uncertainty drift: some settings preserve top-1 behavior while materially changing confidence and margin structure. Third, preservation signals transfer only partially across quantizers. The post-hoc calibration and AWQ analyses suggest that some useful signals are not tied to one quantizer, but the best score-preservation recipe still remains quantizer-specific. The paper is less about one headline average gain and more about establishing the robustness of this target dependence across models and tasks.1b:T5ad,First, it formalizes the core collapse mechanism: when abstention is implemented as a discrete Bernoulli-gated action, both the reward gradient with respect to gate parameters and the restoring force of the forward KL anchor (the variant used in standard RLHF) share the same sigmoid saturation factor σ'(v) from the gate logit. Three necessary conditions for collapse are identified: blanket answering has negative expected score (the exact scenario the penalty rule targets), all prompts share a bounded readout head, and abstention is treated as an action-level choice. It then derives a finite-time collapse law: mean training reward converges to 0 at a 1/t rate (up to log factors) independent of anchor strength β. It further analyzes how advantage estimators modify the law: group normalization steepens decay to 1/t² below an answer rate knee of ~1/G (G = group size) and silently replaces the designed penalty λ with an effective λ_eff=1; dynamic resampling preserves collapse magnitude per rollout. The repair moves abstention out of the action space entirely: during training, the model does not choose to answer or abstain, but is required to output a continuous confidence report trained with a strictly proper scoring rule plus a correctness reward; thresholding for abstention is applied only at deployment. In stage two, the model sees its earlier answer and decides whether to commit it to a user or abstain. This setup allows the same stage-one confidence signal to be evaluated against two targets: objective correctness and later commit/abstain behavior.
