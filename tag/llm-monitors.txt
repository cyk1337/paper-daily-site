1a:T65c,The paper builds a systematic binary-classification setup around Qwen3 8B, 32B, and 235B. Training uses LoRA rather than full fine-tuning, with rank 32 and alpha 32 adapters applied to all attention and MLP projection layers. The supervised objective is masked SFT: all tokens except the final classification token are masked out of the loss, so the optimization signal is concentrated on the classification decision itself. Evaluation is done in two modes. In 1-token classification, the model outputs 0 or 1, and the score is computed from the normalized probability of 1. In thinking classification, the model reasons first and then outputs a confidence score from 0 to 100. The datasets include Chemical harm (50k), Cyber harm (50k), ControlArena (4k), IMDB (25k), and Covert Reasoning (1.5k). Two design choices are especially important. First, the authors separate adjacent-domain transfer from same-domain, new-prompt transfer; the former tests semantic transfer, while the latter tests whether the model is actually following the prompt. Second, they compare ways of combining classification data with general instruction-following data. Simple mixing works poorly because classification examples contribute very few unmasked tokens, so their training signal is diluted by ordinary instruction data. A better strategy is stagewise training: do classification tuning first, then instruction tuning. This preserves more of the classification gain while reducing prompt-following failures. The result is a useful reminder that data mixture should be analyzed in terms of effective loss contribution, not just sample counts.1b:T5e6,There are four main empirical takeaways. First, transfer across adjacent domains is real. A representative result is that training on 'is this cyber harm?' recovers about 75% of the improvement that would be obtained by directly training on the target task 'does the AI do anything dangerous?'. Second, when in-domain data is scarce, adding data from an adjacent domain still helps, even under strong dataset imbalance. Third, there are clear generalization failures: after single-task training, the model can apply the old classification rule to similar transcripts even when the prompt has changed. In other words, it sometimes behaves like a domain-conditioned labeler rather than a prompt-conditioned classifier. Fourth, adding instruction-following training mitigates this, but naive mixing is worse than stagewise training because the classification objective is underweighted in token-level loss. The generative-transfer results are also notable: for Qwen3-8B, training only on single-token cyber-harm classification raises thinking-classification AUC from 0.84 to 0.94. The same single-token training also improves dangerous-action detection when the model is used as a summarizer. The paper further explores an unsupervised elicitation loop where predictions from thinking classification are used to generate new single-token training targets. Metrics are mainly AUROC, with logAUC used for stronger classifiers to emphasize the low-FPR regime from 10^-3 to 10^-1, which is closer to deployment needs.1c:T440,For a quick but useful read, start with the experimental setup: understand the difference between 1-token and thinking classification, the masked-loss training scheme, and the relationships among the datasets. If you mainly care about engineering implications, read the experiments on instruction-data mixing and stage ordering next; about 30 minutes should be enough to extract the main lessons. Second, why single-token training transfers to reasoning-style classification, which hints at changes beyond output formatting.
