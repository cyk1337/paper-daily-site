The second component is trigger steganography, denoted TS(·). Given an arbitrary target caption z, TS maps z into a stealthy visual trigger, either as norm-controlled noise or as a semantically meaningless patch. The key idea is dynamic trigger synthesis: the trigger is generated from the desired target text at inference time, rather than being fixed during poisoning. First, under a log-linear policy class, the authors show that flipping a single preference label induces a fixed shift in the DPO gradient that does not depend on the current parameter. This is the key simplification. It turns poisoning from a trajectory-dependent optimization in policy space into a subset-selection problem in gradient space, where each example contributes one dictionary atom corresponding to its flip-induced gradient shift.

Second, they show that controlling the residual in this gradient-space approximation is a sufficient surrogate for attack success in policy space. This reduces targeted poisoning to a structured binary sparse approximation problem: coefficients are binary because each label is either flipped or not; the goal is either to minimize the number of flips or to find the best attack under a budget K.

Third, they propose two algorithms. BAL-A targets the minimum-flip setting. The paper analyzes how the embedding penalty controls the solution and gives a separation condition under which the true minimum-flip attack is recovered. BMP-A targets the sparse fixed-budget setting. It adapts Binary Matching Pursuit, using normalized correlations to handle non-uniform column norms in the gradient dictionary, and derives coherence-based recovery guarantees.
