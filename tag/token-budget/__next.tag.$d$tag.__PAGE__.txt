9:T5c6,The method builds stop/continue labels from completed reasoning traces. For each trace, it samples candidate prefix boundaries at paragraph breaks, sentence endings, and positions before reflection phrases such as “Wait” or “Alternatively”, with at most 40 boundaries per trace. For each prefix, it appends a closing tag and a final-answer prompt, then greedily decodes a short answer of up to 16 tokens. The terminal reasoning prefix is read out in the same way. A boundary is labeled stable if every later readout is mathematically equivalent to the terminal readout. If any later readout changes, the boundary remains a continue target, even when the current answer happens to be correct. Equivalence is checked by normalized string comparison followed by Math-Verify symbolic checking.

Training directly supervises the probability of the existing </think> token at labeled boundaries. Stable boundaries receive stop targets; earlier boundaries receive continue targets. Other response positions are regularized with a KL penalty toward the frozen base model distribution. Boundary positions are excluded from the KL term. This differs from full-sequence SFT: SFT learns both a particular shortened reasoning continuation and a stopping point, while Settle only trains the stopping decision and treats all positions in the stable suffix as valid opportunities to stop. At inference time, </think> simply competes with other vocabulary tokens under ordinary decoding.a:T85b,The paper formulates recursive social improvement as an RL problem over a population of N agents. Agent i faces its own task distribution D_i and is rewarded only on its own tasks. Each round has two decisions. First, the agent chooses a learning action: search privately, observe a peer, or abstain. Second, it chooses a task action by executing a skill from its current library. A skill can be a simple option in a controlled environment or a full skill file in the LLM setting.

A central design choice is token accounting. Search, observation, and acting all draw from the same budget, and the cost of discovering a copied skill is counted as part of the total social-learning cost. This avoids the common mistake of treating copied knowledge as free.

The experiments proceed in two stages. In the controlled stage, options have known value structure, allowing the authors to test whether established social-learning algorithms can in principle yield an advantage. In the LLM stage, agents write, revise, and exchange skill files for instruction-following tasks. These skill files are inference-time textual procedures rather than weight updates. Policies are implemented by fixed-weight LLMs learning in context: the model decides whether to revise its own skill, copy a peer’s skill, or act.

The paper also makes an important distinction between two notions of success. Efficient social learning means better performance per token. Effective social learning means higher final performance than independent learners at the same total cost. This distinction is one of the strongest parts of the paper because many multi-agent results show speed or cost gains without showing superiority under matched compute.

The authors further test simple interventions such as reserving tokens for acting and requiring some private search. These do not resolve the core issue. Another notable analysis tracks skill lineage over time. Copied skills are often revised again, so the process is not mere imitation. But most final skills end up tracing back to only one or two early revisions, indicating rapid lineage collapse and path dependence.b:T6d7,The empirical picture is consistent and informative. In the controlled environment, a hand-designed social-learning policy does achieve a social advantage. That shows the setting itself does not rule out social learning. The failure appears when LLMs are used as the policy controllers.

Across tested LLMs, populations with peer observation are 30% to 52% less efficient than solo populations of the same model. In other words, useful social information exists, but the models do not manage it well enough; they reduce exploration too aggressively.

In the skill-file experiments, peer access changes learning speed and cost but not the matched-cost conclusion. With peer access, GLM-5.3-Flash achieves on average 1.3 percentage points higher held-out accuracy over the course of learning. GPT-OSS-120B uses about one-third fewer learning tokens. These are real benefits, but they are benefits in trajectory shape or efficiency, not in final performance at equal token cost. When learning tokens are matched, neither model shows a clear advantage over independent learners.

The lineage analysis is especially useful. Skills are copied and then revised again, so there is genuine recursive improvement. But by the end, most agents’ skills descend from only one or two of the population’s earliest revisions. This explains the main negative result: copied options often beat what an agent found alone at that moment, yet population-level exploration collapses, so the group does not surpass independent search overall.

The paper is careful not to oversell a benchmark win. Its strongest evidence comes from matched-token comparisons that separate “social learning looks smarter” from “social learning is actually better under equal cost.”c:T4bf,A good reading order is: formal problem setup, controlled environment results, LLM skill-file experiments, then lineage analysis.
