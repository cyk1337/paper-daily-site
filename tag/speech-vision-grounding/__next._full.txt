18:T51f,This is a benchmark paper, not a training-method paper. The design has three important pieces. First, the taxonomy: the authors split speech-vision hallucination into semantic and temporal dimensions. Semantic hallucination asks whether the content mentioned in speech truly corresponds to visible evidence. Temporal hallucination asks whether the model can identify when the narrated event occurs relative to the speaking moment: past, present, or future. Second, each dimension is probed with three complementary tasks arranged from coarse to fine, all cast in a unified QA format. Questions are mostly binary or multiple choice to support stable scoring and statistical analysis. This matters because the benchmark is not only measuring final accuracy; it is trying to localize failure modes. Third, the dataset construction is centered on speech content–visual evidence relations rather than generic narrated videos. The benchmark includes cases where speech describes the current scene, recalls past events, foreshadows future events, or contains weakly related or misleading commentary. That design prevents the task from collapsing into ASR or generic video QA. The paper’s framing is clear: if a model mainly transcribes speech and answers from language priors, SVHalluc should expose that weakness.19:T475,The main empirical result is straightforward: current open audio-visual LLMs perform poorly on SVHalluc, often close to random guessing, while Gemini 2.5 Pro is substantially stronger but still imperfect. The evaluated models include open-source systems such as Qwen3-Omni and the commercial Gemini 2.5 Pro. The analysis argues that the bottleneck is not simply speech perception. Models may do reasonably well on single-modality tasks like speech recognition, yet fail once the question requires joint reasoning over spoken content and visual evidence. In other words, the failure is often 'heard it but did not ground it,' not merely 'did not hear it.' The experiments are structured more as failure diagnosis than leaderboard reporting: they compare semantic versus temporal difficulty, and examine whether models over-trust speech priors while ignoring visual contradiction.
