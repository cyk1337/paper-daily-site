18:T5cf,The core method is a preregistered multiverse analysis. Instead of choosing one analysis pipeline, the authors enumerate a large grid of defensible specifications, with every setting taken from published implementations rather than invented for the study.

A specification includes multiple analytic axes: discovery objective, ablation operator, corruption distribution, evaluation metric, size threshold, prompt variant, seed, and granularity. The testbed is GPT-2 small on the indirect object identification task, using factorized-graph circuit discovery. Each specification returns a circuit C(s), a subset of model edges.

The key move is that the paper does not stop at graph overlap. It introduces a deterministic claim map φ that converts each discovered circuit into a structured Annex IV-style statement. This shifts the evaluation target from “do the circuits look similar?” to “would the resulting regulatory explanation be the same?” The paper then defines flip rate, modal share π*, and conditional within-group variants, and proposes a filability criterion: evidence is filable at tolerance α only if π* ≥ 1 − α.

A second methodological contribution is statistical. When different specifications produce circuits of different sizes, pooled stability statistics can reverse or mislead. The authors therefore analyze pooled results, size-controlled results, and claim mappings with size information removed. That point generalizes beyond this specific task.19:T56c,The experimental sweep is large: 15,840 preregistered specifications on GPT-2 small and IOI, of which 7,561 produced a claim through the deterministic map.

The main findings are:
1. Claim instability is high. The reported flip rate is 0.7316. With 9 observed claim classes, the theoretical ceiling is 0.8889, so the observed value is 82.3% of the maximum possible. In practical terms, two defensible specifications often yield different regulatory statements.
2. The modal share is too low to satisfy the proposed filability criterion under reasonable tolerances, meaning current circuit evidence would struggle to qualify as stable filing material.
3. The instability survives aggressive controls. It remains even when one analytic axis is standardized, when circuit size is removed from the claim map, and when circuit size is held fixed.
4. The discovered circuits are not random noise. At fixed circuit size, they are more stable than a size-matched random null: 0.2746 versus 0.4230. But functional instability is still substantial: 0.3972 pooled and 0.4777 for the smallest circuits. Cohen’s kappa is only 0.0146, indicating that circuits found under different specifications are functionally almost uncorrelated.

Taken together, the evidence supports a restrained conclusion: circuit discovery contains signal, but in its current form it does not provide audit-grade stability.1a:T514,Do not read this one from the abstract alone. Start with the introduction and especially the section titled “What this paper is not claiming.” The authors are careful about scope, and that section prevents the common misreading that the paper is claiming mechanistic interpretability is useless.

Then read the formalism closely. Three objects matter most: the specification tuple, the deterministic claim map φ, and the filability criterion. The novelty is less about a new discovery algorithm and more about connecting analytic variation to the stability of downstream explanatory claims.

In the experiments, prioritize:
1. the seven analytic axes and where each setting comes from;
2. flip rate, modal share, and within-group stability metrics;
3. robustness checks with one axis fixed, circuit size controlled, and size removed from claims;
4. comparisons to random nulls and the kappa analysis.

If the goal is only the main message, about 45 minutes is enough.
