More interestingly, in the large-n limit the same mechanism implements a KL-regularized alignment optimum: the induced selection rule becomes a softmax policy over rewards. The paper then connects this regularization strength to the regret skyline from prior BoN theory, showing that when the privacy budget exceeds a critical threshold ε*(x), the privacy-imposed noise is exactly in the regret-optimal range. In that regime, privacy is effectively free from an alignment-regret perspective.

The second is Private Inference-Time Pessimism (PrivITP). PrivBoN depends on an unknown coverage coefficient C^{π*}, so the favorable threshold cannot be verified in practice. PrivITP addresses this by combining χ²-regularized rejection sampling with a two-phase Gaussian mechanism. The key move is not simply adding Gaussian noise, but reformulating a ReLU-based rejection sampler as a randomized-threshold mechanism. That yields an ex-post approximate-DP guarantee whose bound is independent of the candidate count n and depends only on the realized halting time. The regret analysis uses a smoothed-weight policy comparison lemma that controls value gaps through the ℓ∞ distance between policy weight functions, leading to a bound of the form O(sqrt(C^{π*}(ε_RM^2 + R_max σ))). This cleanly separates reward-model error from privacy cost and explains why PrivITP is more robust when coverage is unknown.

A notable design choice is that the paper does not solve the problem by retraining the reward model with DP or by using heuristic smoothing. It treats inference-time selection itself as the object of study and chooses mechanisms whose stochasticity is analytically tied to both privacy and alignment.1a:T5b6,The empirical study spans four reward models, two language models of different sizes, and three benchmark families: GSM8K, MMLU, and MATH. 1. Standard BoN is not scaling-monotonic under reward-model error. As N increases, performance can peak and then degrade, which is the practical signature of reward hacking.
2. PrivBoN mitigates this failure mode. With theoretically calibrated Gumbel noise, the performance curve as a function of N becomes much more stable, and the method preserves the benefit of test-time compute without over-amplifying reward-model error.
3. PrivITP consistently matches or outperforms PrivBoN at comparable privacy levels, especially in regimes where large candidate sets make BoN more brittle.

The optimization objective is standard GRPO: for each prompt, sample a group of completions, score them with a reward, compute group-relative advantages, and update the policy with a clipped objective plus a KL penalty to a reference policy. The main experimental variables are the reward semantics and the starting policy.

Four reward designs are compared:
- R0-Lex: lexical suppression. This penalizes target-related words or phrases. It is easy to implement but easy to game through paraphrase, indirect leakage, or refusal.
- R1-AntiRefusal: lexical suppression plus anti-refusal shaping. This explicitly tries to block the common shortcut where the model learns to refuse everything.
- R2-Rubric: a rubric-based reward that shifts the objective from “do not say the target content” to “answer helpfully at a broader topic level without leaking.” This is the most behaviorally aligned reward in the study and the conceptual center of the paper.
- R4-Refusal: an explicit refusal reward used as a diagnostic contrast. It is not meant as the desired endpoint; it is there to test whether benchmarks can mistake refusal-heavy behavior for successful unlearning.
