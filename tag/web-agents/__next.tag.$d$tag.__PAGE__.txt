9:T59d,The setup is intentionally minimal. The authors compare three existing online augmentation methods—AWM, ASI, and ReasoningBank—against a budget-matched vanilla baseline called Vanilla-IB. The augmented agents keep the default actor horizon of up to 10 steps, while spending additional tokens on workflow induction, skill extraction, memory retrieval, reasoning-bank updates, and related control logic. Vanilla-IB removes all auxiliary modules and reallocates the same overall budget to a longer actor interaction horizon.

That design choice is the core contribution. The paper does not propose a new agent architecture; it proposes a stricter control condition. Instead of comparing “augmented agent vs plain actor with a smaller budget,” it compares “augmented agent vs plain actor with the same total token budget.” The study is explicitly online: tasks are processed sequentially, and any reusable knowledge must be accumulated from earlier evaluation trajectories rather than built offline in advance. This makes the trade-off sharper, because online augmentation is sensitive to task order and cannot amortize its cost over a precompiled skill library.

The paper’s methodological contribution is therefore an evaluation protocol with three requirements: report total token usage across all modules, compare methods under matched budgets, and report multi-run variance rather than relying on a single aggregate score.a:T572,The main benchmark is WebArena, evaluated on three domains: Shopping (187 tasks), Reddit (106 tasks), and Admin (182 tasks). The study uses three base models: Gemini 3 Flash, GPT-5.4-mini, and Qwen 3.6-27B. Across these settings, the headline result is consistent: Vanilla-IB achieves the best aggregate success rate overall while often using fewer total tokens than AWM, ASI, and ReasoningBank. In other words, once auxiliary token overhead is counted, spending the budget on more direct interaction is usually better than spending it on online memory or skill machinery.

The authors also test the same question on WorkArena-L1, a 33-task ServiceNow benchmark covering enterprise workflows such as dashboards, forms, knowledge-base search, filtering, sorting, menu navigation, and service-catalog requests. With Qwen 3.6-27B, Vanilla-IB remains competitive with the best augmented methods, suggesting the budget-matching issue is not specific to consumer-style websites.

A second important result concerns variance. The paper argues that single-run success rates hide substantial task-level variability, especially in online settings where later performance depends on earlier trajectories. While the provided text does not include the full numeric tables, the qualitative conclusion is clear: single-run aggregate comparisons are too weak to support strong claims about online augmentation.b:T5ec,The central concept is the Minimal Failure Set (MFS). Starting from successful trajectories, the authors intervene on a given HTML observation by removing pieces of information and asking which minimal subset causes the task to fail when removed. HTML is decomposed into fine-grained units of the form (element id, attr), where attr can be a concrete attribute, @tag, or @text. This is more informative than node-level ablation because it separates the role of text content, tag identity, and attribute values.

Once an MFS is constructed for each step, the paper defines coverage: whether a reduction method fully retains the MFS for that instance, aggregated over the dataset. Coverage is proposed as a proxy for end-to-end success rate. The practical advantage is large: after MFS construction, evaluation requires neither web access nor policy-model inference, and instances can be scored independently in parallel.

Using this framework, the authors compare 11 classes of reduction methods, including heuristic pruning, retrieval-based selection, LLM-guided selection, and accessibility-tree variants. They then use coverage as an optimization objective and apply GEPA, an LLM-based evolutionary optimization framework, to learn benchmark-specific pruning programs. The resulting message is fairly clear: generic LLM-based reducers can preserve more critical information, but their own latency is often too high; benchmark-specific program optimization is a better route if the goal is lower total step latency.c:T634,The experiments are centered on validating coverage as a proxy and quantifying the real trade-off between preservation quality and latency.

1. The motivation is backed by a concrete cost number: on WorkArena L1, evaluating 11 methods across 32 configurations and 33 tasks with Qwen3.5-122B-A10B took 232.4 cumulative hours. That makes exhaustive end-to-end comparison impractical.

2. On both WorkArena L1 and WebLinx, coverage is shown to correlate strongly with end-to-end success rate, while enabling more than 100x speedup in cumulative evaluation time. If this correlation is robust, it makes offline screening of reduction methods much more feasible.

3. In the method comparison, LLM inference-based reducers achieve higher coverage than retrieval-based methods, especially under aggressive reduction, but they incur substantially higher latency. So better reduction quality does not necessarily translate into lower system latency.

4. The paper also finds benchmark-specific differences in what information matters: text content is more critical on WebLinx, whereas HTML tags and attributes matter more on WorkArena L1. This explains why a single generic reduction policy may not transfer well.

5. Using GEPA to optimize pruning programs on benchmark-specific MFS training data, the authors report 2.2x faster per-step latency on WorkArena L1 while retaining 84% of the original success rate, and 3.1x faster on WebLinx while retaining 89%.
