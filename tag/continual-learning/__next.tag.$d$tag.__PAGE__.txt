The paper also gives a statistical argument: if one reuses screening evidence to accept among multiple branches, one pays a union-bound penalty over branches and can easily mistake selection noise for genuine improvement. EvoHeir tackles a deeper issue: whole-state acceptance bundles gains and losses together. A branch may acquire new skills while also degrading old ones, but standard inheritance forces an all-or-nothing choice. EvoHeir splits the round into screening, recovery, and confirmation. Screening chooses the recipient that will supply the next learner’s parameters, from either a branch endpoint or the current root. Recovery then repairs that recipient’s failures using reference solutions drawn from both the current round and an archive of past qualified experience, while trying to preserve existing successes. Confirmation finally applies a HeirCommit-style fresh comparison between the post-recovery proposal and the root. A notable design choice is that qualified experience enters the archive regardless of whether the donor model is inherited. Parameter inheritance can stop without discarding potentially useful experience.

The key detail is that SST projects the applied parameter update, not the raw gradient. That distinction matters because stateful optimizers such as Adam transform gradients through momentum and coordinate-wise rescaling; a projected gradient does not guarantee a projected final step. By acting on the actual update written into the weights, SST becomes optimizer-agnostic.

Conceptually, SST freezes the high-energy spectral structure and pushes adaptation into the tail subspace.

If the SVD basis is computed once and never updated, the allowed complement can become too restrictive as training progresses. SST therefore includes a refresh mechanism that periodically recomputes the SVD on the current weights. This trades off stricter preservation against continued adaptability: no refresh gives stronger protection, while refresh allows the protected subspace to track the evolving model and frees capacity for further learning.

Methodologically, SST is positioned as a composable constraint rather than a standalone fine-tuning paradigm. It can be combined with full-parameter tuning, SDFT-style recipes, and different optimizers such as Adam or Muon.13:T97c,The empirical section is broad and is designed to test the central claim from several angles: can the model learn new tasks, retain prior capabilities, stay behaviorally close to the base model, and accumulate skills across stages? The domains include Science, Medicine, Pathology, Finance, and Tool Use. The paper evaluates both standard SFT and SDFT, and tests compatibility with Adam and Muon.

The headline result is that SST can preserve an extremely large fraction of principal singular directions while still matching or nearly matching unconstrained fine-tuning on target-task performance. The paper explicitly states that even when protecting the top 99% singular directions, SST still learns the new task, whereas standard SFT causes substantial forgetting. This is strong evidence that the non-principal spectral subspace contains enough adaptation capacity in overparameterized LLMs.

Scaling trends are also important. As model size increases, forgetting under SST shrinks further; from 8B onward, retained performance stays within roughly 1% of the base model, while unconstrained SFT degrades much more. The data-scaling result is equally telling: with more fine-tuning data, standard SFT forgets progressively more, but SST remains comparatively flat while still improving target-task accuracy. That suggests SST is not merely “learning less.”

The paper also goes beyond task accuracy. SST reduces KL divergence from the pretrained model by 2–4× relative to unconstrained recipes. On thinking models, standard SFT collapses natural reasoning length, while SST preserves the original reasoning-length behavior much better. When added to SDFT, SST also reduces fabricated reference-solution attributions, indicating better behavioral fidelity.

The sequential fine-tuning experiments are especially relevant for continual learning. In two-stage and three-stage chains, skills learned earlier remain largely intact under SST as later stages are added, whereas standard SFT degrades dormant skills whenever a new stage is trained. This is closer to the real deployment setting than one-shot forgetting tests.

The most explicit numbers visible from the provided content are: 2–4× lower KL, forgetting within about 1% from 8B onward, and successful learning even while protecting the top 99% singular directions.
