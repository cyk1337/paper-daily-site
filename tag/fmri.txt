1a:T899,The method has two parts. The first measures model-brain correspondence during deductive reasoning. Using task-fMRI data focused on brain regions previously associated with deductive reasoning, the authors extract hidden representations from several open-source LLMs while the models process reasoning problems. They then compute neural predictivity, asking how much explainable variance in the fMRI responses can be accounted for by model representations. Two design choices matter here. One is the separation between aggregate predictivity across all reasoning samples and predictivity within specific reasoning types; the former is much higher than the latter, indicating that the shared structure is coarse rather than mechanism-complete. The other is the use of controls, including untrained models and non-reasoning brain networks, to show that the effect is not a trivial artifact of generic high-dimensional similarity.

The second part uses neural data for intervention. The paper proposes Neural Activation guided Representation Intervention (NARI), an inference-time method, and Neural Activation guided Representation Fine-tuning (NARF), a parameter-update variant. NARI identifies directions in a joint model-brain representational structure that are associated with incorrect responses but can be corrected using neural guidance, then steers intermediate hidden states away from error-prone regions and toward representations associated with correct reasoning. This resembles activation steering, but the steering signal is not derived purely from model-internal statistics; it is constrained by the shared structure between model states and brain activity. NARF pushes the same idea into training by adjusting model parameters so that the model is more likely to form internally correct reasoning representations.

A notable methodological choice is that the authors do not try to regress full fMRI patterns as a global supervision target. Given the noise, limited sample size, and subject variability of fMRI, that would likely overfit irrelevant variance. Instead, they frame the problem as error correction in a shared subspace, which is a more realistic use of the available signal.1b:T5a7,The main findings come in four groups. First, on representation alignment, LLM representations explain about 76% of the explainable variance in aggregate neural responses from deductive-reasoning regions, substantially above untrained-model controls. However, when evaluated within specific reasoning types, predictivity drops to roughly 27%, showing that fine-grained alignment remains limited. Second, the alignment is selective: it is stronger for the reasoning network than for the multiple-demand network or the core language network, supporting the claim that the effect is not reducible to generic language processing. Third, both NARI and NARF can convert a subset of previously incorrect model answers into correct ones, indicating that neural signals are useful not only descriptively but also operationally. The truncated abstract does not expose the full result tables, but the body text indicates that gains are concentrated on harder examples and on reasoning categories where the base model is brittle. Fourth, the paper includes controls suggesting that the improvements are not explained by extra test-time compute, prompt changes, or generic smoothing of hidden states.

One useful experimental lesson is that high global neural predictivity does not automatically imply high reasoning accuracy, whereas localized neural guidance can still improve error cases. That makes the paper more interesting than a pure correlation study.1c:T4b0,For a skim, focus on three parts.
