First, it localizes proxy misalignment to the candidate-selection stage of discrete search, rather than the more familiar RLHF or preference-optimization setting. That perspective is useful anywhere a proxy objective drives search, data synthesis, or iterative refinement. Second, it shows that keeping the proposer fixed and changing only the selector can materially change the final outcome. That is a useful reminder for training and search systems: what matters is not the candidate pool alone, but the trajectory induced by the retention rule. Third, it offers a practical recipe for situations where no true reward is available: combine trajectory-aware proxies with reference regularization to reduce short-sighted optimization. The contribution is more mechanistic than foundational, but it is worth reading if you care about alignment optimization, synthetic-data search, or proxy-objective failure modes.a:T8b8,TACS leaves candidate generation unchanged and intervenes only in candidate selection. At step t, a standard gradient-based proposer generates a batch of suffix candidates from the current suffix. The baseline strategy evaluates each candidate with the current attack loss and keeps the lowest-loss one. TACS replaces this with a richer scoring rule built from three kinds of signals.

The first is the standard current-step quality term, L_now, typically the target-token cross-entropy over the training behavior set. This remains in the score because immediate optimization quality still matters.

The second is trajectory-aware information. From the provided text, two components are central. One is an auxiliary early-token loss, denoted as something like L_first, computed only on the beginning of the target response. The motivation is intuitive: jailbreak failure often reappears at the start of the answer, where refusal templates or safety scaffolds come back first. A candidate that already supports the early part of the harmful response may therefore be more promising than one with a slightly lower average loss. The other is a one-step improvement proxy, Δ1(a), intended to estimate whether a candidate leaves room for useful future progress rather than merely fitting the current step. Even without the full formula, the design intent is clear: score candidates by downstream search utility, not just current proxy fit.

The third ingredient is reference regularization. Inspired by ideas from ORPO-style regularized optimization, TACS stabilizes selection by comparing against a reference signal or reference distribution. The role here is not alignment in the usual sense, but preventing the selector from making overly aggressive moves based on noisy or misleading local proxy improvements.

An important practical point is that TACS does not require extra intermediate model queries and does not assume access to a true reward. It only reuses signals already available in standard suffix optimization. Relative to prior jailbreak work, the novelty is in the selector rather than the proposer. Relative to ordinary reranking, the target is not better single-step ranking but better multi-step search trajectories.b:T506,The main empirical claim is that, under the same search budget, changing only the candidate selector improves average attack success rate on HarmBench by nearly 10%. That pattern suggests current-step loss is a poor retention criterion. With TACS, the optimization trajectory becomes more stable and the ASR gains are more consistent, rather than depending on occasional lucky suffix updates.
