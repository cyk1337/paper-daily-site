Each looped model stores 28 Transformer blocks but executes a logical path of 112 block applications. The Ouro-style baseline is R28^4: a 28-block stack repeated four times. The Huginn-style model is P8 R12^8 C8: an 8-layer prelude, a 12-layer tied recurrent core repeated eight times, and an 8-layer coda, again totaling 112 logical block executions. Two feedforward anchors are used: D28, matching resident parameters, and D112, matching logical depth with independent parameters. The ablations show that the sandwich envelope gives the clearest gains, especially on math, context extraction, and reasoning-heavy tasks. Input injection improves knowledge retrieval and prompt-context utilization, but can hurt some quantitative reasoning. Random initialization is not consistently useful, and separate high/low recurrent states add complexity without dependable improvements. The full Huginn design outperforms Ouro on all ten dense benchmarks. In MoE experiments matched by resident and active parameters, Huginn again beats Ouro, balances expert load better, uses different experts across loop iterations, and approaches a larger feedforward MoE while storing far fewer parameters.19:T781,The paper first analyzes token-level refinement dynamics in a looped Transformer. The architecture has untied prelude and coda stacks, with a middle block of k layers reused for d recurrences, so parameter depth is p+k+l while realized compute depth is p+kd+l. The main experiments use a Huginn-style configuration such as (2, 4⊗32, 2).

To characterize instability, the authors define trace entropy, which measures uncertainty along the loop-wise reasoning trajectory for each token. Incorrect reasoning traces tend to have higher trace entropy. Using a logit-lens analysis, they then identify two token behaviors. Easy tokens improve monotonically across iterations, with growing top-1 vs top-2 margins. Hard tokens do not: they often become confident too early and then attempt revision later, or show unstable refinement patterns. These hard tokens account for only about 10%–15% of the reasoning trace but appear to be disproportionately important.

LoopCD builds on this observation. Standard contrastive decoding uses logits from an expert model and an amateur model. LoopCD replaces that external contrast with an internal one: logits from the final loop iteration serve as the refined distribution, while logits from earlier iterations provide the contrastive signal. The intuition is that later iterations are usually more refined, but earlier iterations may preserve useful intermediate evidence before unstable late-stage refinement distorts it. The final sampling distribution is formed by contrasting these loop-wise logits.

A practical advantage is that no extra model is needed and almost no extra forward cost is incurred, because the intermediate hidden states and logits are already produced during looping. The paper also argues that the gains are concentrated on hard tokens rather than spread uniformly across the sequence, which makes the method more targeted than generic decoding heuristics.1a:T5e4,The paper evaluates LoopCD on arithmetic reasoning, code generation, and question answering, and reports consistent gains over standard decoding and stronger decoding baselines. The excerpt provided does not include the full main-table numbers, but several experimental findings are central.

First, trace entropy is strongly associated with reasoning quality. On GSM8K, incorrect reasoning traces show systematically higher entropy than correct ones, suggesting that loop instability leaves a measurable uncertainty signature.

Second, hard tokens are rare but important. They make up roughly 10%–15% of the reasoning trace, yet random replacement experiments in Table 1 show that perturbing hard tokens hurts accuracy much more than perturbing easy tokens. This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in the paper because it moves beyond correlation and shows that these tokens are closer to the actual bottleneck.

Third, the gains from LoopCD are concentrated on hard tokens. The selective-intervention analysis in Section 5.4 indicates that applying the method to those tokens captures most of the benefit, while intervening on easy tokens yields much smaller returns. That supports the paper’s central mechanism.

Fourth, the method is computationally light. Unlike conventional contrastive decoding, it does not require an auxiliary amateur model. It reuses loop-wise intermediate states already computed by the LoopLM, which is attractive in test-time-compute-heavy latent reasoning settings.1b:T446,Start with the Introduction and Motivation. Figure 2 is the backbone of the paper: panel (a) links trace entropy to correct vs incorrect trajectories, panel (b) visualizes easy vs hard token refinement patterns, and panel (c) connects hard tokens to high-entropy behavior. Those figures make the problem setup and the method intuition clear.

Then read the Method section to understand exactly which loop logits are contrasted and how the final distribution is formed. The key question is not the algebra itself, but why earlier iterations can be useful even when the final iteration is nominally more refined. In the experiments, prioritize Table 1 and the selective-intervention analysis; they are the most informative pieces for judging whether the hard-token hypothesis is real.

A useful extension to think about while reading: if earlier loops contain complementary signal, should training explicitly encourage monotonic refinement, or should the model learn token-wise adaptive stopping instead of using a fixed maximum recurrence depth?
