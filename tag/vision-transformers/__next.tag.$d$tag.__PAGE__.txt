9:T659,The paper has three technical components. First, it performs a broad empirical audit of patch-token decoding across many pretrained ViTs. For classification models, intermediate patch tokens are passed through the final classification head and scored by the true-class logit. For contrastive VLM encoders such as CLIP and SigLIP, patch tokens are passed through the image projection head and compared against a text embedding like “a photo of a C,” producing patch-level similarity maps. These maps are then evaluated by comparing scores inside the object region versus the background, allowing the authors to categorize models into aligned, inverted, or unexplainable regimes. Second, the paper runs controlled synthetic experiments where architecture is held fixed or nearly fixed while task structure, loss, data distribution, and training conditions are varied. The central finding is not that one factor deterministically controls explainability, but that these factors change the optimization bias: they alter which among multiple functionally valid solutions training is more likely to find. Third, the paper provides a theoretical toy construction. It defines a simple classification task and a single-layer transformer for which the optimal classifier can be implemented in infinitely many ways. Some of those parameterizations are aligned under token decoding, while others are inverted. This elevates the empirical observation into a structural statement: even when the implemented function is the same, zero-shot decoding maps can differ radically because internal representations are not uniquely identified.a:T574,The experimental contribution is about breadth and consistency of the phenomenon rather than a single benchmark gain. The authors inspect dozens of pretrained ViTs spanning architectures, scales, and training objectives. The main empirical result is that aligned patch decoding is not universal. Many models exhibit inverted maps, and this is especially common in contrastive vision-language encoders such as CLIP and SigLIP, where object-region patches can systematically receive lower target-class scores than background patches. The controlled synthetic experiments then show that, for the same architecture, changing the task, loss, or data distribution can substantially shift the probability of converging to aligned versus inverted solutions. In other words, training conditions affect basin preference rather than enforcing a unique explainability regime. The paper also emphasizes that models with similar task performance can have very different patch-decoding behavior, supporting the claim that functionally equivalent solutions can induce different readout geometries.
