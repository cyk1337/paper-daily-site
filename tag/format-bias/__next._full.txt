18:T6fc,The technical contribution is an attention decomposition framework for augmented in-context learning. The prompt is written as x=[I;D;K;q], where I is instruction, D demonstrations, K injected knowledge, and q the query. Attention from query tokens to K is decomposed into a semantic component and a structural component. The structural term is written as λ·σ(K), where σ(K) measures structural intensity and λ is a model-intrinsic sensitivity coefficient that can vary by layer and head.

σ(K) is defined from observable prompt statistics: density of structural delimiters, frequency of relation/slot markers, and repetitiveness of local token patterns. The point is not to claim an exact generative theory of attention, but to provide a testable abstraction: even when semantic content is held fixed, more structured formatting should capture more attention.

The empirical design is built around format-content decoupling. The authors compare semantically equivalent content rendered in different forms, including natural-language passages, relevant triples, irrelevant triples, and noise triples. They also derive a compression-style bound showing that, under normalized attention, increased structural intensity in K must reduce the attention available to D.

The mitigation section proposes five structure-aware strategies, all aimed at reducing λ·σ(K). Two are tested empirically. S3, format flattening, rewrites triples into weaker or more natural language-like forms to reduce delimiter and slot salience. S1, structural dispersal, breaks up highly regular local patterns to reduce repetitive structural cues. Other ideas, such as logit suppression and training-time regularization, are presented more as mathematically motivated directions than fully validated methods.19:T6ab,The experiments use two model families, Mistral-7B and LLaMA-3-8B, across three QA tasks. Four results matter most.

First, KG triples capture substantially more attention than neutral natural-language text. The paper reports representative structural intensity estimates around 0.70 for KG-formatted content versus about 0.25 for neutral text, and triples absorb roughly 2x to 3x more attention per token.

Second, this attention capture is largely insensitive to semantic relevance. Relevant triples, irrelevant triples, and even noise triples show very similar attention patterns. That is the central empirical support for the paper’s claim: the tax is driven by format before usefulness is even considered.

Third, demonstration attention is compressed by as much as 42% in some settings. So the effect is not a small local preference; it changes the competitive allocation of context budget inside the prompt and can alter ICL behavior.

Fourth, source alignment dominates gating sophistication. On HotpotQA, task-matched BM25 retrieval outperforms mismatched ConceptNet retrieval by more than 30 percentage points, while more elaborate gating contributes no more than about 2 points. The intended message is that choosing the right knowledge source matters far more than adding control logic on top of a poorly matched source.

For mitigation, S3 format flattening is the strongest result: it improves accuracy and reduces attention concentration on structured regions. S1 structural dispersal is mixed and model-dependent, suggesting that reducing structural regularity does not automatically translate into better task performance, possibly because it also harms readability or local parseability.1a:T4bf,For a skim, focus on three parts.

Start with the introduction and Figure 1. They state the main claim clearly: prompt regions compete for a fixed attention budget, and structured snippets can crowd out demonstrations.

Then read Section 3 and the proposition around the compression argument. The exact formalism is less important than understanding how the authors operationalize structural intensity and why it predicts attention diversion.

Finally, read the seven-condition experiments in Section 5, especially the comparison between relevant triples and noise triples. If those two attract similar attention, the format-driven claim becomes much more convincing. Section 5.3 is also worth reading because it reframes the practical takeaway: source quality matters more than sophisticated gating.

A 30 to 40 minute pass is enough to extract the main idea, evidence, and limitations. The appendix for S3 is worth a quick look.
