The reference model is treated as a common origin in behavioral space, and the allowed drift budget defines a local boundary. Within that neighborhood, drift magnitude determines distance from the origin, while direction determines how the limited behavioral change is allocated across capabilities. This gives a common coordinate system in which heterogeneous updates can be compared by directional efficiency: task improvement per unit of behavioral drift.

The second piece is a coarse but systematic layer-selective probe. Rather than introducing a complicated regularizer, the authors vary which layers are trainable while keeping the data, objective, and optimizer fixed as much as possible. This isolates accessible update directions as the main experimental variable. The result is that several layer subsets provide nearby directions that improve the QA-only target task while preserving reasoning and general capabilities. Full fine-tuning often fails because its large update space includes many easy loss-reducing directions that overwrite existing behaviors. The paper splits constraints into two categories. Soft constraints are subjective and preference-like, such as tone, style, or encouraging phrasing. Hard constraints are objective and programmatically checkable, such as valid JSON, required fields, word-count limits, language requirements, or explicit formatting rules.

Stage 1 uses preference optimization to learn soft constraints. A notable design choice is the use of multiple negative responses for the same instruction rather than a single chosen/rejected pair. This matters because failures on complex instructions are often partial: a response may satisfy some constraints while missing others, or may be semantically correct but stylistically off. Multiple negatives expose the model to a richer set of near-miss errors and sharpen its sensitivity to soft-constraint violations.

Stage 2 applies reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, implemented with GRPO. The key idea is to optimize hard constraints directly using deterministic verifiers instead of relying on a learned reward model or a holistic preference objective. For complex instruction following, this is a natural fit because many important failures are discrete and checkable.

On the data side, the paper proposes an automated synthesis pipeline and builds StaInstruct. The construction is not just random constraint concatenation. It explicitly models task-constraint compatibility and filters invalid, redundant, or conflicting constraints. Each example includes a complex instruction, a high-quality positive response, multiple negative responses targeted at soft constraints, and rule-based verification code for hard constraints. This data structure aligns cleanly with the two training stages: preference learning in Stage 1 and verifier-based RL in Stage 2.

Compared with standard DPO or single-stage RL, the novelty is less about a new optimization formula and more about decomposing the training signal to match the structure of the task.
