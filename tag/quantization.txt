18:T622,The paper proceeds in three steps. First, it formulates two-sided GPTQ under a Kronecker-factored quadratic geometry. Standard GPTQ uses only the input-side covariance, corresponding to a Hessian of the form E[xx^T]⊗I. The two-sided extension allows a general A⊗B, which means quantization is guided by both input and output geometry and effectively minimizes ||(B^{1/2})^T(V-W)A^{1/2}||_F^2. Second, the authors extend the high-rate waterfilling view from prior work to this two-sided setting, yielding WaterKron. Instead of assigning uniform precision to all weights, WaterKron allocates row- and column-dependent scales and combines them with entropy coding. Under high-rate, Gaussian, and scalar-grid assumptions, the resulting allocation remains within an asymptotic gap of about 0.25 bits from the information-theoretic limit for the Kronecker distortion. Third, and most importantly, the paper analyzes WaterKron under the true full-Hessian geometry. This yields an explicit mismatch factor Φ that quantifies the multiplicative distortion penalty introduced by replacing the full Hessian with a Kronecker approximation. Choosing A and B is then cast as minimizing Φ. That optimization reduces to a Gaussian covariance-fitting problem and naturally recovers classical flip-flop updates, which motivates the proposed FlipFlop Hessian. The key distinction from prior Hessian choices in the quantization literature is that the approximation is selected to minimize distortion in the true geometry, not merely because it is easy to compute or empirically decent.19:T550,The method keeps the Kashin factorization w = u + P^T \hat{v} (with \hat{v}=Pv), aiming to bound \|u\|_\infty and \|\hat{v}\|_\infty so scalar quantization is less sensitive to outliers. The paper fixes three concrete issues observed in prior LLM applications:
1) Convergence and peak formation: instead of a matrix-level reformulation (which lost guarantees), they stay with the vector-level greedy algorithm and introduce a Greedy Algorithm with Alternating Updates. Updates of u and \hat{v} are executed in fixed blocks of four steps, designed to enforce the symmetric 4-peak distributions rather than letting the joint distribution collapse.
2) Efficient orthogonal transform: they replace a dense random orthogonal matrix with a sign-randomized DCT, reducing each application of P/P^T from O(N^2) to O(N log N) and eliminating matrix storage.
3) Faster clustering: because each greedy step changes factors by ±c_k(r_k) (a function of residual norm), after two steps the four peak locations are exactly ±c1±c2. They use these analytic centers to initialize k-means and run only a few refinement iterations, removing expensive multi-restart search.
They further compose the approach with OPTQ-style sequential error compensation and QuIP-style incoherence preprocessing (Hadamard/Kronecker), and implement the pipeline in JAX with multi-GPU pmap support.1a:T7a3,OmniKVQuant is a training-free extension of TurboQuant with two targeted modifications. TurboQuant decomposes each key/value vector into magnitude and direction, applies a randomized Hadamard rotation to the direction so coordinates look closer to a Gaussian reference, and then quantizes with a fixed codebook. This works reasonably well when one global reference distribution and one shared rotation are adequate.

The paper argues that this assumption fails in Omni-LLMs. The first fix is temporal range adaptation for keys. Even after Hadamard rotation, key coordinates still deviate substantially from the Gaussian target, and the mismatch changes over short temporal windows. Instead of using one fixed global range, OmniKVQuant performs coordinate-wise min-max scaling within a small local token group before codebook assignment. The codebook itself is not relearned; the local scaling simply adapts the same codebook to the current cache region. This is a minimal but well-motivated change that directly addresses local non-stationarity in keys.

The second fix is modality-specific value rotation. PCA visualizations show that audio, visual, and text values occupy distinct regions of representation space. A single shared rotation therefore cannot flatten all modalities equally well. OmniKVQuant calibrates separate rotations for each modality so that each value distribution is better aligned with the quantizer. The key idea is simple: modality-specific geometry persists inside a shared decoder, so the quantization stage should exploit it explicitly.

The paper also includes a fused Triton decode kernel. Instead of inverse-rotating every cached key, it rotates one query per head following a FlashAttention-style computation order. For values, inverse rotations are batched on head outputs rather than applied token by token to the cache. This reduces redundant rotation work and kernel launches without reconstructing a dense FP16 cache.1b:T5c8,The analysis experiments are important because they justify the design. On Qwen2.5-Omni-3B evaluated with WorldSense, rotated keys retain only 30.40% of the variance of the Gaussian reference, with Wasserstein-1 distance 0.778 and KS statistic 0.449. The mismatch is stronger for audio and video than for text. Values are closer to the Gaussian target overall, but their cross-modal geometry is clearly separated. This supports the paper’s split treatment: local adaptation for keys, modality-aware correction for values.

For the main results, the method is evaluated on Qwen2.5-Omni and Qwen3-Omni. On seven audio-visual benchmarks with Qwen2.5-Omni, 2-bit KV caches retain 98.1% of FP16 performance and consistently outperform TurboQuant. The abstract does not list every benchmark score, but the paper presents the gains as stable across tasks rather than concentrated on one dataset. The trend also transfers to Qwen3-Omni, which suggests the method is not tied to a single model implementation.

From an engineering perspective, two takeaways matter most. First, 2-bit KV quantization is viable for Omni-LLMs, but only if the quantizer respects multimodal cache structure. Second, a training-free method can preserve most of the original performance, which makes it attractive for deployment without model retraining. If time is limited, the problem analysis, method overview, and main results are enough for a 20-minute skim. The method works blockwise. For each microscaling block, it selects an exponent-only scale from the local maximum, maps shifted scores into a scale-normalized residual domain, and generates nonnegative E2M1 codes using a single affine index rule. Together with a block-level E8M0 scale, these codes form the final MXFP4 probability operand. The same approximation is used consistently for both numerator and denominator updates, which is important because softmax quality depends on matching those two paths. First, the authors approximate the Hessian with the empirical Fisher, using the standard assumption that a converged pretrained model is near a local optimum. They reshape the full gradient vector into a matrix G and show that the Fisher admits an exact decomposition as a sum of Kronecker products. After expressing the relevant expectation in terms of E[G⊗G], they take its SVD and recover a representation of the form J(w)=Σ_i σ_i U_i⊗V_i. The approximation comes from truncating this sum to the top-r components.

Second, they avoid explicitly constructing the enormous n^2 by m^2 matrix. Instead, they use an implicitly restarted Arnoldi solver that only needs matrix-vector products. The key identity is (G⊗G)vec(V)=vec(GVG^T), which turns the expensive operation into smaller dense multiplications on reshaped gradients. This is what makes the method scalable.

Third, they do not rely on the low-rank Kronecker term alone. They compute the Fisher diagonal exactly and combine it with the truncated decomposition. This hybrid design is sensible: the low-rank term captures correlations, while the exact diagonal preserves per-parameter scale. In practice, that should be more stable than either a pure diagonal approximation or a pure low-rank one.

Compared with K-FAC-style approximations, block-diagonal Hessians, or layerwise curvature proxies used in quantization, the distinguishing feature is the explicit attempt to preserve cross-layer interactions. On the objective side, it replaces the standard layer-local reconstruction MSE with an end-to-end-aligned surrogate called Aggregated Fisher MSE. This surrogate is derived from a second-order Taylor expansion of the global KL divergence at the current transformer block output. Unlike grouped approximations that discard cross-channel interactions, REAL-Q keeps the full aggregated Fisher matrix, so output-channel coupling is preserved. To keep the computation manageable, the expansion point is chosen so that backpropagation only needs to traverse at most two adjacent blocks rather than the full network. The method also introduces a loss sliding window to smooth transitions across neighboring blocks, reducing discontinuities in the optimization landscape during sequential block-wise quantization.

On the optimization side, the paper argues that the core limitation of the GPTQ lineage is its dependence on a closed-form column-wise solver. That requirement forces heavy Hessian compression and then freezes the approximation for the whole layer. REAL-Q instead uses Dynamic Block-wise Gradient Descent. It still follows a sequential column-wise quantization pipeline, but after each column block of 128 columns it inserts one Adam update. The gradient is recomputed online against the actual partially quantized model state, so the method can correct accumulated upstream errors and adapt to the shifted loss landscape as quantization progresses. The authors describe this as increasing the correction granularity from per-layer to per-column-block, roughly two orders of magnitude finer. Adam’s diagonal preconditioning provides an implicit second-order effect without explicit Hessian inversion.
