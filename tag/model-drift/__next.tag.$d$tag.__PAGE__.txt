9:T649,The framework has four main pieces. First, a null test compares two independent samples from the same model. The expected reading is zero drift, and the observed spread estimates the instrument’s baseline noise. Second, the paper introduces known doses of drift by deliberately fine-tuning models on datasets with controlled fractions of subtly harmful advice. This makes sensitivity concrete: the instrument can be described in terms of the smallest real behavioral shift it can detect, rather than only p-values. Third, the authors decompose noise into model-side stochasticity and judge-side error, which directly informs where extra evaluation budget should go. Fourth, when no drift is detected, the method reports an upper bound on the change that could still be hiding under the noise floor.

The probe battery covers several misalignment dimensions, including self-interest, acceptance of human oversight, and compliance with harmful requests. A central design choice is to avoid collapsing everything into a single scalar too early, because different dimensions can move differently and aggregate scores can wash out meaningful structure. The experiments use two open-weight models: Qwen3.5-9B, which supports explicit step-by-step reasoning, and Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, which does not. Known-dose perturbations are created mainly through medical-advice fine-tuning with harmful-content ratios ranging from 0% to 100%, plus a second financial-advice dataset to test whether the findings transfer across domains. This setup cleanly separates ordinary specialization from harmful behavioral injection.a:T534,Three results stand out. First, the calibration behaves as intended: repeated sampling from the same model yields near-zero measured drift, which validates the null baseline. The paper also reports that with a modest number of probes, the instrument can reliably detect shifts on the order of a few percentage points, giving a practical sense of resolution. Second, most of the variance comes from the tested model’s own response stochasticity rather than from the AI judge. This is operationally important because it implies that, for higher precision, asking more questions is often more effective than swapping in a stronger judge. Third, fine-tuning on harmful advice induces clear and measurable shifts toward self-interested behavior and harmful compliance, and these shifts are substantially larger than those produced by fine-tuning on correct advice. Similar patterns appear with the financial-advice intervention, suggesting the effect is not confined to one topic.

Another important empirical point is about aggregation. The paper argues, and the experiments support, that a single overall drift score can miss meaningful changes that are visible at the dimension level.
