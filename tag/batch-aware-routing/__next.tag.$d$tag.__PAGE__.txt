9:T6ee,The method, BASE, stands for Batch-Aware Selection of Experts using Predicted Removal Error. For token t, the MoE output is y_t = sum_{u in R_t} w_{t,u} E_u(z_t). If some experts are not loaded at the batch level, the layer output changes. The paper writes the resulting squared error as a sum of individual removal terms plus cross terms between experts omitted together. The key claim is that selection is robust to dropping the cross terms, which makes it possible to score experts independently and keep the highest-scoring ones under a fixed budget.

The score is a predicted removal error. For a token-expert pair, the removal cost is tied to the squared router weight and the expert output energy ||E_u(z_t)||^2. The difficulty is that the true expert output is unavailable before the expert is fetched and executed, while selection must happen beforehand. BASE addresses this by training a lightweight linear predictor for each expert during calibration. At inference time, it predicts the expert’s output energy from the current token representation, combines that with router weights, aggregates the resulting costs across the batch, and selects the experts with the largest predicted batch-level impact.

The paper also handles the practical case where a token routes to an expert that was not loaded. It uses a backfill mechanism, replacing the missing expert with an available routed alternative. Compared with methods such as SERE and OEA, which build the batch active set more directly from token routing decisions, BASE asks a stronger question: how much does this expert matter to the current batch output? Compared with ExFold, which relies on calibrated expert norms, BASE uses token-conditioned predictions rather than static expert strength estimates.a:T44f,The evaluation spans three MoE architectures and eight benchmarks, with comparisons under different batch budgets. The headline results reported in the paper are strong in the tight-budget regime: BASE improves average accuracy by 3.0 to 29.5 points over prior batch-aware decoding methods at comparable speed. Under more relaxed budgets, it stays within 0.4 points of dense or full-MoE quality while delivering 22% to 60% higher throughput.

The most important experimental checks are likely the ablations behind the method assumptions. One is the effect of dropping cross terms in the removal-error objective; the paper argues this has little impact on the selected expert set. Another is the quality of the output-energy predictor. If a simple linear model is sufficient, that suggests expert output energy is predictable enough from token representations to make the online procedure practical.
