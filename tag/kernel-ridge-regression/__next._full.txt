A cache item is no longer just a discrete query-response pair; it effectively covers a semantic neighborhood defined by a similarity rule or distance threshold. The objective is to minimize long-run serving cost under cache-capacity constraints, where total cost depends on query arrivals, cache hits, and the LLM execution cost on misses.

The first challenge is optimization over a continuous domain. The paper addresses this by discretizing or partitioning the continuous space into finitely many regions, then estimating arrival rates and serving costs at the region level. The key point is not merely clustering, but controlling the trade-off between approximation error and learnability: coarse partitions hurt semantic fidelity, while fine partitions increase variance and exploration burden.

The second challenge is reverse feedback. If a request is served from cache, the system does not observe the true cost of running the LLM for that request. The paper models this as a combinatorial bandit problem with partial and action-dependent feedback. To cope with this, the algorithm must include explicit exploration, such as occasionally bypassing the cache or probing selected regions to recover cost estimates.

From the available text, the method appears to have three layers:
1. Partition the continuous semantic space into finite cacheable units.
2. Learn each unit’s arrival probability and serving cost online, likely under smoothness assumptions that allow generalization across nearby queries.
3. Solve a capacity-constrained combinatorial optimization problem to choose which units to cache while balancing exploration and exploitation.

The theoretical contribution seems to include both approximation guarantees relative to the continuous optimum and regret guarantees under unknown arrivals and unknown costs.
