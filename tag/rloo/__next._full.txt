18:T60b,The paper is a controlled reward-design study rather than a new RL method. It compares several reward choices under two critic-free policy-gradient algorithms, GRPO and RLOO, across multiple pretrained code-capable base models. The reward variants include standard binary reward, direct pass-rate reward, reweighted pass-rate variants, and two-stage schedules that combine dense and sparse rewards.

The analysis has two layers. First, the authors verify that pass-rate rewards are indeed denser: many rollouts receive intermediate scores instead of collapsing to all-zero feedback. So the failure mode is not the absence of signal.

Second, they study whether those intermediate rewards induce useful gradient directions. Their central claim is that pass rate is miscalibrated with respect to full correctness. A program can achieve a high pass rate through brittle heuristics, memorized patterns, or overfitting to the provided tests while still being far from correct. Conversely, a nearly correct solution with a small bug may receive only a modest pass rate. Under group-relative updates, this causes ranking errors inside the sampled batch. When no rollout fully solves the problem, different partial solutions push the policy in different directions, and the resulting update tends to be weak or cancel out.

This is the key distinction from naive reward shaping: the paper argues that numerical granularity is not enough. What matters is whether the surrogate reward preserves a meaningful optimization geometry toward the target behavior.19:T445,From the provided full-text content, the experimental setup spans multiple base models, two critic-free RL algorithms, and several reward designs. The headline result is consistent across these settings: pass-rate rewards do not reliably outperform binary rewards in final code-generation performance.

The most important empirical observations are about training dynamics rather than a single benchmark number:
- pass-rate rewards increase the fraction of non-zero-reward rollouts;
- this extra density does not translate into better pass-all-tests outcomes;
- on hard problems where no rollout is fully correct, partial-pass samples produce noisy and weak policy updates;
- some high-pass-rate programs are shortcut solutions rather than genuinely correct ones;
- reweighted or two-stage variants may help optimization in limited regimes, but they do not overturn the main conclusion.
