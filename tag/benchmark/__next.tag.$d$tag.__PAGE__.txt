a:T818,MGAL is built from long-form reports in the UN Digital Library, using position-aligned multilingual documents so that cross-lingual comparisons are not merely task-level but also semantically and structurally aligned. It covers Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish, with context lengths from 8K to 128K tokens. The benchmark spans four linguistic granularities—word, sentence, paragraph, and document—and includes both closed-ended and open-ended tasks. The paper mentions seven tasks in total, including word-level QA, sentence-level cloze, paragraph filling, document summarization, and translation, with 420 query-response pairs per task per language.

The key design choice is controlled stratification by evidence position. Examples are labeled by whether the relevant evidence appears at the beginning, middle, or end, indexed at both document and paragraph levels. This turns the benchmark into a diagnostic instrument rather than a generic task suite. The choice of UN reports is practical: they provide multilingual parallelism, topic consistency, and relatively regular discourse structure, which makes coherent unit definitions more reliable.

Quality control is manual and strict. The authors state that every query-response pair was audited and flawed samples were removed. This is a smaller-but-cleaner benchmark philosophy, which is appropriate for diagnostic evaluation.

Scoring is hybrid. Closed-ended tasks use precision-oriented reference metrics such as Accuracy, while open-ended tasks use ROUGE-L and, for tasks like paragraph filling where lexical overlap is insufficient, an LLM-as-a-judge setup. That trade-off is sensible: pure reference metrics under-credit semantically correct paraphrases, while pure judge-based evaluation reduces reproducibility. MGAL uses both.

Relative to LongBench, LV-Eval, M4LE, and ONERULER, the novelty is not simply broader coverage. The main difference is tighter control over variables that matter for error attribution: multilingual alignment, discourse granularity, and evidence position.b:T8dd,The experimental section is useful mainly for the error patterns it exposes. The benchmark is evaluated on 12 long-context LLMs. The main findings reported in the paper fall into four groups.

First, performance degrades as granularity becomes coarser. Models do well on word-level tasks, but performance drops at sentence, paragraph, and document levels. This suggests that many current long-context models are stronger at local matching or retrieval than at stable discourse-level integration.

Second, multilingual gaps widen in lower-resource languages. Large open-source and closed-source models are relatively comparable on higher-resource languages, but closed-source systems maintain a clearer lead on lower-resource ones, and smaller open models degrade more sharply. For pretraining, this points to data coverage and multilingual representation quality as major bottlenecks, not just architecture.

Third, position sensitivity remains strong. In sentence cloze, blanks appearing earlier in the text produce the highest error rates, with performance improving toward later positions, consistent with recency-biased attention. The authors also observe an early-option bias: models disproportionately choose earlier answer options such as A or B, indicating reliance on answer-order heuristics rather than evidence verification.

Fourth, the paper identifies two more specific failure modes. One is local semantic crowding: when neighboring sentences share entities, years, or discourse markers, models over-rely on surface overlap and pick the wrong sentence, failing to distinguish functional roles such as background, explanation, or outcome. The other is a fluency-consistency gap: generated outputs are often fluent and stylistically plausible but weakly anchored to the source, leading to factual drift and unsupported claims.

In terms of benchmark scale, MGAL covers 6 languages, 7 tasks, 4 granularities, and contexts from 8K to 128K, with 420 examples per task per language. Even without chasing a single aggregate score, this is enough to support meaningful slicing by granularity and position. The benchmark evaluates four upgrade strategies: freeze, port, refresh, and retrain. Freeze keeps serving the old specialist. Port copies the old adapter or task vector onto the new base. Retention is especially important: it measures how much of the attainable gain on the target hop is preserved by a cheaper strategy relative to the best specialist achievable there. This makes comparisons across hops, tasks, and model scales much more meaningful.

The benchmark covers six application tasks from three classes, at two model scales, and treats training-side cost as a first-class output, including GPU-minutes and energy. That is a strong design choice because deployment decisions depend on cost-to-recover, not just final accuracy.

The paper does not introduce a new transfer algorithm. Its contribution is experimental structure and variable control. The most important analysis concerns porting. A common practitioner assumption is that if two checkpoints are shape-compatible, direct adapter copying is a strong baseline. The paper shows that this assumption is false. What licenses copying is not architectural shape but training continuity: whether the target checkpoint lies on the same weight trajectory, and how far away it is.

The OLMo experiments sharpen this into something close to a mechanistic statement. Along the same documented trajectory, adapter retention decays as continuation distance grows.
