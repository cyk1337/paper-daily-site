9:T529,TwinICL is built around paired counterfactuals. The authors first sample an ICL episode in a modality-agnostic canonical form (hidden rule + demonstrations + query), then render it into two twins: a text twin (shape names as tokens) and an image twin (the same shapes rendered visually). Crucially, answers and targets remain textual in both conditions, so the comparison isolates the effect of presenting evidence visually.

The benchmark uses a shared vocabulary of 12 named shapes and defines 38 procedurally generated tasks grouped into four families: Selection, Relation, Aggregation, and Transformation. A subset is organized into position-controlled groups to probe position/window effects while holding other factors fixed.

Beyond measuring the modality gap across models and shot counts, the paper runs (i) recovery interventions targeting visual access, task framing, and reasoning support, and (ii) decomposition variants that separate induction (inferring the rule from demonstrations) from execution (carrying out a stated rule with multimodal inputs). The key methodological point is that the gap persists even when the task is stated directly, implying a more basic cross-modal execution difficulty; demonstrations then act as both evidence and additional context load, especially in multi-image settings.a:T410,Evaluations span 6 open-weight models, 38 tasks, and multiple shot settings. The main empirical finding is a consistent drop when the same ICL evidence is presented as images rather than text, across all tested model–shot combinations. The gap magnitude varies by task family, with larger gaps reported for Selection and Transformation. Another notable result is that strong text-only ICL does not reliably transfer to multimodal ICL.

On a diagnostic subset of high-gap tasks, individual interventions (visual access / framing / reasoning) show limited or inconsistent gains, while their combination can recover strong multimodal ICL performance (illustrated in the paper’s main figure). Decomposition experiments show the gap remains even without induction (rule given explicitly), supporting the interpretation that execution with visual evidence is a core bottleneck. Each property is evaluated at two cognitive levels. Reasoning tasks present one or more rendered scenes and ask the model to identify a topological relation or predict how a change would affect it. Planning tasks place the model in an interactive environment where it receives rendered observations and must choose actions that build, preserve, or alter the relevant topological structure.

Three design choices matter. First, tasks are procedurally generated from parametric scene states, so difficulty is controllable and labels come from the underlying state rather than manual annotation. Second, the benchmark separates the cognitive taxonomy from the formal target definition: the former determines what capability is being tested, while the latter determines how instances are generated and scored. Third, the paper goes beyond static QA by evaluating closed-loop agents and by studying whether supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and image/video generative augmentation can improve performance. This makes MindTopo more than a question set; it becomes a framework for exposing where action fails even when recognition partly works.

The benchmark contains 11,030 instances across 13 task types: 8,030 reasoning questions and 3,000 planning episodes. The authors evaluate 14 MLLMs, then run SFT and RL experiments on Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct. They also test agent configurations augmented with image and video generation, including three video generative models, to see whether predictive visual rollouts help preserve topology during planning.c:T68f,The main empirical message is clear: current MLLMs are weak at topological reasoning, and they are substantially weaker at topological planning. The most important findings are three stable patterns. First, even the best model remains well below observed human performance, so the benchmark is not saturated. Second, every evaluated MLLM performs better on reasoning than on planning, showing that recognizing a topological relation does not automatically translate into an executable policy. Third, on Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct, SFT followed by RL gives the strongest average performance, but most of the gain appears on reasoning tasks, while planning improves much less. RL also yields uneven transfer to held-out tasks, suggesting limited generalization.

The generative-model-assisted planning experiments are also informative. The paper evaluates agents augmented with image and video generation in the hope that imagined futures or predictive visual rollouts might help action selection. The result is negative in an interesting way: generated observations may preserve local cues and may even look plausible at the endpoint, but audited rollouts do not reliably follow environment dynamics or maintain topological validity across transitions. In other words, visual plausibility is not enough for topology-consistent planning.

The benchmark also appears to support controlled difficulty and property-specific error analysis. While the abstract does not list every score, the full paper should be read for the breakdown by property, cognitive level, and training intervention. The key takeaway is the reasoning–planning gap and the uneven held-out-task gains after training.d:T416,For a quick but useful read, start with the benchmark definition section to understand the five topological properties and the distinction between reasoning and planning. The dataset is built from two expert-grounded sources.

The first source is ICLR 2023–2026 OpenReview forums. The authors run a two-stage LLM pipeline to extract novelty-overlap claims from official reviews and author rebuttals, then have three PhD-level annotators verify prior-work identity, overlap dimensions, and reviewer evidence. The released high-confidence subset contains 236 pairs. This source is anchored in real review disputes and tends to emphasize method and problem overlap.

The second source is survey co-citation grouping. Papers grouped together by survey authors as similar are used to construct positives after annotation, while negatives are created by cross-group sampling from distinct similarity groups mentioned within the same sentence. This is a better negative construction than random pairing because it keeps topical proximity while preserving dimension-specific non-overlap. It also contributes more task-level similarity cases.

Each instance is labeled independently along three dimensions: Task, Problem, and Method. These correspond to application goal, core technical challenge, and solution approach. A key design choice is that these are fixed ground-truth labels rather than post hoc interpretations of free-form model outputs. That makes error localization explicit.

The evaluation protocol is stricter than standard benchmark scoring. Models must not only predict overlap per dimension, but also provide verbatim evidence spans. Outputs then go through a three-stage cascade: per-dimension correctness, evidence hallucination check, and reasoning mismatch check. Only predictions that pass all three stages count toward Verified F1. In other words, the benchmark measures “correct answer with faithful support,” not just label coincidence. The benchmark also includes 50 multi-paper grouping sets to test whether models can organize related work more structurally than pairwise comparison alone.f:T486,The benchmark is evaluated on 18 LLMs. The main empirical message is not that one model reaches a particularly high score, but that failure modes are systematic: even when the final overlap judgment is correct, models often support it with fabricated evidence or with evidence that is only weakly connected to the stated conclusion. These failures vary substantially across Task, Problem, and Method dimensions. The paper also notes that the two data sources induce different distributions—ICLR-derived pairs skew toward method/problem overlap, while survey-derived pairs skew toward task similarity—which helps expose dimension-specific weaknesses rather than hiding them under a single distribution.

In terms of scale, the benchmark contains 619 paper pairs and 50 multi-paper sets. The ICLR source starts from 42,682 OpenReview forums, indicating substantial upstream mining before strong human filtering at release time. For readers, the most important quantitative pattern is the gap between ordinary correctness metrics and Verified F1. That gap shows that getting the label right and grounding it faithfully are materially different capabilities.10:T74a,The core contribution is a benchmark and a probing protocol, not a new model. MotionBlind uses a contrastive minimal-pair design. Each instance contains two near-identical clips recorded in the same scene with the same objects and background, differing only in one motion property such as left vs right movement, faster vs slower motion, or larger vs smaller displacement. Each clip is paired with two complementary yes/no questions, yielding four binary decisions per instance. An instance counts as correct only if all four are correct. This strict instance-level metric is intended to suppress inflated scores from partial guessing.

The dataset is self-recorded rather than scraped from the internet. That choice matters because internet videos are hard to control for static confounds and hard to label precisely for physical motion variables. The paper also prefers relative-motion questions over absolute notions like “fast” or “slow,” since absolute labels depend heavily on context. Comparing two moving references within the same scene reduces semantic ambiguity and makes the benchmark a cleaner test of motion perception.

The probing setup is well designed. First, a no-video text-only probe tests whether questions can be answered from language priors alone. Second, shuffled-frame and reversed-frame probes test temporal-order sensitivity: if performance barely changes when order is destroyed, the model is likely not using motion information. Third, the paper studies frame selection under budgets from 1 to 24 frames, comparing uniform sampling, random sampling, a GRPO-trained learned selector (HORNet), and a training-free key-clip selector (Frame2Clip). This helps separate several possible failure modes: whether one frame is already enough, whether more frames help, and whether better frame selection can recover motion understanding.11:T615,The empirical conclusion is straightforward: current Video-LLMs perform poorly on physically grounded motion understanding, and standard video-QA scores do not reveal this weakness. The paper evaluates models on MotionBlind together with TimeBlind and Video-MME. Non-contrastive benchmarks such as Video-MME can make models look broadly capable, but performance drops sharply when static content is controlled and only motion differs. The introduction cites TimeBlind as a reference point, where even strong frontier models reach only 48.2% instance accuracy while humans achieve 98.2%. MotionBlind narrows the focus further to speed, direction, and magnitude.

The diagnostic results are the most useful part. The text-only probe checks whether benchmark leakage through language priors is possible. The shuffled/reversed-frame probes test whether models are actually sensitive to temporal order. If order corruption causes only limited degradation, that is strong evidence that the model is reading static frames rather than motion. The frame-budget and frame-selection experiments then ask whether the issue is simply missing key frames. The paper’s answer is largely no: increasing frames or using more sophisticated selectors does not fully recover the missing capability.
