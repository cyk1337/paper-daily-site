18:T51d,ArchitectureIQ is built around controllability and executable verification. Each question starts from a sampled synthetic data specification, which is rendered into code and executed to produce fixed train and test sets. Candidate training recipes are then sampled from controlled pools covering model architecture, optimizer and hyperparameters, loss function, batch size, and training duration. The ground truth is not annotated by humans; it is obtained by running the exact generated code. To ensure a clean multiple-choice setup, the benchmark only keeps questions with a clear winner across 10 random seeds. The released static benchmark contains 500 three-way questions.

The benchmark is evenly split into optimizer-only, architecture-only, and mixed questions. That taxonomy is important because it separates different forms of intuition: sensitivity to optimization dynamics versus understanding of architecture-data fit. The paper also includes an auxiliary analysis where model-generated reasoning traces are compressed into a compact knowledge base and then fed back to weaker models. This is not essential to the benchmark itself, but it supports the authors’ broader claim that current model intuition looks more like a collection of empirical heuristics than a structured theory of training.19:T72c,On the 500-question static benchmark, frontier models reach about 76.0% accuracy, well above the 33.3% random baseline. In a 50-question human subset answered by 10 ML researchers, the best human reaches 66.0% and the mean is 51.0%. So the benchmark reveals a real predictive capability, but also a substantial gap to reliable outcome prediction.

The most informative result is the breakdown by question type. Leading models achieve around 85–87% on optimizer-only questions, but only 58–61% on architecture-only questions; GPT-6 Astra reportedly drops to 38% on architecture-only. Mixed questions are usually closer to optimizer-only performance, suggesting that when optimizer signals are available, models lean on them because they are easier and more stable to judge. The implied conclusion is that LLMs are much better at recognizing short-horizon optimization patterns than at reasoning about architecture suitability relative to data properties.

Two additional findings matter. First, in-context learning and test-time scaling do not help much, which the authors interpret as evidence that the intuition is empirical rather than deeply structured. Second, a compact knowledge base distilled from reasoning traces can substantially boost weaker models: GPT-4o with a 20-item KB reportedly matches the raw performance of Claude Opus 5. That suggests a nontrivial fraction of this capability can be compressed into concise, human-readable rules rather than requiring long chain-of-thought.

The provided excerpt does not include the full appendix, so I cannot safely restate the complete leaderboard, the exact candidate pools, or all statistical details. But the main pattern is clear: models are strong on optimizer comparisons, weak on architecture-data coupling, and not greatly improved by extra inference-time reasoning.1a:T54e,This is a skim paper. Start with the introduction, where the target capability is defined carefully: predicting training outcomes, not designing experiments. Then look at the benchmark generation pipeline and the taxonomy of optimizer-only versus architecture-only questions. Those design choices explain why the benchmark is more informative than a generic ML multiple-choice test.

In the results, prioritize the overall leaderboard only briefly, then focus on the per-category breakdown. The optimizer-versus-architecture gap is the real story. After that, read the failure-mode discussion: empirical but not structured, not maximally condensed, and insensitive to data. Even if one does not fully accept every interpretation, these are useful hypotheses to test against future research-agent systems.

If you only need the main idea, about 20 minutes is enough. If you are considering using the benchmark, spend more time in the appendix on the generator details: what architectures and optimizers are included, how synthetic datasets are parameterized, how “clear winner” is defined, and how the human study was sampled. One caution is important: this benchmark measures intuition on small synthetic tasks, so it should not be over-interpreted as direct evidence about large-scale LLM pretraining recipe design. It is better viewed as a clean proxy.1b:T541,The benchmark is built around three design choices. First, the authors structure latent mental variables with a BDI-P taxonomy: Perception, Belief, and Desire, expanded into 12 secondary aspects and 34 fine-grained subdimensions. This turns a vague notion of Theory of Mind into a controlled evaluation space. Second, they introduce Zero-Action Prompting. Prompts describe only internal state and never specify the action, so the model cannot rely on lexical action cues and must infer intention before behavior. Third, they use counterfactual prompt pairs under a fixed visual world state. Only one mental variable is changed, such as true belief versus false belief, or seeing versus not seeing an object. Success on both paired prompts is treated as stronger evidence that the model is tracking latent state rather than exploiting shortcuts.

The evaluation pipeline is also more careful than a standard video benchmark. Instead of only scoring visual quality, they use an automated large-vision-model judge to assess visual quality, commonsense plausibility, intention accuracy, and world-state maintenance. The judge is prompted with structured reasoning steps rather than a single scalar query. They also compare the automated pipeline against judgments aggregated from 10 human experts on 100 generated videos, reporting strong agreement.1c:T49b,MindWorldBench contains 372 expert-designed images and 744 prompts arranged as counterfactual pairs. The paper evaluates 11 image-to-video models, including Veo 3.1, Kling V3, Wan 2.6, and CogVideoX. The main empirical pattern is consistent across models: visual realism and physical commonsense are noticeably stronger than mental-state consistency. In other words, models often produce plausible actions, but not the action that follows from the character’s subjective state.

The most informative experimental finding is the Omniscient Bias. In false-belief scenarios, a character should act on what they believe, even when that belief is wrong. Models frequently ignore that distinction and generate behavior aligned with objective truth. The paired counterfactual setup is important here because single-prompt success can be accidental; paired success under minimal mental-state changes is a stricter test. The abstract does not expose the full numeric table, but the full text makes the ranking less important than the shared failure mode: even strong proprietary and open models remain much better at physical continuation than at belief-conditioned behavior generation.1d:T61c,The framework decomposes each composite multimodal task into unit tasks and represents their dependencies with a structural causal model. Each unit task targets a relatively isolated capability, and directed edges encode prerequisite relations. During evaluation, each prerequisite can be left unassisted, supplied with a verified correct answer, or supplied with a plausible incorrect answer. This yields three capability metrics. NC measures natural performance without assistance. IC measures intrinsic capability when prerequisites are corrected, reducing contamination from upstream cascading errors. RC measures resilience when prerequisites are wrong, showing whether the model follows or resists misleading intermediate information. The paper also defines N-Score and S-Score for causal contribution. N-Score measures how much success is lost when one prerequisite is degraded, corresponding to necessity. S-Score measures how much failure is recovered when one prerequisite is supplied, corresponding to sufficiency. These metrics adapt probabilities of causation to MLLM evaluation without assuming monotonicity, which matters because wrong premises can sometimes improve answers through prompt effects or spurious correlations. The benchmark, CADET, contains 10 composite tasks decomposed into 46 unit tasks and more than 33,000 unit-task questions. It covers perception, spatial, temporal, and cognitive capabilities. Each question has human-created and verified correct answers plus plausible incorrect answers, enabling the three-state interventions.1e:T4e6,The paper evaluates 12 frontier MLLMs. The main result is that end-to-end accuracy hides very different failure structures. At the capability level, cognitive tasks have the lowest natural score, NC=0.41, but rise to IC=0.72 when correct prerequisites are supplied, becoming the second strongest category under intervention. This removes 54% of natural cognitive errors, suggesting many apparent reasoning failures originate upstream.
