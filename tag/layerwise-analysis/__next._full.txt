18:T69f,The method is straightforward. The authors apply Logit Lens to track the layer-by-layer evolution of answer-option logits in MCQA prompts. For each sample, they cache hidden states from every transformer block in a single forward pass. At each layer, they take the hidden state at the answer position, apply the model’s final layer norm, project it through the unembedding matrix, and extract only the logits for the option-label tokens such as A/B/C/D. This yields a ranking trajectory across layers. HDL is then defined as the point beyond which the relative ranking of options remains stable.

That definition is stronger than simply checking whether top-1 stops changing. It captures whether the model’s internal preference structure has settled. The paper then tests whether this phenomenon is architecture-level rather than an artifact. It does so through cross-model and cross-dataset evaluation, LoRA before/after comparisons, varying the number of answer options, and changing label formats. The label-format result is important: alphabetic labels and Arabic numerals produce sharper HDLs than Roman numerals, which hints that tokenizer statistics and token geometry affect how clearly commitment can be read out.

Relative to prior work, the contribution is diagnostic rather than algorithmic. No learned exit policy is trained, and no layers are removed. The paper tries to measure committed inference as a naturally emerging property of standard transformers. The main caveat is also clear: the analysis depends on Logit Lens, whose faithfulness on intermediate layers is still debated, and the setup is restricted to single-token answer labels rather than open-ended generation.19:T5a4,The study covers four model families—Qwen, Llama, Granite, and Mistral—on four MCQA benchmarks. The main empirical pattern is that a distinct HDL appears across models, and layerwise accuracy often jumps sharply around that layer before flattening. The headline number in the abstract is a gain of up to +0.61 accuracy for Qwen on CommonsenseQA at the HDL relative to earlier layers, after which performance remains largely stable. That supports the interpretation that later layers mostly reinforce rather than revise the decision.

The most informative ablations are threefold. First, label-format ablations show that A/B/C/D and 1/2/3/4 yield clearer decision boundaries than Roman numerals. This suggests that token identity, pretraining frequency, and tokenizer behavior matter for observability. Second, varying the number of options does not remove the HDL, which argues against the phenomenon being a quirk of four-way prompting. Third, LoRA fine-tuning leaves the HDL largely unchanged, supporting the claim that it is tied to model architecture more than to downstream adaptation.

For a pretraining-oriented reader, the most interesting patterns are not the exact benchmark numbers but two structural observations: ranking stabilization looks abrupt rather than gradual, and ranking freeze is not the same as confidence formation. Later layers may continue widening the logit gap even after the option order has stopped changing.1a:T435,This is a skim paper. Start with the introduction to see the distinction between natural commitment and learned early-exit policies. Then read the HDL definition carefully in the methodology: whether stability means top-1 invariance or full ranking invariance, and how it is aggregated across samples. After that, inspect the main layerwise plots. The key question is whether the transition is genuinely sharp or just a broad plateau.

The two sections worth extra attention are the label-format ablation and the LoRA invariance result. Those determine whether HDL should be treated as a prompt artifact, a tokenizer artifact, or a more structural property. If you only spend 20–30 minutes, read the introduction, HDL definition, main figures, and those two ablations.
