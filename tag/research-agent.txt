18:T490,Deep Research Pretraining (DRP) is an offline reverse-synthesis pipeline.
- Inputs: a document corpus plus an evidence graph (citation graph or Wikipedia hyperlink graph).
- Construction: pick a citation-bearing / link-bearing passage as the target report r; recover its evidence set Er (linked/cited documents that substantively support the passage); sample graph-neighborhood alternatives that are related but not selected.
- Proxy objective: infer a research objective u whose answer is expressed by r.
- Rendering: convert (u, evidence, alternatives) into standardized search–open–write trajectories.
- Training objective (predictive navigation): conditioned on the objective and the evidence observed so far, predict what to search, which documents to open, and how to synthesize/write grounded text.
- Key trade-off: no live retrieval environment and no executed policy rollouts; candidate sets and document contents are assembled offline, prioritizing scale and controllability over perfect interaction realism.
- Instantiations: DRP-Paper (citation graphs, Related Work passages) and DRP-Web (Wikipedia hyperlinks with filtering to keep substantive links).19:T446,Setup and reported findings from the provided excerpt:
- Starting point: Qwen3-14B-Base.
- Continual pretraining: 1B tokens on DRP-Paper or DRP-Web.
- Downstream: agent SFT on controlled fractions of 13K GPT-5-generated deep-research trajectories (DR Tulu), measuring performance vs number of expert trajectories.
- Results: consistent gains over no-DRP SFT at low-data budgets on DeepResearch Bench across multiple independently sampled subsets; with 1/4 trajectories, DRP-Web exceeds a fixed full-data Base checkpoint.
- Transfer: improvements carry to ResearchQA, WebWalkerQA, SimpleQA; the 1/16-SFT gain persists on Qwen3-30B-A3B.
- RL continuation: from matched 1/16-SFT checkpoints, DRP-Web stays ahead of Base through 80 steps of ARPO-style agentic RL.
- Controls: source-matched raw-document CPT and evidence-mismatch controls suggest the benefit comes from evidence-conditioned navigation rather than domain exposure or merely imitating agent formatting.
(The excerpt does not include exact score deltas or detailed data composition; those would come from the results tables/appendix.)1a:T6bf,The method has three main components. The first is synthetic data construction. Quest-8K is built as more than query-answer pairs: each instance couples a complex research query with a task-specific rubric tree. The rubric encodes subgoals, evidence requirements, and output structure, so it serves both as supervision and as the basis for downstream reward design. Compared with synthesizing only final answers, this is better aligned with long-horizon tasks because both intermediate behavior and final reports can be assessed in a structured way.

The second component is context management. Long web-research trajectories quickly run into context bloat: search results, snippets, discovered facts, unresolved subproblems, and partial drafts all accumulate. Quest maintains compact summaries of the evolving information state instead of naively replaying the full history into the context window. This is not only a token-saving trick. It pushes the model toward abstraction and state tracking, which is closer to how a practical research agent should operate than simple retrieval concatenation.

The third component is the training pipeline itself. The paper emphasizes a full MT → SFT → RL recipe. Mid-training adapts the base model to the distribution and behavior patterns of deep-research tasks. SFT aligns the model to the target interfaces for tool use, task formatting, and report generation. RL then uses rubric-based signals to improve the harder objectives, especially report synthesis and citation grounding, where exact-match supervision is weak or unavailable. The novelty is less about a single module and more about unifying objective and open-ended tasks under one rubric-driven optimization framework.1b:T626,The reported results span model sizes from 2B to 35B and evaluate across eight benchmarks covering the three capability groups. The headline numbers are Quest-35B reaching 64.6% on BrowseComp, 30.7% on Mind2Web 2, and 48.2% on DeepResearch Bench, with the paper claiming it surpasses strong proprietary systems including OpenAI DeepResearch. If these results hold under the paper’s setup, the important point is that the unified recipe does not appear to trade away one capability class to gain another.

The more informative part is the stage-wise ablation story. Figure 2 and the surrounding discussion make a clear argument: prior open systems often support only fact seeking or only report synthesis, and many omit either mid-training or rubric-based RL. Quest is designed to show that SFT alone gives incomplete coverage; adding mid-training improves adaptation to search-style tasks and long-horizon state handling; adding RL further improves open-ended report quality and citation-constrained behavior. The excerpt provided here does not include the full ablation tables, but the paper explicitly positions controlled studies as a core contribution.

Another notable result is scaling behavior. The study covers 2B, 4B, 9B, and 35B models, aiming to show that deep-research-agent capability scales with model size but is not exclusive to large models. The authors also highlight strong small-model results, including Quest-2B-SFT and Quest-4B-SFT, suggesting that the gains come not only from model capacity but also from better training signals and task structure.1c:T443,This is a skim paper, so read for recipe design rather than implementation minutiae. That is the most transferable idea in the paper.1d:T41e,Its main contribution is not a new model architecture but a stronger training signal for agent post-training. The paper shows that when the target capability is long-horizon, tool-using, evidence-sparse scientific reasoning, data construction becomes the central bottleneck. There are three concrete contributions. First, it proposes an automated task-construction framework tailored to frontier science rather than reusing general-domain information-seeking recipes. Second, it explicitly includes computational scientific reasoning, not just conceptual QA, forcing the agent to chain retrieval, model identification, parameter extraction, and solving. Third, it closes the loop from data construction to SFT to RL to trajectory analysis, showing how post-training changes not only benchmark accuracy but also search depth, tool-use frequency, and adaptive budget allocation. For researchers interested in the pretrain/post-train interface, this is a useful case study in how the structure of generated supervision shapes the resulting reasoning policy.1e:T73c,The method has two main parts: automated data construction and agent post-training. On the data side, the authors start from a curated pool of frontier scientific entities. They then build two pipelines. The first is a conceptual pipeline: a web agent browses academic sources starting from seed entities, gathers evidence, and generates grounded questions. Complexity is then increased through iterative anchor-based task augmentation, where an anchor is the decisive scientific entity that serves as the central handle for the question. The second is a computational pipeline: the system performs a three-level evidence selection process to identify scientific models associated with seed entities, including governing equations, mechanistic understanding, and required input parameters. It then generates computational questions and obtains reference answers through voting-based solver verification. The key idea is not merely to add arithmetic, but to supervise the full chain of finding the right model, extracting the right quantities, and executing the computation. These pipelines produce SciResearcherQA. On the training side, the authors follow a fairly standard but carefully executed deep-research post-training recipe on top of Qwen3-8B: supervised fine-tuning with rejection sampling, followed by reinforcement learning with GRPO, yielding SciResearcher-8B. A notable point is that RL is used not only to improve final accuracy but also to shape longer, more tool-intensive, and difficulty-adaptive trajectories. Compared with prior work, the main differences are the shift from entity-centric web knowledge to heterogeneous academic evidence, the inclusion of computational reasoning as a first-class target, and a more stable automatic loop built from anchor augmentation and solver verification rather than shallow template expansion.1f:T512,Several numbers stand out. The behavioral analysis is equally important: compared with the baseline, post-training produces trajectories that are 0.3x to 2.7x longer and uses tools more frequently. More interestingly, RL induces adaptive behavior relative to the SFT checkpoint.
