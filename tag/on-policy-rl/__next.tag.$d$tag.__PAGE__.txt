5:T4ef,GROW replaces trajectory-level policy-gradient RL with advantage-weighted flow-matching regression. For each prompt, the current policy samples a group of utterances. Each utterance is scored by two rewards: intelligibility and speaker similarity. Each reward is standardized within the group, then combined into a signed group-relative advantage. This advantage directly reweights the standard flow-matching loss, so samples above the group mean are reinforced and those below it are downweighted. Two design choices matter. First, the group-mean reward baseline turns reward weighting into advantage weighting, which removes prompt-level scale variation and amplifies subtle but meaningful differences among samples from the same prompt. Second, a Wasserstein-2 velocity penalty anchors the updated velocity field to a frozen pretrained reference, limiting drift and helping preserve naturalness. Compared with Flow-GRPO-style methods, GROW keeps deterministic ODE sampling, avoids ODE-to-SDE conversion, and does not require per-step likelihood evaluation. The paper also argues against purely positive exponential weighting: in bounded, tightly clustered TTS rewards, it does not provide enough contrast and can be dominated by the shared imitation component.6:T433,Experiments are run on LibriSpeech-PC test-clean and the English and Chinese Seed-TTS test sets in a zero-shot setting. The main metrics are WER, speaker similarity, and UTMOS for naturalness. Averaged over the three test sets, GROW reduces WER from 2.016 to 1.558, an absolute drop of 0.458, and increases speaker similarity from 0.676 to 0.715, an absolute gain of 0.039, while keeping UTMOS at roughly the pretrained level. The paper reports that these gains surpass the DiTAR-GRPO baseline overall. On efficiency, GROW with 10-NFE training rollouts reaches performance comparable to its own 32-NFE setting, and is about 2.9× faster to train than the 32-NFE DiTAR-GRPO baseline. At matched training-rollout NFE, GROW is still about 1.1× faster. All final evaluations use 32 NFE to keep inference quality comparable. Ablations cover rollout NFE, CFG, reward weighting variants, and reward composition. The main empirical message is that group-relative advantage works better than naive reward weighting, and the W2 anchor is important for stability and quality retention.7:T76c,ARMOR has two main components.

The first is Anchor Rollout. In standard on-policy RL, rollouts come only from the current policy, so the training distribution can quickly collapse together with the policy itself. This reinforces a small set of shortcut reasoning patterns. ARMOR injects off-policy samples from the reference policy during rollout and treats them as anchors. The point is not merely to add more data, but to explicitly preserve solution modes already present in the reference distribution, which are more likely to be broad and generalizable. This differs from reverse KL in an important way: KL penalizes deviation from the reference, but it does not guarantee that representative reference samples are actually present in the training stream. Anchor Rollout stabilizes the process at the data-distribution level.

The second is Mixed Optimization. Instead of adding KL as an auxiliary penalty, the method directly optimizes a mixed policy, alpha times the current policy plus one minus alpha times the reference policy. The intended effect is an adaptive trust region. The current policy is still allowed to explore beyond the reference when it finds better reasoning paths, but the optimization target never fully discards the reference distribution. According to the authors, this addresses both failure modes of reverse KL: Anchor Rollout mitigates instability from mode collapse, while Mixed Optimization mitigates stagnation from overly uniform regularization.

The paper develops the method in the GRPO and DAPO setting. It reviews GRPO’s clipped objective and loss-level reverse KL, then uses DAPO as a primary baseline because DAPO without KL makes over-optimization easier to expose. ARMOR is therefore positioned as a plug-in layer on top of existing RL pipelines, modifying both rollout generation and the optimization target rather than replacing the whole algorithm.8:T642,From the available full-text excerpt, experiments cover multiple base models, including Qwen2.5-Math-7B and Qwen3-8B-Base, and multiple RL algorithms, including DAPO and QAE. The main empirical claim is that ARMOR narrows the reward-validation gap: training reward continues to improve, while validation performance remains stable for longer and keeps increasing instead of turning downward early as in the baseline.

Although the excerpt does not include the full result tables, several conclusions are already clear. First, the paper explicitly compares reverse-KL-based stabilization against ARMOR and argues that reverse KL alone does not prevent collapse onto shortcut modes. Those ablations are central, because the method’s conceptual claim is that the two components address two different failure modes: instability and stagnation.
