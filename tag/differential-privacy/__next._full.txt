18:T4b7,The framework is “privacy in embedding space + guided diffusion decoding.” (1) Encode real text into continuous semantic embeddings; run DP clustering/aggregation in that space to obtain privatized semantic prototypes or a target semantic distribution. The DP guarantee is argued to carry over to the final synthetic text by DP’s post-processing property, as long as generation depends only on the DP outputs. (2) Use a masked diffusion language model that denoises a whole sequence in parallel. (3) Introduce Guided Diffusion: at each denoising step, steer the sampled discrete sequence toward the target semantic embedding by minimizing a semantic distance/score mismatch. (4) Two key mechanisms make continuous guidance effective for discrete sampling: semantic-aware sampling (bias token selection using a semantic alignment score, not just local token likelihood) and semantic-aware re-masking (re-mask positions that contribute most to semantic deviation so later steps can correct early mistakes). (5) The same interface is extended from DP to DSI aggregation, aiming at formal protection against corrupted sources and memorization, while keeping the generation stage as a post-processing step.19:T44d,Skim path that preserves the key technical content:
1) Introduction: the argument that DP in token space is inherently high-variance and utility-destructive is the central motivation.
2) Preliminaries: confirm the exact DLM variant (masked DLM / discrete diffusion) and how masking/denoising steps are defined.
3) Method: focus on the concrete implementation of semantic-aware sampling and semantic-aware re-masking—how semantic scores are computed (which encoder, differentiability/approximation), how they are combined with token probabilities, and how re-masking selects positions/ratios.

Expect ~30–45 minutes for a solid skim; deeper reading is needed if you plan to reproduce or adapt the privacy accounting and the guidance mechanics.1a:T8ce,The algorithmic proposal is DP-Muon, but the real contribution is the analysis. Muon can be viewed as applying approximate orthogonalization to momentum for matrix-valued parameters. More abstractly, it fits into a linear minimization oracle (LMO) framework. This unifies Muon with Scion, SignSGD, and normalized SGD as different geometries of direction selection over norm balls.

The key theoretical observation concerns the expected clipped gradient, μ(g)=E[clip_c(g+ξ)]. The paper shows that it can be decomposed into a positive scalar multiple of the true gradient g plus a residual term r(g) that changes direction. Since an LMO is invariant to positive rescaling, LMO(λg)=LMO(g) for λ>0, the dominant effect of clipping is invisible to the update rule. Only the directional residual enters the optimization error, and that residual is controlled by the centered gradient variance. This is the central idea of the paper. It avoids the usual strong assumption of bounded raw per-example gradients and does not require the clipping threshold to grow with the horizon.

Building on that decomposition, the authors derive a convergence guarantee for private momentum-LMO methods on smooth nonconvex objectives in any finite-dimensional norm. The bound separates optimization error, momentum tracking error, sampling noise, privacy noise, and a clipping floor. The floor scales as ρ1 min{σ, σ^2/c}, where ρ1 is the norm-conversion constant into the relevant dual norm. The interpretation is that passing clipped gradients through an LMO costs at most a geometric conversion factor relative to clipped SGD, rather than introducing a qualitatively new instability. At fixed noise scale, the rate is about O~(K^-1/4); with noise calibrated to an (ε, δ) privacy budget, the dependence matches the familiar DP scaling.

Algorithmically, DP-Muon still follows the standard central example-level DP pipeline: per-example clipping, Gaussian noise addition, averaging, then momentum and Muon orthogonalization. Privacy comes entirely from the first stage; the optimizer-specific part is post-processing. The novelty is therefore not a new privacy mechanism, but an explanation of why Muon-style post-processing remains analyzable under active clipping.1b:T5e6,The empirical study has two parts: validation of the theory’s implications and optimizer comparisons under DP. The experiments cover CNNs and a 30M-parameter GPT language model, with sweeps over batch size, learning rate, and momentum, comparing DP-SGD, DP-Adam, DP-Muon, and Scion. Two findings stand out. First, under DP the optimal batch size shifts upward by one to two orders of magnitude relative to non-private training. That alone is an important recipe change. Second, optimizer ranking becomes batch-size dependent; unlike the non-private setting, where Muon tends to dominate more consistently, there is no single winner across the DP regime.

On CIFAR-10, DP-Muon achieves the lowest mean test loss at all but one batch size, although at intermediate batch sizes the gaps are often within one standard deviation. So the advantage is real but not overwhelming. On language modeling, DP-Muon is best at small batch sizes, DP-Adam is strongest near the optimal batch size, and Scion performs best at large batch sizes. This is consistent with the theory: once clipping and privacy noise interact, geometry, momentum dynamics, and batch-dependent noise scale jointly determine which update rule is preferable.

If the full paper provides more detail, the most useful pieces for LLM researchers are the GPT setup, the privacy budget, the token budget, and the computational overhead of Muon’s orthogonalization. The language-model batch-size sweeps are much more informative than the vision results.1c:T541,For a skim, start with Section 3. The goal is not to absorb every proof, but to understand two claims clearly: clipping mainly induces a positive rescaling along the true gradient, and an LMO is blind to that rescaling, so only the directional residual matters. Once those two points are clear, the rest of the theory becomes much easier to place. Then read Theorem 1 and the surrounding discussion to see how the error terms are separated, especially the clipping floor and the role of ρ1.

For experiments, prioritize the language-model results over the vision section. Look first at the batch-size sweeps, then at the optimizer comparisons near tuned hyperparameters. For a deeper read, include the appendix discussion against concurrent work, especially the distinction between centered-variance assumptions and raw-gradient moment assumptions, and why this paper does not need a clipping threshold that grows with training horizon.

And since the language-model experiment is only a 30M GPT, how far these conclusions extrapolate to billion-scale pretraining remains open.1d:T55f,There are three main technical moves. First, the paper formalizes a subgroup-targeted membership inference game. Targets are sampled from a designated pool, while the rest of the private dataset is sampled from its complement. This makes it possible to audit high-risk groups, random controls, or unions of groups under a common reference distribution. Second, the authors adapt normalized-evidence membership inference to synthetic text releases. The exact likelihood-ratio test over an entire released synthetic dataset is intractable, so they replace it with measurable proxy evidence extracted from the release, such as n-gram overlap and embedding-based distances. These proxies are then normalized through scenario-dependent calibration. The separation is important: evidence comes from the release itself, while calibration captures attacker knowledge. Third, attacker capability is modeled explicitly through three release-based scenarios: release-only, raw-reference, and generator-assisted release. This lets the audit distinguish what can be inferred from the released samples alone, what auxiliary reference data adds, and how much stronger attacks become if the generator is also available. The paper also goes beyond pool-level metrics by estimating per-record vulnerability scores from repeated attack episodes and relating them to intrinsic record attributes.1e:T522,The study spans four datasets, three DP text generators, five privacy budgets, and 32 proxy attacks. For high-risk records under non-private generators, the strongest attacker reaches AUC 0.79–0.83, with 34–61% true-positive rate at under 5% false-positive rate. Even a release-only attacker can recover 39–52% of true members in that setting, which means the released synthetic text alone can carry substantial membership evidence. The more important pattern is heterogeneity. High-risk pools remain more distinguishable than random controls under every DP budget, so DP does not flatten risk uniformly across groups. That concentration is the most actionable finding, because it suggests that identifying and treating vulnerable records may matter as much as tightening epsilon. The paper also compares mechanisms, proxies, and attacker scenarios, showing that residual leakage depends on both the data distribution and the synthesis method.1f:T841,技术上分成两个层次。第一层是 PrivBoN。标准 BoN 是从基模型采样 n 个候选，用奖励模型打分后取 argmax。作者在每个候选分数上加入独立 Gumbel 噪声，尺度设为 sigma=2Δr/ε，其中 Δr 是奖励模型对相邻偏好数据集的敏感度。这样做有两个效果：从隐私角度，它等价于指数机制，直接给出 ε-DP；从优化角度，带 Gumbel 噪声的 argmax 在大样本极限下对应 softmax 选择，也就是求解一个 KL 正则化的最优策略。论文进一步把这个 regularization 强度与 Huang 等人关于 BoN regret skyline 的理论接起来，证明当隐私预算超过阈值 ε*(x) 时，DP 所要求的噪声强度正好落在 regret 最优区间，因此不会额外损伤对齐。

从方法取舍看，作者没有走训练时 DP-RM 或 DP-RLHF 的路线，因为目标是冻结模型、服务时对齐；也没有只做 heuristic smoothing，因为那样没有正式隐私保证。选择 Gumbel/指数机制和 Gaussian 两阶段机制，都是为了把 selection rule 写成可证明的随机机制，而不是把隐私当作附带噪声工程。20:T87e,The method has two layers.

The first is Private Best-of-N (PrivBoN). Standard BoN samples n candidates from a base policy, scores them with a reward model, and returns the argmax. PrivBoN adds i.i.d. Gumbel noise to each reward score with scale sigma = 2Δr/ε, where Δr is the reward-model sensitivity under neighboring preference datasets. This gives ε-differential privacy because noisy argmax with Gumbel noise is equivalent to the exponential mechanism. More interestingly, in the large-n limit the same mechanism implements a KL-regularized alignment optimum: the induced selection rule becomes a softmax policy over rewards. The paper then connects this regularization strength to the regret skyline from prior BoN theory, showing that when the privacy budget exceeds a critical threshold ε*(x), the privacy-imposed noise is exactly in the regret-optimal range. In that regime, privacy is effectively free from an alignment-regret perspective.

The second is Private Inference-Time Pessimism (PrivITP). PrivBoN depends on an unknown coverage coefficient C^{π*}, so the favorable threshold cannot be verified in practice. PrivITP addresses this by combining χ²-regularized rejection sampling with a two-phase Gaussian mechanism. The key move is not simply adding Gaussian noise, but reformulating a ReLU-based rejection sampler as a randomized-threshold mechanism. That yields an ex-post approximate-DP guarantee whose bound is independent of the candidate count n and depends only on the realized halting time. The regret analysis uses a smoothed-weight policy comparison lemma that controls value gaps through the ℓ∞ distance between policy weight functions, leading to a bound of the form O(sqrt(C^{π*}(ε_RM^2 + R_max σ))). This cleanly separates reward-model error from privacy cost and explains why PrivITP is more robust when coverage is unknown.

A notable design choice is that the paper does not solve the problem by retraining the reward model with DP or by using heuristic smoothing. It treats inference-time selection itself as the object of study and chooses mechanisms whose stochasticity is analytically tied to both privacy and alignment.21:T5b6,The empirical study spans four reward models, two language models of different sizes, and three benchmark families: GSM8K, MMLU, and MATH. The main findings are consistent with the theory.

1. Standard BoN is not scaling-monotonic under reward-model error. As N increases, performance can peak and then degrade, which is the practical signature of reward hacking.
2. PrivBoN mitigates this failure mode. With theoretically calibrated Gumbel noise, the performance curve as a function of N becomes much more stable, and the method preserves the benefit of test-time compute without over-amplifying reward-model error.
3. PrivITP consistently matches or outperforms PrivBoN at comparable privacy levels, especially in regimes where large candidate sets make BoN more brittle.
4. In multi-query deployment, the ex-post composition structure matters. When combined with the FSRC accounting framework, PrivITP can answer substantially more queries under a fixed privacy budget than standard worst-case composition.

The most informative part of the experiments is not any single headline number, but the shape of the scaling curves: whether BoN degrades at large N, whether the private mechanisms restore monotonicity or at least suppress the pathology, and whether the observed privacy-performance tradeoff matches the threshold story from the theory.
