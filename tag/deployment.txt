18:T4ff,The method, DPQ (Doubt-Preserving Quantization), is a family of pre-quantization calibration-data recipes rather than a new quantizer. The pipeline is lightweight. First, run the full-precision model on candidate examples. Then select target-aware 'fragile' examples using FP uncertainty signals. Two signal families matter most: high-doubt examples, such as high entropy, low confidence, or small top-2 margin; and answerability-boundary examples, especially for answer/no-answer settings like SQuAD2. These are mixed with generic anchor examples and then passed to an unchanged quantizer such as GPTQ or AWQ. The theoretical argument is modest but useful: if the deployment target puts more mass near decision boundaries, while the calibration mixture is dominated by generic anchors, the optimal calibration set will differ from the one preferred by broad MCQA-style targets. The paper calls this mixture mismatch. The framing is built around two preservation risks: a distributional risk that measures deviation between quantized and FP predictive distributions, and a boundary risk that measures decision flips in low-margin regions, optionally reweighted toward answerability or abstention-heavy inputs. This is a better fit for real systems than accuracy-only calibration.19:T539,The evaluation spans 8 language models, 9 NLP benchmarks, and 22 comparison methods or configurations. The metric suite is broader than standard quantization papers: besides ECE, adaptive ECE, NLL, and Brier score, the authors report FP-behavior metrics such as FP agreement, JSD between quantized and FP predictive distributions, confidence shift, top-two margin drift, and margin correlation. On SQuAD2 they also evaluate answerability-boundary behavior directly. Three findings stand out. First, the best calibration recipe depends strongly on the target. For boundary-sensitive answerability preservation, a high-boundary mixture such as DPQ-s128-r75 works best. For broad MCQA, milder recipes such as DPQ-r50 or single-signal confidence-only / entropy-only selection are often better. Second, accuracy can hide substantial uncertainty drift: some settings preserve top-1 behavior while materially changing confidence and margin structure. Third, preservation signals transfer only partially across quantizers. The post-hoc calibration and AWQ analyses suggest that some useful signals are not tied to one quantizer, but the best score-preservation recipe still remains quantizer-specific. The paper defines four aging mechanisms. Compression aging happens when information is already underspecified or over-compressed at write time, so future-critical details are lost. Interference aging happens when many similar memories accumulate and the target fact becomes buried or confused with nearby entities. Revision aging happens when changed, retracted, or derived state is not updated correctly. Maintenance aging happens when lifecycle operations such as flushing, recompaction, migration, or prompt changes silently alter behavior. To make these mechanisms measurable, the benchmark uses a temporal dependency DAG that encodes cross-session dependencies, superseding facts, confusable entities, and controlled lifecycle events. Evaluation is then performed over agent trajectories, producing aging curves over an operational lifetime rather than a single snapshot score. A particularly useful design choice is the use of paired counterfactual probes. One probe replaces the agent’s retrieval with oracle retrieval over the agent-written memory; another replaces both writing and retrieval with gold context. This helps localize failures to the write/store/retrieve/use pipeline. If oracle retrieval fixes the issue, retrieval is the likely bottleneck; if even gold context does not fix it, the problem is more likely in memory utilization, prompting, or the harness itself.
