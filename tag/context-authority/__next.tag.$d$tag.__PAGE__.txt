9:T5d8,There are two main components. First, FakeContext-bench. Each instance contains demonstrations consistent with a contextual rule P, a query, and two reference answers: y_R under the real rule and y_P under the misleading contextual rule. Outputs are categorized into Reality, Context, or Other using a deterministic rule-based judge, with human validation of the scoring protocol. The benchmark spans seven domains: physics, mathematics, chemistry, biology, statistics, history, and computer science. The point is not domain difficulty, but controlled construction of cases where the context is coherent yet lacks real authority.

Second, Jurisdiction In-Context Learning (J-ICL). The method keeps the standard few-shot interface and uses only query-only LM loss. In J+ episodes, the demonstrated rule genuinely applies to the query, so the correct answer should follow the context. In J- episodes, the demonstrations still support a coherent rule, but that rule is private, conflicting, or undeployed, while the query requires the reality-grounded or formally valid answer. No explicit authority labels, auxiliary classifier, or rationale supervision is added. The model is expected to infer applicability from natural-language cues and episode structure. This is the key distinction from methods such as MetaICL or Symbol Tuning, which mainly improve rule extraction from demonstrations. J-ICL tries to train both rule induction and rule applicability judgment within the same prompting format.a:T63d,The empirical story has three important parts. First, baseline diagnosis: both proprietary and open-weight models are vulnerable on FakeContext-bench. Larger scale and stronger pretraining do not reliably yield context-authority discrimination. The failure is often not that the model misses the demonstrated pattern, but that it successfully infers the false rule and applies it where it should not.

Second, existing ICL-tuning methods can worsen the problem. The paper reports that prevalent ICL fine-tuning approaches improve conventional few-shot performance but can reduce reality-grounded accuracy by as much as 14.95 percentage points relative to the base model under misleading context. This is the central result, because it shows a systematic objective-level trade-off rather than a mere capability gap.

Third, J-ICL mitigates that trade-off. Training with mixed J+ and J- episodes improves robustness to misleading context while preserving, and in some settings improving, standard ICL ability. The abstract does not expose the full table of numbers, but the full paper appears to emphasize: (1) higher Reality rates and lower Context rates on FakeContext-bench, (2) no major collapse on conventional few-shot tasks, and (3) ablations showing that the gain does not come from simply adding negative examples, but from exposing the model to both apply-context and reject-context episodes in a shared format.
