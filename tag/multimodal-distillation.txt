18:T788,The method builds a proxy for the “visual direction” using a controlled intervention, then projects the teacher correction onto that direction. During training, the student generates prefixes on the full image x^0. A fixed teacher is queried on the same textual prefix under two training-only views: an evidence-present view x+ and an evidence-removed or degraded view x-. The difference between their centered log-probabilities defines u_t, a signed proxy for how revealing or removing the relevant evidence changes token odds. The sign matters: visual evidence can support some candidates and actively refute others.

VAD then avoids directly matching the privileged teacher distribution. Instead, it starts from the teacher correction relative to the student distribution and extracts the component aligned with u_t through a regularized projection. Intuitively, the teacher may want to move the student in many directions, but only the part consistent with the intervention response is treated as distillable visual correction. The remaining component is kept as an unexplained residual rather than being assumed to be useful supervision. A new target is reconstructed around the student’s current full-image distribution: tokens supported by the intervention are raised, tokens refuted by the intervention are suppressed, and teacher shifts not captured by the proxy are excluded.

Two stabilization choices matter. First, the method keeps a weak regularizer derived from the original teacher correction, so optimization does not rely entirely on a noisy counterfactual proxy. Second, u_t is split into support and refutation branches, allowing separate control over which candidates should be promoted and which should be pushed down. This is a meaningful departure from positive-only visual-advantage weighting. The paper’s central claim is that VAD changes what to distill, not merely how strongly to distill it.19:T65c,The evaluation uses 4B and 9B students on six fine-grained visual benchmarks. The most important empirical signal is not a single leaderboard number but a set of consistent diagnostics. First, the paper shows that in Vision-OPD only 23.2% of the strongest response-token corrections for the 4B model and 22.8% for the 9B model are strongly aligned with the evidence-conditioned teacher response. That diagnostic is the empirical basis for the source-mixing claim.

Second, VAD consistently outperforms direct privileged-view distillation and visual-advantage weighting across both model scales and all six benchmarks. The introduction does not expose the full table values, but the paper presents the gains as broad rather than concentrated in one task, which supports the target-reconstruction argument.

Third, the token-level semantic analyses are particularly useful. The intervention-derived proxy and the proxy-aligned correction are enriched in visually relevant, task-relevant, and answer-correct tokens, rather than generic language continuation tokens. This is one of the stronger pieces of evidence that the method is capturing visually attributable correction instead of introducing another heuristic bias.

Fourth, controlled offline studies indicate that the reconstructed targets preserve useful visual correction while filtering teacher shifts that the proxy cannot explain. The key ablations to inspect are the removal of the projection step, the support/refutation split, and the weak teacher regularizer, since those determine whether VAD is a coherent training recipe rather than just counterfactual scoring.1a:T451,For a quick read, focus on three parts. First, read the introduction carefully, especially the critique of Vision-OPD, VA-OPD, and V-Zero, along with Figure 1 and the ~23% alignment diagnostic. If that premise is not convincing, the rest of the method is much less compelling. Second, read the method overview, Figure 2, Algorithm 1, and the definitions of u_t, centered log-probabilities, the regularized projection, and the support/refutation split. Those sections tell you whether the paper is doing genuine target reconstruction or just another weighting scheme. Third, read the token semantic analysis and the offline target studies; they are more informative than the headline benchmark tables.

A practical skim should take about 25 to 40 minutes. If the goal is only to decide whether to track the idea, the introduction, method overview, main result table, and analysis figures are enough. If the goal is adaptation or reproduction, then inspect how evidence-present and evidence-removed views are constructed, the cost of the extra teacher queries, and the exact form of the regularization term.1b:T6b8,The central tool is answer divergence, denoted as Delta, used as a proxy for the distillation value of a prompt. Instead of estimating sequence-level divergence between teacher and student distributions, which is expensive and noisy for long multimodal reasoning outputs, the paper measures divergence over the induced discrete answer distribution. Prompts with Delta approximately zero are treated as zero-delta prompts; prompts with non-zero Delta are considered informative because they reveal a functional gap between teacher and student.

A key observation is that simple filtering is not enough. If one only keeps non-zero-delta prompts from existing datasets, the remaining pool becomes much narrower in skill coverage. So the authors do not stop at selection. They build a staged synthesis pipeline. First, seed-guided synthesis expands the prompt pool from existing chart/document datasets, using them as seeds rather than as direct training data. The goal is to search for prompt variants with higher answer divergence. Second, skill-targeted augmentation explicitly generates prompts around reasoning skills that the student still fails to exhibit, so the dataset improves both disagreement and coverage.

The resulting dataset, DeltaPrompts, contains 200k synthetic prompts designed to satisfy two constraints: non-zero answer divergence and broad skill diversity. The same dataset can be used for on-policy distillation or as an off-policy SFT resource. The main difference from prior distillation work is conceptual: earlier work mostly treated prompts as fixed and focused on the objective, decoding, or policy mismatch; this paper treats the prompt distribution itself as the primary optimization target.1c:T630,The most important empirical findings are straightforward and useful. First, in standard chart/document reasoning datasets, up to 69% of prompts are effectively zero-delta, meaning the teacher and student induce the same answer distribution. This explains why distillation gains saturate quickly even when more data is added. Second, simply filtering to non-zero-delta prompts improves per-sample usefulness but hurts diversity, so filtering alone is not a sufficient solution. Third, the synthesized DeltaPrompts dataset produces consistent gains across settings.

The paper reports a 200k-prompt synthetic corpus and shows up to 15% relative improvement in on-policy distillation on chart/document reasoning and high-resolution perception-centric reasoning tasks. Importantly, these gains are obtained on top of an already strong student, such as Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, rather than on a weak baseline. The gains also transfer across model families: the same DeltaPrompts data improves GLM-4 models without regenerating the dataset. There is also out-of-domain transfer to STEM reasoning, suggesting that the prompts are not merely overfitting benchmark templates. In addition, DeltaPrompts works well as an off-policy SFT resource and outperforms standard off-the-shelf data mixtures.

The ablation logic is one of the stronger parts of the paper. It separates the effects of more data, higher Delta, and broader coverage. More zero-delta data saturates quickly. High-Delta data without coverage is brittle.
