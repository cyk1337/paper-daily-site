9:T71c,The paper does two things. First, it independently reproduces Orthrus training and inference with a configurable framework, so the conclusions do not depend on a single released checkpoint. Second, it evaluates “losslessness” at the sequence level under different numerical precisions instead of relying on downstream task scores.

For training, the AR backbone is frozen and a diffusion view is trained to propose multiple future tokens in parallel. The main methodological change is the distillation data distribution. Rather than using generic human-written continuations, the authors build a teacher-generated corpus: they take prompts from public Hugging Face datasets, ask Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B to produce greedy continuations, and train on those prompt-response pairs. This better matches the states the frozen AR model actually visits during generation, reducing the mismatch between teacher forcing and on-policy decoding. They keep prompts between 50 and 1,000 characters and obtain 4,113,358 distilled samples, training on eight H100 GPUs.

For evaluation, the criterion is exact token-by-token identity with the corresponding frozen AR model. Orthrus and the AR baseline are run under different precisions, and the generated trajectories are compared directly. The central argument is that Orthrus’s consensus mechanism may preserve equivalence in an ideal arithmetic model, but finite precision introduces tiny differences in logits, normalization, or cache state. Because decoding is a discrete decision process, those small perturbations can cross a decision boundary at some step and cause the entire continuation to diverge. The paper also analyzes the relationship between divergence and response-conditional perplexity, showing that uncertain generations are more sensitive to numerical perturbations.a:T664,There are three main empirical findings.

First, the core losslessness test. On 1,190 prompts from 12 domains, exact trajectory matching under BF16 is only 45% for the released Orthrus checkpoint and 43% for the independently trained model. In other words, more than half of the generations diverge at some point from the reference AR trajectory. Under FP32, the same evaluation yields exact matching, which supports the paper’s claim that the issue is not the abstract consensus logic but finite-precision implementation.

Second, training data distribution matters for parallel decoding efficiency. The independently trained model, built from teacher-generated on-policy distillation data, achieves higher Tokens Per Forward than the released checkpoint on most evaluated domains. The abstract does not list all per-domain numbers, but the full text presents this as a consistent pattern, suggesting that the diffusion view benefits from seeing prefixes that the backbone actually produces.

Third, divergence does not necessarily imply worse benchmark performance. The paper reports cases where Orthrus scores slightly higher than the corresponding AR model despite sequence-level mismatch under BF16. This is an important caution: benchmark parity does not establish exact inference equivalence, and small numerical perturbations can occasionally move a generation toward a benchmark-preferred answer. After that, read the training setup, especially the construction of the teacher-generated distillation corpus and the motivation for preferring it over generic human continuations. Finally, look at the analysis linking divergence to response-conditional perplexity, which helps explain when numerical precision matters most.
