c:T99e,There is no new model or training recipe here, but the argument is structured carefully. The paper starts by criticizing the “fix it in post” mindset: when undesirable behavior appears, the default response is finetuning, RLHF, or input/output filtering, rather than asking how the behavior was produced during pretraining or intermediate training. The authors use examples such as brittle safety finetuning and superficial bias mitigation to argue that post-hoc interventions often change outputs without addressing the underlying causal mechanism.

The paper then borrows from Kuhn’s criteria for scientific theories and asks what a science of AI should look like. The answer is not just better benchmark optimization. A scientific account should support empirical prediction, internal consistency across optimization/data/architecture/representation changes, compatibility with adjacent theory, broad scope beyond one benchmark, simplicity where possible, and the ability to generate new experiments.

The most actionable part is the three-level agenda around training dynamics. First is prediction: using early training signals to forecast later capabilities, failure modes, and alignment properties, not only final loss. This points toward studying gradient statistics, representation geometry, sample learning order, subdistribution absorption rates, and the timing of capability emergence. Second is intervention: if a trajectory is going wrong, one should be able to steer it through data mixture changes, sampling schedules, optimizer settings, regularization, or stage transitions. Third is design: replacing large amounts of trial-and-error with training procedures that are intentionally constructed to yield target properties.

Methodologically, the paper argues for longitudinal measurement rather than endpoint-only evaluation. That means linking checkpoints, internal states, behavior, and exposure history across training time. This stands in contrast to much of mechanistic interpretability, which often studies final models, and much of alignment work, which often studies post-trained behavior without tracing its developmental origin.
