19:T795,The authors extend language generation in the limit to a nested-language setting. The universe is a countable set of strings U. An instance is a pair H⊆F⊆U, both infinite. F is the ambient formal language, accessible through a membership oracle; H is the hidden valuable language. At round t, the generator observes the current literature prefix S_t, may query the oracle for F, and must output a fresh string not already in S_t. Outputs fall into three categories: valuable, trivial, or hallucinated. Performance is measured in the limit by coverage of H, using lower-density style notions, rather than finite-horizon accuracy.

The first main result, Theorem 3.1, is the key conceptual one: for exact or approximate breadth, the oracle does not improve what families are learnably generable beyond the oracle-free case. The characterization remains fiber-wise and is governed by Angluin-style tell-tale conditions. In plain terms, the verifier can identify the ambient formal shell F, but it cannot distinguish among candidate valuable sublanguages H that live inside the same F. Taste must come from examples, not from the checker.

The second main result, Theorem 3.2, shows what the verifier does buy: sound coverage. The oracle can eliminate hallucinations by ensuring outputs stay inside F while still pursuing coverage of unseen valuable statements. But this only removes one failure mode. It does not solve triviality.

The later results establish the paper’s strongest impossibility claim: any generator that achieves broad coverage of unseen valuable mathematics must emit infinitely many trivial statements. The trivia rate can become asymptotically negligible, but the total amount cannot be finite. That is the “flood and harvest” trade-off in the title. Valuable unrecorded mathematics is reachable only through an exploration process that necessarily produces an unbounded stream of certified but worthless outputs.1a:T455,This is a theory paper, so there are no model-scale experiments, training runs, or benchmark tables. The empirical role is played by constructions and formal separations. The motivating example in the introduction is useful: the authors show how one can cheaply generate a machine-verified statement decorated with famous mathematical objects while its actual content is still just 1+1=2. That example is not a theorem result by itself, but it captures the gap the theory formalizes.

The main takeaways from the results are threefold. First, the oracle does not help with value identification for breadth-style goals beyond identifying F. Second, it does help with soundness by ruling out hallucinations. Third, broad discovery of unseen valuable statements necessarily comes with infinitely many trivial outputs. What can be improved is the asymptotic rate of trivia, not its total elimination.
