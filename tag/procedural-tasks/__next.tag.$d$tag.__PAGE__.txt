9:T5dc,VBVR-Pro is a suite, not a single architecture. Its method has three main layers. First, task generation: the authors build 300 procedural tasks with randomized parameters, allowing scalable sample generation. Each task emits aligned outputs in multiple modalities, including videos, keyframe images, and interleaved text-visual representations. This is important because it lets image generators, video generators, and interleaved generators be trained and compared on the same underlying task distribution rather than on loosely matched datasets. Second, verifiable feedback: instead of relying on generic MLLM judges, the suite pairs tasks with structured semantic extraction and task-grounded verification scorers. Generated visual trajectories are parsed into structured semantics and then checked against task rules programmatically, producing rewards that can be used both for evaluation and reinforcement learning. The paper stresses that reward quality is validated at the instance level through arena-style human studies, not just through aggregate correlations. Third, optimization and substrate comparison: with these verifiable rewards, the authors run multi-task RL for visual reasoning and compare image, video, and interleaved generative substrates under controlled conditions. They also include diagnostics such as nearest-neighbor analysis and same-prompt visual counterfactuals to test whether gains come from reusable reasoning operations rather than instruction-template fitting.a:T664,The empirical story has three main parts. First is transfer. Models trained on VBVR-Pro reportedly gain consistently on seven external visual reasoning benchmarks, often by more than 20 percentage points. The named benchmarks include RISE-Video, V-ReasonBench, RULER-Bench, MME-CoF-Pro, VideoThinkBench, BabyVision, and IntelligentVBench. The authors use this to argue that broad coverage of reasoning mechanisms matters more than simply scaling the number of examples from a narrow task family. Second is reward quality. The paper analyzes failure modes of strong MLLM judges such as GPT-5.5, Qwen-3.7-plus, and Gemini-3.1-Pro, highlighting numerical imprecision, missed fine-grained evidence, and rule misunderstanding. In contrast, the task-grounded verifiers show stronger agreement with human judgments in instance-level arena studies and provide cleaner learning signals. Third is optimization. When these verifiable rewards are used for multi-task RL, performance improves steadily even from strong baselines, suggesting that the rewards are useful for training rather than only for offline scoring. The paper also includes anti-shortcut diagnostics: nearest-neighbor analysis suggests that external benchmark gains are not explained by close training-set matches, and same-prompt counterfactuals show sensitivity to changes in visual state rather than just textual prompts.
