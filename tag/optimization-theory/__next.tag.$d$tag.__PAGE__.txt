5:T5cb,The authors first isolate a simplified Muon update by removing momentum and Newton-Schulz implementation details. The remaining operation replaces each matrix gradient by its polar factor, keeping singular directions while removing singular values. This isolates the effect of gradient orthogonalization. The theoretical testbed is Burer-Monteiro factorized matrix sensing, which is connected to training two-layer quadratic neural networks and allows the gradient and Hessian geometry to be controlled through the sensing operator. The key model is mixed-spiked matrix sensing. Its sensing operator decomposes into signal, spike, and bulk components. The signal component is task-relevant anisotropy aligned with the ground truth. The spike component is strong but task-irrelevant anisotropy. The bulk component captures the remaining more isotropic directions. This construction mirrors a common feature of LLM training: gradients are highly anisotropic, but not every dominant direction is useful. The authors then analyze the landscape using a river-valley decomposition. The river direction corresponds to meaningful progress toward the ground truth, while hill directions capture sharp transverse deviations. In this geometry, GD quickly descends from the hillside into the river but then makes slow progress along the river. Muon moves rapidly along the sides of the valley, which improves exploration, but its normalized update can prevent fine convergence near the solution.6:T443,The analysis moves from the standard ℓ2 geometry to an ℓ∞/ℓ1 geometry. The objective satisfies ℓ∞ smoothness, stochastic gradients follow a separable noise model with a coordinate-wise variance vector σ = [σ1, ..., σd], and convergence is measured by ℓ1 stationarity. This setup aligns with SignSGD because each coordinate update only uses the sign of the stochastic gradient, so the relevant question is whether the coordinate direction is reliable, rather than how the gradient magnitude aggregates in ℓ2. The main bound for SignSGD is tight: E[min_t ||∇f(x_t)||_1] = Θ(sqrt(L∞Δ/N) + (||σ||_1^2 L∞Δ/N)^{1/4}). The paper then proves an ℓ1 lower bound for SGD under the same assumptions. Comparing the two gives a dimension-dependent separation: when coordinate noise is sparse or strongly heterogeneous, SignSGD has better dimension dependence, with up to a d-fold complexity gap in favorable cases. The framework is also extended to matrix optimization, where Muon is analyzed through a nuclear-norm stationarity measure with matching lower and upper bounds.7:T44e,The framework comes from Stack Theory. An environment is a set of possible states; a program is a subset of states; a vocabulary is the set of constraints expressible by the learner. A statement is a consistent conjunction of programs. A completion is any stronger consistent statement that extends the current one, and weakness is the number of such completions. Intuitively, a weak learned function leaves many compatible extensions and is less over-committed. The author extends this notion to continuous domains and proves reparameterization invariance by defining the quantity over behavior and expressible constraints rather than over a particular parameter coordinate system. To apply it to ReLU networks, the work introduces two vocabularies. The region-class vocabulary treats activation regions approximately independently. The feature-classifier vocabulary respects shared weights and reduces single-point extension checks to linear feasibility problems. The latter is more faithful to the architecture, although the pair proxy is still a sum of marginals rather than a full extension count.8:T73f,The method has three layers. The first is operator-level theory. The paper separates Transformer components into linear and nonlinear operators, and studies what happens when the input activation of an operator is compressed. The key result is that if the compressor is unbiased, then for a linear operator the induced gradient error is itself unbiased and does not propagate further upstream to earlier operators. For nonlinear operators, neither property holds. This distinction is the central theoretical contribution because it turns a vague engineering intuition into a concrete rule for module selection. The second layer is model-level convergence analysis. The authors study mini-batch SGD when all linear operators use activation compression, derive an upper bound on gradient variance, and decompose it into two additive terms: standard mini-batch sampling variance and compression-induced variance. The compression term is a sum over operators, controlled by the second-order magnitude of activation errors, so the total increase remains manageable rather than exploding layer by layer. Under the standard L-smoothness assumption, they further show that after the same number of updates, the stationarity metric degrades by at most a factor tied to the fourth root of the cumulative compression error. The third layer is the algorithm. The key observation is that once a linear operator's activation is low-rank, its weight gradient is naturally low-rank as well. The proposed method therefore jointly compresses activations and gradients by reusing the low-rank factors of compressed activations to maintain factorized weight gradients throughout training. This reduces both activation and gradient memory without introducing additional gradient error beyond what activation compression already causes, and with little to no extra computation.9:T51f,The experiments cover both Qwen and LLaMA families, and include pretraining datasets as well as fine-tuning datasets, which makes the evaluation broader than work limited to LoRA or SFT. The empirical study is organized around three questions. First, does the theory match practice: is compression on linear operators much safer than compression on nonlinear ones? Second, does joint activation-plus-gradient compression provide better memory savings than activation compression alone? Third, how does the method compare with other memory-efficient training approaches in the accuracy–compression trade-off? The reported conclusion is that the empirical results align well with the theory: compressing linear operators causes mild degradation, compressing nonlinear operators is much riskier, and the joint method achieves substantial memory savings while remaining competitive in model quality. The provided excerpt does not include the final numeric tables, so it would be unsafe to state exact compression ratios, throughput changes, or accuracy deltas. The most informative results are likely the ablations by operator type, the relation between compression rank or error magnitude and optimization degradation, and the comparisons against checkpointing, offloading, and prior low-rank activation methods.a:T475,Read this one in the order of theory first, experiments second. Start with the problem setup and the operator-level analysis. Focus on the two criteria that drive the whole paper: whether the induced gradient is unbiased, and whether the error propagates upstream. Once those are clear, the rest of the design becomes easy to follow. Then move to the model-level variance bound and convergence guarantee. You do not need every proof detail on a first pass, but you should understand which terms depend on compression error and why the effect is additive rather than multiplicative across layers. After that, read the algorithm section to verify whether the joint gradient compression truly adds no extra error and what low-rank factors must be stored in practice. In the experiments, prioritize: (1) linear vs nonlinear operator compression, (2) memory–quality trade-offs across models and tasks, and (3) comparisons with checkpointing, offloading, and prior activation compression baselines.
