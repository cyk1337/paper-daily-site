18:T8e2,The paper first performs a systematic diagnosis, then proposes a mitigation method. The study covers two representative MLLMs: Wan2.2 for multimodal generation and Qwen3-VL for multimodal reasoning. It evaluates multiple low-precision formats, including MXFP8, MXFP4, and HiF4, across a wide set of components: vision encoders, text encoders, attention blocks, feed-forward layers, multimodal projection layers, and MoE modules. The analysis shows that FP8 is nearly lossless, while FP4 degradation is highly heterogeneous. In Wan2.2, the visual generation backbone is the main bottleneck and the UMT5 text encoder is relatively robust. In Qwen3-VL, the vision encoder is especially sensitive, though the language backbone also degrades under FP4. Crucially, ablations separating weight-only from activation quantization indicate that activation quantization is the dominant source of performance loss.

Based on this diagnosis, the paper proposes Residual Fallback Quantization (RFQ). Unlike threshold-based outlier fallback methods, which detect large values and route them through a higher-precision path, RFQ uses a fully low-bit and differentiable decomposition. It first quantizes the activation into a primary FP4 representation, then explicitly computes the quantization residual and sends that residual through a secondary low-bit quantization path for reconstruction. Conceptually, the approximation is q(x) + q_res(x - q(x)), rather than a hard split between normal values and outliers.

This design has several practical advantages. It is differentiable and suitable for end-to-end training. It avoids threshold tuning, which is difficult when outlier behavior varies by module and evolves during training. It does not require full-precision fallback, making it more compatible with true FP4 execution. And because it models the current quantization error directly, it is better aligned with dynamic training-time activation statistics than PTQ-style calibration methods.

Methodologically, RFQ sits between static PTQ smoothing and mixed-precision QAT fallback. It accepts that FP4 cannot faithfully represent the full activation range in many MLLM modules, and therefore models the error instead of forcing the original distribution through a single quantizer.19:T644,From the provided full text, several empirical conclusions are already clear.

1. Precision formats separate cleanly in quality: MXFP8 is close to lossless, while MXFP4 and HiF4 incur substantial degradation. This suggests that 8-bit training/deployment is already fairly mature for current MLLMs, whereas 4-bit remains the hard regime.
2. Activation quantization hurts much more than weight quantization. The paper runs extensive ablations to isolate their contributions, and the main takeaway is that compressing weights alone is often manageable, but pushing activations to FP4 causes the major collapse.
3. Sensitivity is strongly module-dependent. In Wan2.2, the WanDiT visual generation backbone is the main failure point, while the text encoder is relatively stable. In Qwen3-VL, the vision encoder is the most sensitive, though language modules are not immune. This argues against a single uniform quantization strategy across the whole MLLM stack.
4. RFQ recovers a meaningful portion of the FP4 loss without using high-precision fallback. The abstract excerpt does not provide exact numbers, but the framing makes clear that RFQ primarily addresses activation-outlier-induced instability rather than offering a small average gain everywhere.

If the full paper includes detailed tables, the most informative numbers to inspect are the relative gaps among FP8, FP4, and HiF4 on both generation and reasoning tasks, and the decomposition into weight-only, activation-only, and joint quantization. For pretraining researchers, those diagnostics are more useful than a single final benchmark score.1a:T574,Read this paper with diagnosis first and method second. Start from the experimental setup and module-level ablations before diving into RFQ details.

First, read the introduction and the main experimental tables to confirm that the study spans both generative and reasoning MLLMs rather than a single architecture. That context matters when interpreting which conclusions are likely to transfer.

Second, focus on the activation-versus-weight ablations and the ranking of module sensitivities. This is the highest-information part of the paper because it answers why FP4 fails, not just whether it fails.

Third, move to the RFQ formulation and implementation details. Pay attention to how the residual is quantized, what extra storage or bandwidth it introduces, and what the training and inference overheads are. If your interest is low-precision pretraining, these system costs matter as much as the recovered accuracy.

Fourth, if the paper includes activation histograms, outlier statistics, or layerwise error heatmaps, those are worth careful inspection. They usually explain the mechanism more directly than the final benchmark tables.

The paper identifies usable symmetries in attention and MLP blocks. Value vectors in attention admit an SO(d_k) rotation, with corresponding transformations of W_V and W_O preserving the exact output; under GQA, each KV head group shares its rotation. Query/key rotations are constrained by RoPE: they must commute with the RoPE matrix, reducing the available symmetry to U(1)^{d_k/2}, which the paper does not exploit. In the SwiGLU MLP, the down-projection boundary also admits an invariance, but the rotation cannot be fully absorbed upstream because SiLU and the element-wise product do not commute with a general dense rotation. As a result, H·R remains a runtime operation while R^T can be fused into W_down. Instead of simulating quantization, the method adds a LogSumExp penalty on rotated boundary activations. LogSumExp acts as a smooth dense proxy for the infinity norm, which matters because the uniform quantization step size is driven by the largest outlier. A stop-gradient operator ensures that this auxiliary term updates only the rotation matrices, while the base model parameters are still trained only by cross-entropy. Compared with SpinQuant-style post-training rotation search, GaugeQuant does not require calibration data, a frozen model, or a non-differentiable quantization operator in the optimization loop.1c:T5fd,The main contribution is Ledger Residuals. A standard pre-norm transformer has a single width-d residual stream that every sublayer reads from and writes to. Ledger Residuals factor this into two width-d streams. The Deliberation stream D is a scratchpad: sublayers can write to it freely and can also erase stale content. The Commitment stream C is an append-only ledger: sublayers may commit information into it through a gate, but cannot overwrite what is already there, and the unembedding reads only from C. Initialization is D0 = embed(x) and C0 = 0. Each sublayer produces both an update to D and a gated contribution to C. The design is intentionally asymmetric: D is where computation happens, C is where the model stores the representation it will be judged on. Importantly, the architecture reduces to a standard pre-norm transformer at a known gate setting, so it serves as a controlled instrument rather than a completely unrelated model family. The causal test is straightforward. If massive activations are just a workaround for the residual stream’s dual role, then a protected decode-only channel should make them unnecessary. The authors also add a commit-sparsity penalty to encourage selective use of C, hoping to keep the commitment channel clean. Instead, stronger sparsity pressure makes the rebuilt outlier more persistent and more concentrated. That is the key technical result: the model actively recreates the feature in the readout channel rather than passively inheriting it from a flawed architecture.1d:T523,The experiments are modest in scale but well controlled. The authors train language models at 160M and 290M parameters and compare Ledger Residuals against standard transformers at matched loss, so the mechanistic conclusions are not confounded by large performance gaps. Three findings matter most. First, a protected decode-only commitment channel does not remove massive activations. The model rebuilds the canonical fixed-dimension outlier in C, and it remains strongly concentrated on the sequence-start token. Second, the rebuilt outlier is somewhat smaller in magnitude than in a standard transformer, but it is more sharply localized in both dimension and position. So the phenomenon is not gone; it is reorganized under the new architectural constraints. Third, the obvious remedy backfires. With stronger commit-sparsity regularization, dominant-dimension persistence rises from 0.58 to 0.96, and start-token concentration rises from 3.4 to 4.7. In other words, the model does not reduce reliance on the outlier channel; it makes that channel more stable and more focused. This paper tests a stronger claim: when the architecture is changed to provide a dedicated readout channel, does the model still choose to recreate the outlier? The answer is yes.1f:T836,OffQ has a simple three-stage logic: identify the outlier direction, concentrate outlier energy into very few channels, then convert that energy into offsets that can be absorbed by the quantizer zero-point.

First, it identifies a low-dimensional outlier subspace using a tailored top-1 PCA procedure. The motivation comes from recent observations that high-norm activation outliers share similar directions across layers, token identities, and sequence positions. Instead of expensive full PCA or learned transformations, OffQ extracts the dominant outlier direction with a lightweight top-1 procedure.

Second, it applies an orthonormal rotation so that large-magnitude activations are concentrated into one or a few channels. This is related to rotation-based methods such as QuaRot and SpinQuant, but the goal is different. Those methods mainly try to spread energy and make the distribution more Gaussian-like. OffQ explicitly tries to gather structured outlier mass into a minimal set of channels so that it can be removed cleanly afterward.

Third, it performs group-wise offset absorption. Channels are reordered into groups, each assigned one outlier channel. A specialized Hadamard rotation is then applied within each group to transform the outlier energy into a shared offset. That offset is absorbed into the zero-point of standard low-bit quantization. Intuitively, instead of forcing the quantizer to represent a very large outlier channel directly, the method rewrites its effect as a group-level baseline shift. The remaining values then have much smaller variance and can be quantized more accurately.

The design choices are important. It keeps uniform quantization rather than switching to non-uniform codebooks, which helps hardware compatibility. It avoids mixed precision, so execution remains homogeneous. It also avoids backpropagation-based rotation learning, reducing calibration cost and implementation burden. The main assumption is that outliers are indeed low-dimensional and directionally stable; if that structure breaks, top-1 concentration may become less effective.20:T645,The paper focuses on the difficult W4A4KV4 setting, where weights, activations, and KV cache are all quantized to 4 bits.
