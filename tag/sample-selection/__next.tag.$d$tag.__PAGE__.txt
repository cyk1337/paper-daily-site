9:T675,PGB Curriculum has two main components. The first is gradient-based learning-unit discovery. Motivated by an NTK-style view, the paper treats sample-level gradients as approximate descriptors of local optimization effects, projects them into a lower-dimensional representation, and clusters samples based on those representations. The resulting curriculum units are not grouped by task type or difficulty, but by whether they induce similar parameter-update directions under the current policy. The rationale is simple: group-level scheduling is meaningful only if samples inside a group have reasonably consistent optimization effects.

The second component is a predictive multi-armed bandit. Instead of using recent average advantage or success rate as a direct estimate of utility, the method trains a lightweight temporal predictor that jointly models external learning signals and internal policy states to forecast each group’s future utility. Those predicted utilities are then converted into a Boltzmann sampling distribution, increasing the probability of high-utility groups. The key distinction is that the scheduler predicts future usefulness rather than smoothing past usefulness, and it conditions on policy state rather than only on visible reward-like signals.

The method is designed to avoid two common failure modes. Some samples are already mastered and provide little marginal gain if oversampled. Others are currently too hard, producing nearly indistinguishable rewards and weak gradients. PGB tries to keep sampling mass on the region that is most useful for the current policy, and to move that region as the policy improves.a:T48d,The empirical results are straightforward. Relative to uniform-sampling GRPO, PGB improves in-domain performance by 8.8% to 14.3% and out-of-domain performance by 14.7% to 16.3%. Relative to existing curriculum baselines, it gains another 4.9% to 9.2%. The paper also reports better training efficiency: PGB reaches the final performance level of the GRPO baseline earlier in training, so the benefit is not only higher final quality but also faster progress.

The OOD gains are notable. They suggest the method is not merely oversampling easy in-domain prompts, but may actually be improving the quality of the learning signal.
