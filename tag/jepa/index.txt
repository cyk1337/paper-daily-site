18:T554,The theory defines a world as a finite set of states, with each modality providing an injective rendering of those states. A JEPA in each modality is trained on positive pairs whose underlying world states are sampled from a symmetric positive-pair distribution. For JEPAs with whitened second-order anti-collapse, the optimum is the spectral embedding of the positive-pair kernel. If multiple modalities share the same positive-pair distribution over world states, and the optimum is unique up to rotation, then each modality solves the same spectral embedding problem in different coordinates. Their representations therefore match up to a rotation, which the authors call the Platonic Representation Theorem. When the positive-pair distributions differ, misalignment is bounded by the mismatch between those distributions. The paper also analyzes nuisance variables: if positives preserve more of a modality-specific nuisance direction than the k-th world direction, a k-dimensional embedding will spend capacity on that nuisance, and no rotation can align it away. Good-view JEPAs use two observations of the same scene as positives; bad-view JEPAs form positives by corrupting or continuing a single observation. Next-token prediction is placed in the latter class, with prefix-continuation pairs and the softmax log-partition acting as an anti-collapse term.19:T682,The empirical results test the good-view claim across text, image, and video settings. With matched texts and encoders, good views usually produce the strongest alignment, from 145k Flickr captions to text associated with 940M web images, with both trained-from-scratch and frozen image encoders. Alignment follows a scaling law in the fraction of good views and the number of samples seen; the fitted law predicts 85 new runs with error comparable to rerunning the same configuration with another seed. On the PRH benchmark, a 33M-parameter text JEPA trained on the text of 50M web images reaches 0.125, compared with 0.034 on Flickr captions, roughly matching Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B. On the same corpus, the good-view JEPA reaches with 21.6M training samples the alignment that next-token prediction reaches with 900M samples, a 42x sample gap. A 109M-parameter text JEPA trained on the text of 940M web images and exposed to 116B text tokens reaches 0.184, above the evaluated language models up to Qwen2.5-72B at 0.154 and above the CLIP text encoder at 0.124. It also aligns better with several image encoders, including MAE, I-JEPA, and CLIP; for CLIP ViT-H, the reported comparison is 0.230 versus 0.210. The work further shows that an orthonormal-row linear map can connect frozen DINOv2/DINOv3 image encoders to a language model fine-tuned on text alone, reaching CIDEr 0.838 on COCO captioning. In an unpaired setting, the rotation can be estimated from the geometry of images and captions, outperforming the reported model pairs in the Schnaus et al. benchmark and exceeding a same-condition Qwen3-8B rerun by 17 ImageNet-100 zero-shot accuracy points.1a:T570,Read this one carefully. Start with Section 2.1, where the key step is showing why a whitened JEPA learns the spectral embedding of its positive-pair distribution and why shared positives imply alignment up to rotation. Then read Section 2.2 for the mismatch bound, which is the bridge from the clean theorem to imperfect real data. Section 2.3 is important for pretraining design because it gives a condition under which nuisance variables enter the embedding; this directly informs augmentation, masking, cropping, and multi-view sampling choices. Section 2.4 is the most relevant part for language-model researchers, since it reframes next-token prediction as an alignment-inefficient objective. In the experiments, focus on Sections 3 and 4, especially the good-view comparisons, the scaling law, and the sample-efficiency result of 21.6M versus 900M samples. Section 5 is useful as an application check: it tests whether a simple rotation or orthonormal linear map is enough to connect vision encoders and text-trained language models. The theoretical assumptions should be read with care: finite world states, injective renderings, identity predictors, whitening, spectral gaps, and uniqueness up to rotation are cleaner than real pretraining. The practical message still comes through clearly: improve the structure of positives before relying on next-token scale to produce alignment.1b:T442,The method combines bidirectional cross-modal latent prediction with per-modality distributional regularization. Given an image encoder and a text encoder, image embeddings are passed through a modality-specific predictor to predict stop-gradient text embeddings, and text embeddings predict stop-gradient image embeddings in the reverse direction. There are no in-batch negatives, no softmax denominator, no sigmoid pair classification over all image-text pairs, no learned temperature, and no momentum teacher. Collapse is handled by SIGReg applied separately to each modality, encouraging the marginal embedding distribution to be close to an isotropic Gaussian. During training, LeVLJEPA embeddings are not L2-normalized because the regularizer operates in Euclidean space; normalization is used only at evaluation time when cosine similarity is needed. This differs from CLIP, which uses symmetric InfoNCE; from SigLIP, which uses independent pairwise sigmoid losses; and from VL-JEPA, which uses a JEPA-style prediction framing but still relies on InfoNCE negatives to avoid collapse.1c:T461,The method is evaluated at two data scales: CC12M with 12 million image-text pairs and Datacomp-L with 92 million pairs. At the Datacomp-L scale, contrastive baselines retain an advantage on zero-shot transfer, which is expected because their loss directly optimizes global image-text similarity. LeVLJEPA remains on par under linear probing of the global class representation and improves background robustness. The largest separation appears in dense-token evaluations: LeVLJEPA outperforms contrastive baselines on semantic segmentation.
