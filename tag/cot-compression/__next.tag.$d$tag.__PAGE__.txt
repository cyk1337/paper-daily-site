a:T769,The method defines Visual Information Gain, or VIG, at each reasoning position. Conceptually, VIG measures how much the image reduces uncertainty about the token generated at that position, conditioned on the question and prior context. The paper motivates this as a conditional mutual information quantity, but the practical implementation uses an entropy difference computed from two forward passes of the same policy: one pass with the image, and one pass with visual tokens removed. If the image makes the predictive distribution much sharper, the token is treated as visually grounded and receives a higher VIG score. If the entropy barely changes, the token is likely recoverable from text context alone and is treated as filler.

These token-level scores are aggregated over the <think> span into a sequence-level reward, which is then combined with standard answer-accuracy and format rewards inside GRPO. A key design choice is that the method never rewards shorter chains directly. Shorter reasoning emerges because the policy learns to keep tokens that earn visual support and drop tokens that do not. This is different from length penalties, which can suppress both useless and necessary reasoning. It is also different from trace distillation, which depends on external compressed chains and inherits teacher bias.

Another notable choice is the use of entropy reduction rather than only the log-probability gap for the sampled token. Entropy reduction captures whether the image globally resolves uncertainty at that step, not just whether it happens to boost the chosen token. In that sense, the reward is closer to a token-level grounding diagnostic than a simple preference score. Methodologically, this is a post-training RL contribution rather than a new pretraining objective, but the signal itself is general and could plausibly inspire pretraining-time multimodal objectives.b:T664,The evaluation spans three Qwen3-VL-Thinking model sizes, six main multimodal reasoning benchmarks, and an additional test on R1-Onevision-Bench. The paper emphasizes the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier rather than reporting accuracy alone. On Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, VIG reaches 64.92% average accuracy and 52.42 ACC per 1k tokens. Relative to the strongest compression baseline, ThinkPrune, it improves accuracy by 2.37 points and efficiency by 39.4%.

The qualitative example in the introduction is also informative. On a simple geometry problem, the base model spends 823 reasoning tokens and still answers incorrectly. An L1-style length penalty reduces the chain to 412 tokens and recovers the correct answer. VIG solves the same problem in 105 tokens by directly reading the visual label and applying the correct relation. That example supports the paper’s claim that the issue is not merely verbosity, but visually disengaged reasoning.

From the available full text, the main empirical story is built around three comparisons. First, VIG is compared against base, plain GRPO, L1, ThinkPrune, and TALE, showing that a visual-dependence reward outperforms purely textual compression signals. Second, the gains persist across model sizes, suggesting the effect is not tied to one checkpoint. Third, on R1-Onevision-Bench, the method ranks first overall and first or tied-first in three of five subject categories, which argues against narrow benchmark overfitting.
