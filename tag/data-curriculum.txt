18:T66e,The method is a two-stage Diagnose-and-Generate loop. In the first stage, Diagnose & Allocate, the system estimates the effective supervision yield of the current practice distribution: given the current student and a set of prompts, how likely is it that sampled rollouts will contain both correct and incorrect trajectories, producing a usable preference pair? Rather than making topic-level decisions from raw counts, DIAG uses Empirical Bayes shrinkage to stabilize estimates under low sample counts. This prevents overreacting to noisy topics that happen to look good or bad due to small numbers. It also allocates budget between exploration and exploitation, so the curriculum does not collapse too early onto a narrow subset of topics.

In the second stage, the teacher generates follow-up problems conditioned on the student’s recent mistakes. This is the key departure from standard active learning. Instead of selecting uncertain prompts from a static candidate pool, DIAG actively creates new prompts that target the student’s current failure modes. The paper also uses uncertainty-weighted resampling to concentrate compute near the competence boundary rather than spreading it uniformly.

Conceptually, the method optimizes two quantities at once: pair yield and pair quality. Pair yield determines how much supervision is available per unit budget. Pair quality determines whether preference learning happens on examples that are actually informative for the current student. Compared with brute-force scaling of rollout width, DIAG tries to increase the probability that each sampled problem produces useful contrastive signal.19:T5af,From the provided full-text content, the experimental emphasis is not on a single benchmark headline but on showing that DIAG produces more informative preference pairs under the same or lower budget, and converts that higher signal density into better mathematical reasoning performance. The introduction explicitly claims that static practice distributions cause a rapid decline in natural contrastive supervision, while DIAG improves preference-pair yield through signal-aware pacing, Empirical Bayes topic scoring, and mistake-conditioned generation.

Because the supplied text does not include the full result tables, the confirmed findings are directional rather than fully numeric. First, fixed prompt pools become less effective over iterations as the student improves. Second, robust topic allocation outperforms greedy allocation based on raw observed yields. Third, mistake-conditioned generation is better than practice-agnostic generation at staying near the student’s competence boundary, which increases the chance of obtaining discriminative preference supervision. When reading the full paper, the most important numbers to verify are: the valid-pair rate per iteration, final benchmark gains under matched training budgets, and the variance or stability of different topic-allocation strategies. Since around 10% of FineWeb-Edu words lack AoA annotations, the authors impute missing values using a linear model over Zipf word frequency. A cheap FastText model performs broad filtering, and a much stronger but roughly 50x more expensive ModernBERT model is applied to about 266M samples whose grade-band prediction agrees with the AoA assignment. This step removes only about 0.1% of already retained documents, so it mainly cleans a residual tail rather than acting as a broad math filter. Stage four tightens the boundary further using term-based exclusion. Because the post-filter corpus is still larger than the target training size, the authors do not subsample uniformly. Instead, they identify terms disproportionately associated with Beyond-K–5 material and remove candidate documents containing them. The logic is explicit: sparse occurrences can still teach factual knowledge, so controlled-exposure studies should prefer over-filtering to leakage. Validation is done on CommonCoreText, a held-out dataset with ground-truth grade labels.
