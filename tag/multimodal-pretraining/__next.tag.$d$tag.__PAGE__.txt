9:T48f,ALPS uses a two-stage recipe. Stage 1 trains a ViT-B/16 from scratch with fixed sinusoidal positional embeddings and raw final-layer patch tokens. The same tokens are supervised by four objectives: image-text contrastive learning, autoregressive captioning, pixel reconstruction, and dense correspondence. The contrastive loss uses both web captions and sentences sampled from longer synthetic captions, with separate softmaxes so that captions for the same image are not treated as negatives. The caption decoder predicts the full synthetic caption from image tokens and previous text. A lightweight convolutional decoder reconstructs images from clean and noisy tokens with L1, SSIM, and image-gradient losses. Dense correspondence adds local spatial supervision across crops and scales. Stage 2 then performs cross-view self-distillation: a student sees an augmented view and predicts frozen Stage-1 teacher patch tokens at corresponding locations in a clean view. The staged design matters because it strengthens state information after a joint multimodal foundation has been established, instead of retrofitting a CLIP-like encoder and losing language alignment.a:T69f,The paper builds its case with three complementary analyses. First is context-invariance testing. The authors fix one anchor modality, especially speech, and re-encode the same input under different text and image contexts: a neutral prompt plus gradient image, a transcription prompt, an emotion-recognition prompt, a real photo, and a combined text+image change. They then extract layerwise token representations and compute CKA similarity between the neutral-condition representations and the altered-context representations. If early layers already perform strong cross-modal contextualization, the same speech input should diverge early across contexts. If those layers behave like an encoder, similarity should remain high for a substantial prefix of layers. Second is an activation-patching style functional test. Instead of only measuring representational similarity, they ask whether multimodal reasoning performance remains similar when modalities are encoded independently through early layers and fused only later, compared with full joint encoding from layer 1. If performance is preserved, early joint processing is not doing much task-relevant fusion. Third is encoder-style evaluation of the learned internal representations. The authors compare early speech-layer representations against established speech encoders such as WavLM and HuBERT, and they also evaluate these internal layers on standard encoder benchmarks like SUPERB and MIEB. The strength of the paper is that these three pieces fit together: invariance suggests delayed fusion, patching suggests functional separability, and benchmark transfer suggests that the lower layers really do learn encoder-like features.b:T595,The clearest quantitative result is the speech analysis on Gemma-4 12B. Using 1,000 utterances from LibriSpeech dev-clean, the authors build neutral and altered multimodal contexts and extract 48 layers of 3840-dimensional representations. For Gemma-4 12B, CKA similarity of speech-token representations stays very high, generally above 0.9, through roughly the first 12 layers despite changes in text and image context, and only then begins to decline with depth. When the internal layers are evaluated as standalone encoders on SUPERB and MIEB-style tasks, the outcome is mixed: they are competitive on some tasks, but they still underperform much smaller dedicated encoders on others. This matters because in many realistic settings, single-modality or modality-text data are abundant, while high-quality jointly sampled non-text data are the real bottleneck. The paper is also careful about its claim: MSP does not remove cross-modal priors altogether. It still depends on CLIP/CLAP-like encoders and modality-text supervision. The encoders are pretrained contrastive models, such as CLIP-like vision encoders and CLAP-like audio encoders. Training proceeds in two stages. Stage one is per-modality projector training, where standard modality-text data are used to train projectors so that each modality can be mapped into a shared language embedding space. Stage two is self-play composition training, which is the core idea. Given one observed modality, say an image, the system generates proxy text for an unseen modality, for example a textual description of what the corresponding audio might contain. This proxy text is encoded by a pretrained text encoder and then passed through a bridge plus an MLP projector to produce a representation in the shared language space. During training, the model sees the real observed modality together with the proxy-derived representation of the missing modality, but never the true image-audio pair. The goal is not to reconstruct the unseen modality exactly. The goal is to provide a stable semantic anchor that lets the model learn how to combine modalities in the shared space. Compared with approaches based on shared encoders or modular concatenation, MSP explicitly uses text as the bridge and adds a learned calibration layer to correct the mismatch between proxy-text embeddings and real modality embeddings. That calibration is important because raw text-encoder outputs do not naturally follow the same distribution as actual modality features.10:T601,The main experimental setting is strong and clean: no real audio-vision pairs are used in training, only image-text and audio-text supervision, while evaluation requires joint multimodal reasoning. The most informative findings are in the ablations rather than in any single leaderboard number. First, removing the learned bridge weakens alignment between proxy-derived embeddings and real modality representations, and multimodal reasoning performance drops accordingly. Second, proxy quality matters: if the generated proxy text is vague or semantically unstable, the model tends to fall back to single-modality reasoning or language priors. First, it replaces pixel reconstruction as the training signal for the discrete representation with feature reconstruction. Instead of the conventional encoder-quantizer-pixel-decoder pipeline, the tokenizer is trained with an encoder, a quantizer, and a feature decoder that predicts patch-level features extracted from a frozen pretrained vision model. The paper studies several feature sources, including SigLIP, SigLIP2, DINOv2, and DINOv3. The important point is not that feature losses are added on top of pixel losses, but that feature-space supervision becomes the primary and exclusive signal for shaping the encoder and codebook. Second, image rendering is explicitly decoupled from representation learning. A separate image decoder is still trained to reconstruct pixels from the discrete tokens, but the tokens are detached before entering this renderer. Pixel losses therefore improve visual reconstruction without pulling the tokenizer back toward pixel-friendly but generator-unfriendly representations. The paper also introduces global-augmented generative modeling. A global image representation, such as a CLS token from the encoder, is discretized and generated before the patch tokens.
