18:T5af,There are three technical moves worth noting. First, the authors define a row-associated bilinear operator for each gated-FFN output row and compute its Frobenius norm and singular spectrum exactly, instead of relying on a diagonal approximation. This is important because cross terms between hidden units can be substantial, especially in genomic models, so the diagonal proxy is not reliable for cross-model structural claims. Second, candidate rows are selected from activation-derived high-gain signals rather than from weight magnitude alone, and then compared against same-layer random controls and same-layer top-norm controls. This separates rows that are dynamically amplified from rows that merely look large in parameter space. Third, functional importance is measured through interventions: partial suppression and full ablation, evaluated by changes in the model’s native language-model loss; in one generative genomic case study, nucleotide composition shift is also tracked. For multi-row settings, the paper compares additive response models against richer calibrated and pairwise-interaction models to test whether effects are approximately additive or genuinely circuit-like.

Relative to earlier super-weight papers, the novelty is not another catalog of important parameters. The novelty is the explicit separation between structural descriptors and causal function, tested across a 22-model frozen census spanning text and genomics.19:T778,The empirical core is a causal census over 22 frozen models from text and genomics. As a retrospective calibration step, the authors revisit published super-weight coordinates in Llama-7B, Mistral-7B, and OLMo-7B. The down-projection output row containing the published super weight ranks first within its layer under their row-level proxy, which links the new row-based analysis back to the earlier scalar super-weight phenomenon.

The main findings are fourfold. First, activation-selected high-gain rows are functionally enriched: under both partial suppression and full ablation, they induce larger native-loss changes than five same-layer controls. Second, this enrichment is not a calibrated predictor. Signed full-ablation effects vary widely, from negligible or even negative changes to several-hundred-percent increases in native loss. So the candidate set contains both genuinely critical rows and structurally conspicuous but functionally weak ones. Third, neither leading spectral concentration q1 nor operator magnitude predicts the signed full-ablation effect within an architecture class, and the highest-norm rows in the same layer are often causally close to inert. This directly argues against a simple “bigger or sharper means more important” picture. Fourth, the case studies show qualitatively different causal organizations: DNABERT-2 exhibits a robust pretrained pair interaction, suggesting nontrivial circuit dependence, while in GENERator the disruption of a spectrally concentrated row produces a native-loss phenotype mediated mostly at the beginning-of-sequence position, together with a nucleotide-composition shift that tracks damage magnitude and does not require the row’s learned direction.

The paper is careful about effect distributions, controls, and failure cases rather than headline benchmark gains.
