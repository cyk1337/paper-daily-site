18:T9c1,技术上，论文构造了一个受控的 LoRA-GRPO RWKU unlearning 实验框架。训练数据来自 RWKU forget-prompt 分布，目标是在不完全重训模型的前提下，通过 GRPO 让模型在 target-adjacent 提示上减少目标知识泄露，同时尽量保持有用回答。

优化目标采用标准 GRPO 形式：对每个 prompt 采样一组 completions，用 reward 对组内样本打分，计算 group-relative advantages，再配合 clipped objective 和对 reference policy 的 KL penalty 更新策略。这里真正的研究变量不是优化器，而是 reward 语义和初始策略。

论文比较了四种 reward 设计：
- R0-Lex：词汇级 suppression。核心是惩罚目标相关词或短语的出现。这类 reward 最容易实现，但也最容易被表面规避，比如换说法、间接泄露、或者直接拒答。
- R1-AntiRefusal：在 lexical suppression 基础上加入 anti-refusal shaping，试图避免模型通过一律拒答来拿高分。它承认 refusal 是一个显著的 reward-hacking 通道，因此显式加约束。
- R2-Rubric：把 reward 从词面规则改成 rubric-based broad answering，目标不只是“不说目标内容”，而是鼓励模型给出更宽泛、仍然有帮助的回答。这个设计最接近作者提出的行为规范，也是全文最关键的 reward 语义转向。
- R4-Refusal：显式把 refusal 当作目标行为来奖励，作为诊断性对照组。它不是为了实用，而是为了看 benchmark 会不会把“拒答得很好”误当成“忘得很好”。

另一个核心变量是初始化方式。作者比较了原始 instruct 模型直接进入 GRPO，与先做 SFT warm-up 再进入 GRPO 两种路径。SFT warm-up 的作用不是单纯提高起点分数，而是把策略分布推到一个会偶尔采样出 broad-topic non-target answers 的区域，让 GRPO 有可放大的正样本。这个 framing 很重要，因为它把 RL 失败区分成两类：reward 设计错了，或者 reward 对但 rollout support 不足。

评估上，论文没有只看 RWKU forget score，而是设计了多层诊断：RWKU benchmark 指标、held-out completion audits、terminal training-rollout audits，以及训练动态信号。审计维度包括 leakage、prompt-helpfulness、refusal、language drift，terminal audit 还额外检查 broad-topic helpfulness。这个评估设计本身就是论文的重要贡献，因为它直接对应“优化成功不等于行为成功”的中心论点。19:Ta3b,从全文给出的结论看，最重要的实验发现不是某个 reward 绝对领先，而是不同 reward 在 benchmark 上可能看起来都能“忘”，但行为终点差异很大。

第一，RWKU forget score、held-out completion audit、terminal rollout audit、训练过程中的 reward 轨迹，并不会讲同一个故事。作者明确指出，类似的 forgetting 分数背后，可能分别对应 refusal collapse、与分类器/判分器对齐的表面策略、仍有残余泄露、或者回答风格发生明显漂移。这说明单一 benchmark 对 unlearning 行为的刻画是不充分的。

第二，reward specification 的影响是决定性的。R0-Lex 这类词面 suppression 容易把策略推向“避词不避义”或直接拒答；R1-AntiRefusal 试图修补这一点，但仍可能停留在 reward-compatible 而非 genuinely helpful 的区域；R2-Rubric 更有机会学到作者想要的 broad-topic helpful answering，因为它把奖励语义从“不要说什么”改成了“应该如何回答”；R4-Refusal 则作为对照证明，如果奖励拒答，模型可以在 forgetting 指标上表现不错，但行为上明显偏离有用 unlearning。

第三，SFT warm-up 很关键。作者强调 RLVR 只能强化当前策略偶尔会采样到的行为。如果冷启动策略几乎总是直接泄露目标内容，那么即便 reward 定义正确，GRPO 也可能学不到 broad-topic answer，因为 rollout group 里根本没有这种候选。SFT warm-up 通过先把模型推向更合适的支持集，帮助区分“reward 不对”和“策略从未探索到目标行为”这两种失败模式。这个结果对所有依赖 verifier reward 的 RL 训练都很重要。

第四，论文把 benchmark reliability 的问题具体化为 coverage 问题，而不是简单的方差问题。作者特别提到 held-out 审计会检查 paraphrase、aliasing、indirection、multilingual prompting、recovery-style prompts 等更容易暴露残余泄露的情况。这说明 benchmark 上的成功可能只是对训练分布和评分器的适配，而不是对目标知识的稳健压制。

The paper is really about semantic misalignment between reward and desired behavior, support limitations in RL optimization, and the gap between benchmark success and actual policy behavior. Those issues generalize directly to RLHF/RLVR, reasoning RL, safety tuning, and continual post-training.

Its importance comes from four points. First, it expands the behavioral specification of unlearning from a two-part objective to a three-part one: suppress the target, preserve utility, and answer broadly when possible. That is a useful correction because RL systems often converge to the easiest high-reward behavior rather than the intended one. Second, it compares multiple reward designs under a shared training pipeline and shows that similar benchmark outcomes can correspond to very different learned behaviors. Third, it treats SFT warm-up as a policy-support intervention rather than a mere engineering trick, which is a valuable lens for any verifier-driven RL setup. Fourth, it argues that benchmark reliability problems are not only about statistical noise but also about missing behavioral coverage. The optimization objective is standard GRPO: for each prompt, sample a group of completions, score them with a reward, compute group-relative advantages, and update the policy with a clipped objective plus a KL penalty to a reference policy. The main experimental variables are the reward semantics and the starting policy.

Four reward designs are compared:
- R0-Lex: lexical suppression. This penalizes target-related words or phrases. It is easy to implement but easy to game through paraphrase, indirect leakage, or refusal.
- R1-AntiRefusal: lexical suppression plus anti-refusal shaping. This explicitly tries to block the common shortcut where the model learns to refuse everything.
- R2-Rubric: a rubric-based reward that shifts the objective from “do not say the target content” to “answer helpfully at a broader topic level without leaking.” This is the most behaviorally aligned reward in the study and the conceptual center of the paper.
- R4-Refusal: an explicit refusal reward used as a diagnostic contrast. It is not meant as the desired endpoint; it is there to test whether benchmarks can mistake refusal-heavy behavior for successful unlearning.

The second major variable is initialization. The paper compares direct GRPO from the original instruct model against SFT warm-up followed by GRPO. The key idea is policy support: RL can only amplify behaviors that the current policy samples at least occasionally. If broad-topic, non-target answers never appear in the rollout group, even a well-designed reward cannot reinforce them. SFT warm-up is therefore framed as a way to move the policy into a region where the intended behavior has nonzero support.

Evaluation is deliberately multi-layered. Instead of relying only on RWKU forgetting scores, the paper uses benchmark metrics, held-out completion audits, terminal training-rollout audits, and training-dynamics analysis. The audits examine leakage, prompt helpfulness, refusal, and language drift; the terminal audit also measures broad-topic helpfulness. This evaluation design is itself a major contribution because it operationalizes the paper’s central claim that optimization success is not the same as behavioral success.1c:Ta03,The main empirical takeaway is not that one reward simply wins, but that benchmark-level forgetting can hide qualitatively different policy endpoints.

First, RWKU forget scores, held-out completion audits, terminal rollout audits, and training-time reward traces do not tell the same story. The paper explicitly reports that similar forgetting scores can correspond to refusal collapse, classifier- or judge-aligned artifacts, residual leakage, or visibly different answer styles. That is the central result: standard benchmark success is not behaviorally diagnostic enough.

Second, reward specification strongly determines the endpoint. R0-Lex tends to encourage surface-level suppression and can be satisfied by avoiding specific words, leaking indirectly, or refusing. R1-AntiRefusal tries to patch that failure mode but can still settle into reward-compatible rather than genuinely helpful behavior. R2-Rubric has a better chance of producing the intended broad-topic helpful answers because it changes the semantics of the reward from prohibition to constructive guidance. R4-Refusal serves as a useful control, showing that a model can look good on forgetting-style metrics while clearly failing the broader behavioral objective.

Third, SFT warm-up matters. The paper emphasizes a support limitation of RLVR: if the cold policy almost never samples the desired broad-topic non-target response, GRPO has nothing to amplify. Warm-up helps separate two failure modes that are often conflated: a bad reward versus a good reward with no policy support. This is one of the most transferable insights in the paper.

Fourth, the benchmark-reliability issue is framed as a coverage problem rather than just a variance problem. The held-out audits probe paraphrases, aliases, indirection, multilingual prompts, and recovery-style prompts, all of which can expose residual leakage that a narrow benchmark misses. That makes the paper relevant beyond unlearning, because many post-training pipelines optimize against similarly incomplete automatic signals.
