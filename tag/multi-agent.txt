18:T497,The contribution is an experimental design rather than a new model.
- Setup: N=10 agents (same base model) discuss one factual QA item for T=16 rounds. Each round every agent speaks once; speaking order is reshuffled each round. Agents condition only on the last H=5 rounds to control context.
- Adversary: k∈{0,1,3} attacker-controlled agents repeatedly advocate a fixed, plausible false answer. The attacker only posts messages (context-only attack).
- Dual measurement: after the initial state and after every round, each benign agent is privately probed in a separate call (not visible to peers) for its own answer. In parallel, each public chat message is labeled for stance toward the false claim (endorse/reject/hedge/ignore).
- Infection labeling: private answers are judged as true/manipulated/mixed/uncertain; only “manipulated” counts as infection, avoiding conflating uncertainty with belief change.
- The key analytical lens is the mismatch structure between public stance and private belief, revealing that the dominant failure is often private shift without public endorsement (minority-influence-like), which makes transcript-only monitoring unreliable.19:T433,Evaluated across 6 models and 3 factual-QA datasets (18 model–dataset cells) under k=0/1/3 attackers.
Main quantitative findings reported in the paper:
- No attacker: final infection is only 0.6% of benign agents.
- One attacker: final infection rises to 44.8%.
- Three attackers: final infection reaches 60.5%.
- Hidden infection gap (k=3): across exposed turns, private false belief occurs 51.6% of the time, while public endorsement of the false claim is only 19.7%.
- Mismatch asymmetry: when agents privately remain correct, they almost never endorse the false claim publicly (2.3% of turns). When agents privately become incorrect, they still often hedge or ignore publicly (58% of turns).
- Weak correction: agents who privately keep the true answer explicitly reject the false claim in only 61.9% of their public turns.
- Knowledge is not robustness: two models with similar factual knowledge (~85%) can differ drastically in how many facts they abandon under attack (25% vs 80%), suggesting conversational susceptibility is not explained by static knowledge alone.1a:T80b,The framework is a sequential-auction MDP. A task is modeled as a finite-horizon MDP, but actions are not chosen by one global policy. Instead, a society of agents bids for the right to act on the current state. The winning agent transforms the state and then resells the resulting state into the next auction. When the task reaches a terminal solution, the final reward is propagated backward through the resale prices, implementing a bucket-brigade style temporal credit assignment.

The starting point is the theory of Chang et al., where Vickrey auctions align local bidding incentives with the globally optimal MDP policy under perfect competition. The paper argues that this assumption is economically unrealistic for LLM societies. With exact clones, the winner pays essentially its true valuation, so expected profit goes to zero and participation becomes irrational. At the other extreme, if an agent is the only eligible buyer at some step, it behaves as a monopsonist and bids the minimum needed to win. The clearing price collapses, upstream contributors are underpaid, and the multi-step chain becomes unsustainable.

The proposed shift is to study imperfect competition. Agents are similar enough to compete, but differentiated by inference cost, prompting, heuristics, and capability. Since LLMs are poor zero-shot estimators of their own success probabilities and token costs, the paper does not rely on direct self-evaluation. Instead, each agent learns a lightweight valuation policy with reinforcement learning to decide whether to bid and how much to shade its bid. The paper benchmarks four bidding strategies: Mirror Descent, Thompson Sampling, Bayes-Optimal, and PPO. It also compares multiple auction rules, including first-price, second-price, and a credit-conserving Vickrey variant.

Methodologically, the contribution is less about one new algorithm and more about building an experimental framework that combines sequential auctions, LLM agents, learned valuation policies, and market-density analysis under imperfect competition.1b:T4ed,The experiments have two roles. One is to validate the economic failure mode: in synthetic settings, the authors show monopsony failure, where lack of competition collapses bids and prevents value from flowing upstream. The other is to test whether realistic intermediate regimes work: they run market-density sweeps and compare decentralized markets against centralized orchestration baselines on task success and agent profit.

The domains mentioned in the paper include math reasoning and a newly constructed synthetic news market. The main empirical conclusion is that no competition fails, but perfect clones are not required either. A middle regime of imperfect competition is sufficient to sustain positive profits while also achieving higher task success than centralized orchestration baselines. That is the main result to retain.

The provided full-text excerpt does not expose all numeric tables, so it would be unsafe to invent exact gains. What is clear is the experimental structure: systematic comparison across auction types, density sweeps over market competition, multiple bidding learners, and evaluation on both accuracy and profitability. That last point matters, because accuracy alone would miss whether the market is economically viable.1c:T732,The system has two main components. The first is 3DCodeVerse-2M. Instead of merely aggregating public code, the authors perform substantial representation unification and executabilization. They collect diverse 3D sources, including CAD construction sequences, Blender scripts, Shadertoy programs, Three.js / React Three Fiber projects, Thingiverse assets, and more. They then convert heterogeneous formats into runnable source code: for example, DeepCAD construction sequences are translated into CadQuery programs, and Articraft geometry routines are inlined while preserving URDF articulation specifications. Each sample is packaged as a standalone executable program with source files, assets, textual descriptions, provenance, and available verification records. The hard part here is not scale alone, but removing specialized or unavailable dependencies so that samples can actually run in the declared environment. The second component is the 3DCodeVerse Harness. It decomposes a complex 3D project into a DAG of coding and assembly tasks, lets multiple agents generate and integrate source files, executes and renders the result, and then uses an independent judge agent plus spatial consistency checks to evaluate it. The spatial checks are important because they prevent visually plausible but geometrically invalid outputs from scoring well. Feedback is converted into localized repair objectives, enabling repeated execution-evaluation-revision cycles. This differs from generic ReAct-style loops because the process is centered on executable artifacts, structured verification, and targeted repair rather than open-ended natural-language reflection. The harness is also designed as a data engine: it records successful programs, failed attempts, targeted repairs, and preference comparisons for later SFT and preference learning.1d:T453,The headline results are straightforward.
