First, it solves a minimal linear setting exactly. The setup uses two binary concepts, such as shape and color, giving four classes. The dataset kernel is assumed to have a structured block form called 2FS, which compresses the geometry into a small number of entry types and corresponding modes. In this basis, the representation can be decomposed into interpretable components: concept-aligned modes and an orthogonal interaction mode that reduces parallelism between concept vectors. This interaction mode acts as a noise direction for abstraction. With this decomposition, abstraction, measured as cosine similarity between counterfactual concept vectors, becomes an explicit function of mode amplitudes. The authors then derive exact gradient-descent trajectories for these amplitudes, yielding a closed-form training-time trajectory of abstraction rather than only a convergence statement.

Second, the theory is extended to arbitrary depth in deep linear networks. The key result is not that depth always helps abstraction. Instead, depth changes the relative learning dynamics of different spectral modes. Final abstraction and the maximum abstraction reached during training both depend on how depth reweights the competition between concept modes and interaction modes. This is a more nuanced statement than the common intuition that deeper representations are simply more abstract.

Third, the paper moves to infinite-width nonlinear networks. In this regime, it proves that nonlinearities weaken abstraction in features relative to preactivations. For ReLU in particular, the dynamics become more input-driven and less target-driven, which makes it easier for interaction modes to persist and therefore lowers abstraction. The paper also provides a theoretical explanation for prior empirical observations that tanh-like nonlinearities often produce more abstract representations than ReLU.

An especially useful distinction is between final abstraction and the maximum abstraction attained during training.
