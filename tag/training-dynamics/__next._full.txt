18:T42e,The authors study an online teacher-student model with a single softmax layer trained by cross-entropy. Inputs are sampled from an isotropic Gaussian and RMS-normalized, mirroring hidden states before an LM head. A fixed teacher matrix produces hard one-hot labels from the argmax of its softmax output. The student learns with different optimizers. The setup preserves the key nonlinearity behind peaked distribution learning: reducing loss requires both growing the student weight norm, which sharpens the output distribution, and aligning the student direction with the teacher. The analysis decomposes the loss into a radial channel, controlled by norm growth and softmax saturation, and a tangential channel, controlled by directional misalignment. These channels decay with exponents α_r and α_t. SGD places both near one third. Adam produces a different dynamic regime, with α_r near one half and α_t near zero. Across seven optimizers, the exponents obey the relation 2α_r + α_t = 1, which yields the same tuned data scaling L*(D) proportional to D^{-1/3}.19:T4a4,The empirical study uses public Pythia checkpoints at four model sizes, controlled LLaMA-style 70M runs, three initialization families, multiple data arms, optimizer-state records, and frozen-checkpoint interventions. The main observations are consistent measured core magnitude profiles after balancing, successful prediction of pooled-shape departure from field width on held-out runs and data arms, alignment of profiles across projections that share a channel space, and movement of q/k profiles with reassigned RoPE frequencies. Training trajectories show broad early formation followed by component-specific continuation or recession. AdamW second-moment factors align with functional channel spaces. Frozen-checkpoint edits separate an invariant reciprocal balance from a loss-sensitive relative gain: flattening the relative gain increases loss while matrix norms and the balanced core are held fixed, and the effect exceeds Gaussian controls after displacement normalization. The fitting protocol also reports that refitting W/s reproduces the scale ratio within about 0.2% and the shape within about 0.004; over k = 1.17 to 1.25, the relevant Weibull ratio varies by roughly 4%.1a:T70a,The paper analyzes discrete heavy-ball momentum dynamics in a river-valley model of the loss landscape. The geometry is decomposed into a low-loss, low-curvature, slowly bending river manifold and sharp orthogonal valley directions. The optimization challenge is not merely to descend, but to move quickly along the river without letting oscillations in the sharp directions destabilize the trajectory.

The main result is that momentum enlarges the largest tolerable learning rate by a leading factor of roughly (1+β)/(1-β) relative to vanilla GD. This factor is classical for quadratic objectives, but the nontrivial part here is showing that it still governs the leading stability threshold in a non-convex setting where the river and valley subspaces rotate and interact over time. Because of that rotation, gradient and momentum components leak across subspaces, so one cannot reuse a fixed-eigenspace argument.

To handle this, the authors develop a new inductive analysis that tracks both gradient and momentum projections onto river and valley directions, while controlling their mutual interference across iterations. A second key conclusion is interpretive: for a flat and slowly spinning river, momentum itself does not substantially change the intrinsic speed along the river. The acceleration mainly comes from enabling a much larger stable learning rate. In that sense, momentum acts more as a stabilizer that permits aggressive step sizes than as a direct source of extra river-direction acceleration.

The paper also improves the largest tolerable learning-rate bound for vanilla GD in the same river-valley framework. That side result suggests the proof machinery is not narrowly tailored to momentum alone, but strengthens the analytical understanding of the landscape model itself.1b:T5fb,From the available full-text content, this is primarily a theory paper, with experiments serving to validate the mechanism rather than to establish a new large-scale training recipe. The empirical phenomenon tied to the theory is clear: under WSD-like schedules, a long high-LR phase can produce higher observed loss than cosine scheduling because of oscillations across sharp valley walls, while still making faster progress along the river. Once the learning rate is sharply decayed, those oscillations are suppressed and the hidden progress becomes visible as a lower final loss.

The paper also mentions synthetic river-valley loss constructions for visualization and connects its analysis to prior probing of LLM pretraining landscapes in Wen et al. (2025). The most important empirical checks are therefore: (1) momentum remains stable at larger learning rates on synthetic river-valley landscapes; (2) the predicted stability-threshold enlargement matches the observed stable region; and (3) the seemingly paradoxical pattern of higher intermediate loss but better final optimization can be explained by separating river progress from valley oscillation.

The provided text does not include full tables, model sizes, dataset sizes, or exact improvement numbers, so those should not be invented. When reading the original, the most useful experimental artifacts to extract are the stability-boundary plots, the comparisons against vanilla GD, and any quantitative alignment between the theorem and observed training dynamics.1c:T58c,Read it in the order of phenomenon, model, theorem, then proof technique.

Start with the introduction and lock onto the practical question: why can a long high-LR phase plus sharp decay outperform smoother schedules, and what role does momentum play there? Then jump to Theorem 4.1. That is the central statement of the paper: the maximum stable learning rate is enlarged by about (1+β)/(1-β).

After that, spend time on Lemma 5.1 and the surrounding proof structure. This is where the real novelty sits. The important part is not only the final factor, but how the authors control dynamics when the river and valley subspaces rotate and when gradient and momentum components interfere with each other. While reading, focus on three things: how the river/valley projections are defined and updated, what assumptions encode “flat” and “slow-spinning,” and how the error terms are prevented from accumulating into instability.

If time is limited, read the theorem statements, proof sketches, and the main figures first, then return to the technical induction details. Also check the remark on the improved vanilla-GD learning-rate bound; it helps assess whether the analytical framework has broader value beyond the momentum case.

A careful read will likely take 1.5 to 2.5 hours. For readers focused on training stability and optimizer dynamics, this is worth reading with equations and figures side by side.1d:T485,The technical contribution is a clean training framework rather than a new architecture. The paper keeps architecture, model size, tokenizer, and overall language mixture fixed, while varying two main factors: the L1 identity and the order in which the two languages are presented during pretraining. English is always the L2, and the suite spans 21 L1s. The authors train under five exposure regimes, covering more interleaved versus more staged curricula, at three FineWeb scales: 100M, 2B, and 24B tokens, plus a human-scale 100M condition. A strong design choice is the inclusion of matched monolingual baselines for 20 languages, which helps separate genuine bilingual transfer from simple data-composition effects. Conceptually, the framework unifies structured exposure and sequential bilingual training ideas from earlier work, but treats exposure timing itself as the primary intervention. Evaluation is also broader than perplexity: the main target is psycholinguistic alignment, especially L2 eye-tracking reading times on MECO, with additional probes on cross-lingual structural priming and CEFR-stratified learner-error discrimination on BLiSS.1e:T4d9,The most consistent result is that ordered or staged exposure tends to align better with human L2 reading times than fully interleaved training. The paper argues this effect is not explained away by higher overall proficiency or better language balance, which is important because those are the obvious confounds. In other words, the point in training when a language is introduced appears to be a meaningful determinant of human-like L2 processing behavior. A second important finding is that the curriculum effect does not transfer uniformly across tasks. On structural priming and BLiSS learner-error discrimination, balanced exposure is often competitive or even better. So staged curricula seem to shape specific processing dynamics rather than universally improving bilingual competence.
