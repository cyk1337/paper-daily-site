9:T4c8,The method starts from the geometry of the prediction-loss and predictive-entropy plane. A likelihood-only detector uses a horizontal threshold on loss. This fails when many non-members have low loss because they are predictable. The proposed detector instead uses an inclined boundary by correcting loss with predictive entropy. The resulting score has the algebraic form of Helmholtz free energy: token loss plays the role of an energy term, predictive entropy is the uncertainty term, and a coefficient similar to temperature trades them off, giving a score of the form loss minus an entropy correction before sequence-level aggregation. The theoretical analysis shows that when members and non-members have the same mean predictive entropy, the correction preserves the expected membership gap and its lower bound. When the loss gap and entropy gap are positively correlated, an appropriate correction reduces the variance of the gap while preserving its mean, improving standardized separation. The analysis is also extended to settings with a nonzero mean entropy gap. Compared with reference-model or perturbation-based calibration, ETD uses the target model’s own token-level predictive distribution more directly.a:T400,The technical idea is to replace inferred membership labels with exact-match copy counts. The paper queries infini-gram indexes over OLMo-mix-1124 and the Pile to obtain the number of times a sentence appears in each corpus. These counts are conservative floors: punctuation changes, quote normalization, line breaks, and unindexed annealing data are not counted. The central design compares the same sentence across two model families. If a sentence appears more often in the OLMo corpus than in the Pile, the test asks whether an OLMo-2 model assigns it lower loss than a Pythia model, and vice versa. Because the sentence is identical, fluency, length, author quality, and wording are held fixed. The paper also evaluates standard loss-based detectors across copy-count regimes, tests one-word edited controls, and swaps in controls with different register. The purpose is not to build a stronger attack, but to isolate which parts of the apparent signal come from exposure and which parts come from evaluation artifacts.b:T52c,The experiments use five models from the OLMo-2 and Pythia families, ranging from 1B to 13B parameters. For ordinary sentences with 1 to 1,000 copies, loss-based membership evidence is nearly flat within noise. A 1B model reaches about 0.60 AUC on ordinary sentences, while famous lines with a median of roughly 1,200 copies reach 0.83 AUC. In the same-sentence design, using 747 sentences and within-book comparisons, the rank correlation between relative exposure and relative loss is about −0.08 for 7B models and −0.07 for 13B models, where −1 would be a perfect exposure-lowers-loss relationship and 0 means no relationship. This accounts for less than one percent of variance and does not strengthen with scale. One-word edits are shown to be poor non-member controls: in more than 90% of pairs, the original sentence has lower loss than the edit, by about 0.4 nats per token, or roughly 1.5× average token probability. The gap is similar whether the original appeared once or a hundred times, indicating that it measures wording quality rather than memory. Above about 1,000 copies, the gap grows with model size on the twelve available sentences. 1) Word-level probability estimation: represent the target text as a sequence of words. For each position, query the model multiple times conditioned on the preceding words, collect sampled continuations, and estimate P(next_word = w_t | prefix) by frequency. Local kernel smoothing is applied to reduce variance and avoid brittle zero counts under finite sampling.

2) Sequence-level likelihood estimator: aggregate the estimated word probabilities via a chain-rule style log-likelihood (typically mean log-likelihood) to obtain a membership score that mirrors classic likelihood-based MIAs.

3) Prefix-conditioned contrast: build two conditioning contexts (member-like vs non-member-like prefixes) and estimate the sequence likelihood under each, then contrast them to amplify the membership gap. A notable design choice is to contrast at the sequence level (after aggregation) instead of taking unstable word-level probability ratios.

4) Practical threat model: explicitly assumes the attacker does not know the target tokenizer, motivating word-level modeling; relies only on text sampling, matching typical proprietary LLM APIs.d:T55f,There are three main technical moves. First, the paper formalizes a subgroup-targeted membership inference game. Targets are sampled from a designated pool, while the rest of the private dataset is sampled from its complement. This makes it possible to audit high-risk groups, random controls, or unions of groups under a common reference distribution. Second, the authors adapt normalized-evidence membership inference to synthetic text releases. The exact likelihood-ratio test over an entire released synthetic dataset is intractable, so they replace it with measurable proxy evidence extracted from the release, such as n-gram overlap and embedding-based distances. These proxies are then normalized through scenario-dependent calibration. The separation is important: evidence comes from the release itself, while calibration captures attacker knowledge. Third, attacker capability is modeled explicitly through three release-based scenarios: release-only, raw-reference, and generator-assisted release. This lets the audit distinguish what can be inferred from the released samples alone, what auxiliary reference data adds, and how much stronger attacks become if the generator is also available. For each target token, a fixed scoring language model evaluates the token under a sequence of nested left-context windows rather than only under the full prefix. These evaluations form a structured array indexed by context length and relative token position, with additional features derived from the model’s predictive distribution. Arrays from documents of different lengths are aligned onto a common domain. LAR-1 learns additive contributions from individual cells to the class log odds and aggregates them over tokens and context lengths. LAR-2 adds within-path second-order features for the same target token, including same-context quadratic terms and products across different context lengths. Basis expansions and random squared projections produce finite-dimensional representations, which are estimated with ridge logistic regression. The theory analyzes the within-path quadratic model, decomposes finite-estimator error into approximation, regularization, estimation, and squared-projection components, and connects prediction error to AUC convergence.10:T5f3,The core premise is a hard utility constraint on rational adversaries: no matter what obfuscation tactics are used, a model that retains expert-level domain performance cannot fully erase intrinsic statistical fingerprints of proprietary data, which sit at the intersection of semantic substance (e.g., domain reasoning logic) and lexical form (e.g., terminological preference).
