Instead of probing arbitrary natural text, the authors exploit words that can appear either as a single canonical token or as an artificially split sequence of subwords. They define a canonicity measure by comparing the final-layer residual stream at the canonical token position with the final-layer residual stream at the last subword position in the split run. High similarity means the model has successfully reconstructed the canonical word representation from fragments.

To separate the roles of attention and MLPs, they build paired examples with shared subwords. In one setup, pairs share the last subword but differ in earlier fragments, isolating the contribution of cross-position information. In another, pairs share the first subword but differ in the final fragment, isolating the contribution of the local final-subword identity. They then perform activation patching: replace attn_out or mlp_out from a source run into a target run at a specific layer and position, and observe which word the target ultimately reconstructs. This is the key causal move. In Layer 1 at the final subword position, patching attention output is already sufficient to redirect the target toward the source word, showing that attention carries the decisive imported signal. Patching the MLP output then shows how that signal is transformed together with the local final-subword representation into a more canonical word-level state.

The resulting mechanism is fairly crisp. For words split into two to six subwords, composition consistently happens at the last subword position. Intermediate positions mainly act as relays when the word is longer. Layer 1 attention transports a weak but linearly readable token-specific signal from earlier fragments. Layer 1 MLP composes that imported signal with the local subword embedding through a continuous transformation. Subsequent early layers continue the process, but the decisive initialization happens immediately.

The authors extend the analysis to 12 transformer LMs and compare architectures and positional encodings. Their claim is not that every model detokenizes in exactly one layer, but that detokenization is concentrated in the first 6% to 30% of layers, with the span largely determined by positional encoding. The paper also discusses failure cases where the imported early-layer signal is too weak or ambiguous, making later recovery incomplete. That aligns with known tokenization-related failures on undertrained tokens and difficult segmentations.
