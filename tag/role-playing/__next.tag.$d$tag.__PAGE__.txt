9:T62b,The methodology is simple but well controlled. The authors evaluate four locally runnable open-weight instruct models at or below 8B scale: Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen3 8B, Gemma 4 e4b, and Apertus 8B. All models are served locally through Ollama with temperature set to zero, specifically to avoid confounding effects from remote API guardrails.

The evaluation has two parts. First, they sample 200 prompts from each of five benign OR-Bench categories that are especially relevant to legal discourse: violence, sexual, harmful, illegal, and unethical. This isolates the “benign but safety-adjacent” regime. Second, they build a real-world corpus from Swiss Federal Supreme Court criminal judgments, extracting fact-section paragraphs and obtaining 1,652 aligned paragraphs across English, French, and German. This tests whether the effect survives outside synthetic benchmark prompts.

The main intervention is prompt prefixing. They compare no prefix, a lawyer prefix, a supreme-court prefix, and a jailbreak-style prefix. The legal prefixes are meant to reflect realistic workflow prompts rather than adversarial attacks. The jailbreak prefix serves as a control to test whether the model interprets legal authority framing as a role-play override. Refusal detection is done with an LLM judge based on Qwen2.5-14B. That choice is not the paper’s strongest component, but the experimental design is the real contribution: same request, same model, same decoding, only the framing changes; and the effect is tested across models, languages, and both benchmark and real legal text.a:T562,The headline result is that legal authority prefixes increase refusal rates by roughly 2.2x to 20x relative to the no-prefix condition, and this pattern appears across all four model families. The introduction also notes that ordinary legal-role framing can trigger more refusal than an explicit jailbreak-style prefix, which is a striking sign that the models are not merely reacting to obvious adversarial wording.

Two additional findings matter. First, the effect is multilingual but uneven: English, French, and German show noticeably different magnitudes. That suggests refusal boundaries are not language-invariant and may depend on training-data composition, alignment coverage, and prompt-template transfer. Second, the effect persists on real criminal-judgment text when the task is information extraction rather than open-ended answering. That makes the result harder to dismiss as a benchmark artifact.

From the available full-text content, the paper includes the right controls for a study of this kind: local inference to avoid platform-level moderation, deterministic decoding, prefix robustness checks, and a jailbreak comparison condition. What seems less developed is fine-grained error analysis.
