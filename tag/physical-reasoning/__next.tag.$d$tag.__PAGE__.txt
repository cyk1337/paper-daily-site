5:T53a,The paper makes three concrete corrections. First, it improves prompt quality. The authors identify ambiguous prompts in the original benchmark and rewrite them to be more distinctive, while also introducing a templated prompting scheme that better matches model-specific prompting practices. The point is not to make the task easier in an artificial way, but to reduce variance caused by poor prompting so the score better reflects model capability.

Second, it changes metric aggregation. The original Physics-IQ score is defined at the dataset level, which means samples can contribute unevenly and failure modes are hard to localize. The paper introduces a sample-level Physics-IQ Verified score so that each sample and each metric can be analyzed more directly and aggregated more fairly. This improves interpretability and statistical hygiene.

Third, it cleans artifacts in the ground-truth videos. The authors show that many reference videos contain spurious activations unrelated to the target physical phenomenon, such as incidental motion or visual noise that can trigger IoU- or MSE-based metrics. They therefore clean the references so the metrics respond more selectively to the intended physical effect. This is a pragmatic benchmark-repair strategy: clean the measurement target before redesigning the entire metric stack.6:T50f,The most important result is not that one model scores higher than another, but that model rankings change materially once the benchmark is corrected. The authors evaluate six image-to-video models under both the original and the verified protocols and find that models respond differently to the benchmark fixes, leading to substantial ranking shifts. That means the noise in the original benchmark is not harmless random noise; it can systematically favor some models over others.

The scale of the benchmark issues is also nontrivial. The paper reports that the refinement removes measurement errors in 57.6% of all samples, affects 29.8% of videos, and corrects more than 34.8% of prompts judged to be highly ambiguous. The benchmark itself contains 66 physical experiments, each recorded from 3 viewpoints and repeated twice, for a total of 396 videos. Each 8-second video is split into a 3-second conditioning segment and a 5-second continuation to be predicted. The evaluation still uses the original four metrics—Spatial IoU, Spatiotemporal IoU, Weighted Spatial IoU, and MSE—but revisits how they should be aggregated and what reference signal they should operate on. The practical takeaway is that these are not cosmetic fixes; they are large enough to alter benchmark conclusions.7:T675,The method has two parts: benchmark construction and paired evaluation. First, What-If World builds a contrastive benchmark around physically meaningful interventions. Two domain experts define and merge intervention variables into six primitives spanning autonomous driving and robotic manipulation. Environment-side primitives include surface friction, material/medium, and obstacle configuration. Agent-side primitives include spatial alignment, force/degree, and temporal sequencing. Each test case is a paired triplet: the same initial frame x0 plus two prompts that differ in exactly one semantic variable. Construction proceeds in three steps: filter suitable clips from nuScenes or DROID; extract the frame right before the action starts and pair it with shared scene descriptors; then author a prompt pair that changes only the intervention variable. The goal is to approximate ceteris paribus through prompt design so that output differences can be attributed to the intervention. Second, the APEO evaluation framework decomposes success into four criteria: Adherence, whether the intervention was executed; Physics, whether the dynamics are valid; Environment, whether the non-intervened scene stays stable; and Outcome, whether the paired outputs diverge as the intervention predicts. The first three are scored in both single-video and paired modes, while Outcome is inherently paired-only. This decomposition is useful because it separates different failure modes: the intervention may be ignored, executed without sufficient divergence, or confounded by scene drift. The implementation uses a VLM judge validated against human annotation.8:T483,The benchmark is applied to nine state-of-the-art video generation world models, including four open-source and five closed-source systems. Three results stand out. First, no model exceeds 52% on the paired metric, and the four open-source models cluster around 28%, indicating that causal sensitivity is still weak. Second, there is a substantial gap between single-video scores and paired scores, which the authors call the contrastive bottleneck: models can generate two individually convincing videos while still failing to differentiate them in the way the input requires. Third, performance appears to track the visual salience of the intervention’s consequences more than robust physical understanding. Force/Degree, which often causes large visible motion changes, reaches 40.4%, while Surface Friction, which induces subtler trajectory differences, drops to 14.2%. The benchmark itself contains 319 paired triplets built from real embodied data sources, nuScenes and DROID.
