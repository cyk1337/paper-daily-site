18:T76d,PIVOT is a dual-level learning framework. The first part is trajectory-level anchoring. Instead of turning the problem into generic off-policy RL, the method builds a self-calibrated replay pool that stores historical responses judged to be both visually grounded and reasoning-stable. Visual grounding is estimated through a counterfactual measure: compare the model’s predictive distribution under the original image and a corrupted image, and use the KL divergence as a proxy for visual dependency. If the trajectory changes a lot when the image is corrupted, it likely relies on visual evidence rather than language priors. Stability is approximated by average trajectory entropy, motivated by prior observations that lower-entropy trajectories tend to be more reliable under RLVR. These replayed trajectories are used as retrospective anchors, not as hard imitation targets, so the method tries to preserve useful behavior without collapsing exploration.

The second part is token-level anchoring through vision-guided advantage allocation. Standard DAPO/GRPO-style methods broadcast one sequence-level advantage to all tokens. PIVOT instead estimates token importance using counterfactual visual support and each token’s effect on future reasoning. Tokens that appear more visually grounded or more consequential for downstream reasoning receive stronger optimization weight. The PPO-style clipped objective remains, but the advantage becomes token-modulated rather than uniform.

Relative to prior work, the differences are clear. It does not merely replay successful samples; it filters replay candidates using visual dependency and stability. It does not stop at sequence-level reranking; it pushes credit assignment down to tokens. And it does not require extra annotation or a new verifier, relying instead on counterfactual signals extracted from the model’s own multimodal behavior.19:T500,From the available full-text excerpt, the experimental setup spans multiple data scales and model scales, with consistent gains over standard multimodal RLVR baselines, likely including DAPO-style group-based policy optimization. The most important evidence is not just end-task accuracy, but whether the two proposed mechanisms are independently validated.

Three experimental questions matter most. First, whether the replay selection criteria are meaningful: trajectories with high visual dependency and low entropy should outperform naive replay or reward-only replay. Second, whether token-level advantage allocation improves over uniform sequence-level credit assignment, length-normalized variants, or simpler token masks. Third, whether the two modules are complementary: replay-only, token-allocation-only, and full PIVOT should show that trajectory reuse and token credit assignment address different bottlenecks.
