First, it reframes memory retrieval as an implicit non-parametric policy and the stored utility score as a proxy reward. Under this view, writing high-scoring episodes and reusing them later is a form of policy improvement in memory space. The Echo Gap is then defined as a systematic mismatch between self-assessed reward and true success, amplified by retrieval frequency.

Second, the paper provides a diagnosis through the Error-Independence Assumption (EIA). The key claim is that a stronger verifier is not sufficient if its errors are correlated with the original self-grading bias. The recoverable benefit of correction depends on two quantities: how well verifier scores align with true utility, and how decorrelated verifier errors are from the original bias. This is the most useful conceptual contribution: correction requires not just more capability, but a different error channel.

Third, the paper proposes mitigation through per-memory de-inflation rather than global calibration. A label-blind global remapping of scores cannot identify which specific memories are overvalued. Instead, the authors argue for memory-specific demotion using auxiliary signals that are less likely to share the same bias. They instantiate this idea with an answer-free de-inflation method and test it in a Memento-style retrieve–inject–write loop.8:T55b,The experiments are split into bank-level diagnosis and end-to-end behavioral impact. At the memory-bank level, the paper shows that incorrect episodes are systematically over-rewarded across the studied model families and factual memory banks, and that the inflation correlates with future reuse. This is not mere random judge noise. More capable re-graders are often better on average, but their residual errors can still align with the original self-grading bias, which limits their corrective value.

At the agent level, the paper evaluates a faithful memory loop on BIRD text-to-SQL. The agent retrieves similar past episodes, injects them into context, solves the current task, and writes the new episode back without access to ground-truth labels at write time. Final evaluation uses official execution accuracy. The reported result is that self-graded write-back progressively corrupts the memory bank, while the proposed de-inflation method consistently improves over the self-graded baseline across random seeds. This shows that the Echo Gap is not only a scoring artifact; it translates into measurable behavioral degradation.

While building a bilingual Turkish/English faithfulness-judgment corpus, the authors accidentally shared one decoding-budget parameter across both judging calls and generation calls. That budget was fine for judges that only needed a short output, but disastrous for generators that were supposed to produce paragraph-length hallucinated answers. As a result, one producer’s Turkish negative examples were silently truncated into very short fragments. Those corrupted items then entered the judge evaluation pipeline and produced what looked like a strong cross-lingual bias effect.

The main contribution is not merely finding a bug. It is explaining why standard checks failed to catch it. The authors applied four aggregate-level robustness procedures, and all of them reinforced the false story rather than exposing the fault, because they only inspected judge behavior and not the stimuli themselves. The paper then contrasts two corpus-construction regimes:
1. LLM-generated-negative design: negatives are sampled from a model, so there is no cheap oracle to tell whether a short or odd output is a valid hallucination or a degenerate artifact.
2. Mechanical-perturbation design: negatives are derived from gold answers by deterministic transformations, so the relation between gold and negative can be checked mechanically, for example with string-level comparisons.

To turn this from an argument into evidence, the paper includes a positive control. The authors inject the closest mechanical analogue of their fault into a minimal oracle-bearing corpus and show that a simple gold-to-negative string comparison catches it with 100% accuracy, at zero API cost and zero human reading. For settings where oracle-less generation cannot be avoided, they propose a practical validation protocol: before computing any aggregate statistic, manually inspect 15 to 20 raw generated items per generation condition and report their length distribution and degeneration rate. The protocol is deliberately simple because, in their case, simple manual inspection was the only thing that actually worked.c:T626,The empirical core is a real A/B-verified pipeline failure. On the corrupted corpus, one judge appeared to suffer a 32-point collapse in selection accuracy in Turkish relative to English. The effect replicated from N=50 to N=500. The authors could even build a three-layer mechanistic account for it, and a controlled producer-swap experiment also supported the apparent story. Yet the effect was fabricated by stimulus corruption.

The corrected-data analysis is especially useful. The paper shows that generation faults can corrupt conclusions in two qualitatively different ways: they can fabricate an effect that does not exist, or they can distort a real effect into the wrong magnitude or shape. Aggregate metrics cannot distinguish these cases. So even when a bias is not entirely fictional, upstream stimulus degeneration can still bend the measurement in a misleading direction.

The positive-control experiment then demonstrates the design asymmetry. When an analogous fault is injected into a mechanical-perturbation corpus, a simple string-based oracle detects it with 100% accuracy and no additional API calls or human effort. This does not prove that mechanical designs are always better overall, but it does show a clear auditability advantage.

The limitations are also clear.
