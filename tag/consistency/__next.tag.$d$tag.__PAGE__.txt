a:T4be,The method is called C3. For each task instance, the authors generate multiple context perturbations that preserve the task semantics while changing the surrounding framing. They then compare the model’s output distribution under the original prompt and under the perturbed prompts. The magnitude of that distributional shift becomes the instance-level C3 score: smaller shifts indicate higher credibility. A notable strength is that C3 is not tied to one output format. The paper adapts the comparison procedure across multiple-choice, short-answer, long-form factuality, and code generation settings, using task-appropriate estimators of output-distribution change. The motivation section uses a logical-semantics framing: if a model stably supports a proposition, semantically irrelevant contextual changes should not move its induced answer distribution much. This is not a mechanistic theory of LLM internals, but it is a coherent behavioral rationale. Relative to self-consistency, C3 measures stability across prompts rather than repeated samples from one prompt. Relative to paraphrase consistency, it tries to avoid changing the question itself, reducing false inconsistency caused by altered semantics.b:T518,The evaluation spans 26 models and 6 benchmarks, with 16 models in the main analysis and an additional 10-model case study. The task mix includes reasoning, factuality, and code generation. The main empirical findings are threefold. At the instance level, smaller cross-contextual shifts are associated with higher correctness or factuality. At the model level, aggregated C3 scores produce a robustness profile that distinguishes models that are merely benchmark-competent from those that remain behaviorally stable under semantically neutral perturbations. At the method level, the signal appears robust across perturbation sources and across different estimators of distributional difference. The paper also argues that C3 remains informative when standard benchmarks are less discriminative because of saturation or contamination, since it does not rely directly on answer-key scoring. For a closer read, the key numbers to inspect are the correlation between C3 and accuracy/factuality on each task, whether the effect holds for both open and closed models, which error types show the largest shifts, and how C3 compares with self-consistency, paraphrase consistency, and calibration-style baselines. The data comes from AIRBench-2024 and covers two broad risk families: operational misuse, including automated decision making and advice in regulated industries, and more explicit harm categories such as violence, extremism, self-harm, political misuse, and criminal activities. The authors start from 50 operational-misuse seed queries and 39 violence-related seed queries. For operational misuse, they use GPT-4o-Mini to generate five culturally adapted variants per seed by changing names, places, currencies, and demographic details without changing the underlying semantics, yielding 300 queries in total. For violence, model-generated variants were often refused, so they manually created one to two variants per seed, ending with 56 queries.

All responses are then generated by a single generator model, GPT-4o-Mini, to keep the judged content controlled. On top of that, the authors create semantically equivalent transformations of each query-response pair, including translation into five languages and paraphrasing. This isolates judge robustness from generator variability. Six widely used LLMs are then used as judges, and each output is evaluated along six safety-related criteria. The full excerpt does not list all six criteria, but the paper clearly moves beyond binary harm detection and includes dimensions such as helpfulness, warnings, and refusal behavior.

The analysis has two layers. Self-consistency checks whether one judge remains stable across original, translated, and paraphrased versions. Cross-consistency checks whether multiple judges converge on the same judgment. A key methodological point is the use of chance-corrected agreement rather than raw agreement alone. In safety evaluation, some criteria are naturally imbalanced, so naive agreement can look high even when judges are not meaningfully aligned.10:T458,The study evaluates roughly 350 safety prompts across two risk families and six languages, using six LLM judges. The main empirical pattern is clear. LLM judges are much less reliable on regulated advice and automated decision-making content, where safety depends on nuanced policy interpretation, than on explicit harm such as violence. The same judge often changes its verdict after translation or paraphrase, indicating low self-consistency and suggesting a realistic attack surface for judge jailbreaks through surface-form changes. Different judges also disagree substantially on the same content, with cross-consistency varying by criterion, domain, and language.

Another important result is methodological: raw agreement tends to overstate reliability, while chance-corrected metrics reveal much weaker consistency.
