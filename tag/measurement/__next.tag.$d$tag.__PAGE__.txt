9:T419,This is not a pretraining recipe paper, but it is useful for pretraining researchers because it improves the methodology for studying internal representations. A lot of current SAE analysis still relies on one-off feature stories: feature A went down, feature B went up, therefore the model changed its internal reasoning. Those stories are often fragile under prompt reformulation. RSS is an attempt to replace anecdotal feature tracking with a reproducible support selected across prompt variants and validated on held-out variants. That makes it more relevant for interpretability, alignment audits, and robustness analysis. For foundation-model work, the main value is methodological: if you want to study how tokenization choices, stylistic shifts, instruction templates, or synthetic data formats alter internal representations, you need a way to distinguish stable redistribution from incidental perturbation. The paper also makes an important conceptual separation: recurrent feature change is not the same thing as downstream causal effect.a:T975,The central object is the Redistribution Sensitivity Score, or RSS. It does not simply rank features by absolute change. Instead, it requires three properties at once: large response magnitude, recurrence across multiple transformed variants, and directional consistency. For a clean prompt and a set of transformed variants, the authors compute the activation change of each SAE feature at the aligned target position. They first form a candidate pool using average absolute change h_f so that opposite signs do not cancel. Then they measure how often each feature responds above a threshold tau and whether those responses mostly share the same sign, producing a recurrence score r_f and a consistency score c_f. Only features satisfying r_f >= rho and c_f >= kappa are eligible. The final support S is the top-K eligible features by response magnitude, and MeanRSS summarizes the average response strength of that support.

Two design choices matter. First, selection and evaluation are separated. The support and its dominant directions are chosen on selector variants and then frozen; evaluation is performed on disjoint held-out variants without recomputing the support. That is the right way to avoid post hoc feature storytelling. Second, RSS is defined without using readout weights. It first answers which representation changes are stable, and only afterward asks whether those changes matter for a downstream linear readout.

The readout analysis is also well structured. For a fixed linear probe, the paper gives an exact decomposition of each recurrent feature change into its contribution to the readout logit. That contribution depends on activation magnitude, probe weight, directional alignment, and the clean margin to the decision threshold. This separates three levels that are often conflated: representation redistribution, readout score change, and threshold crossing. A feature can change reliably and still have little behavioral effect if its readout weight is small or if multiple contributions cancel.

The paper also provides a finite-sample directional stability guarantee. With n=5 and rho=kappa=0.8, every selected feature must have at least four responses in the dominant direction and at most one in the opposite direction, so removing one observation cannot flip the selected sign. That makes the selected support a more reproducible object than a one-shot ranking of changed features.b:T6a8,The experiments cover 27 pretrained SAEs spanning eight language models. The empirical questions are whether prompt transformations induce redistribution profiles that reproduce on held-out variants, and whether those recurrent changes explain variation in frozen safety readouts. The main findings are threefold.

First, the supports selected by RSS produce reproducible redistribution profiles on held-out variants, which suggests that RSS is capturing stable structure rather than one-off feature fluctuations on the selector set. Second, on two frozen safety-readout audit tasks, the supports are selected without any readout information, yet they still reconstruct a substantial fraction of held-out logit change. That indicates a stable link between recurrent representation change and downstream readout variation. Third, when RSS supports are combined with the clean margin, they retain strong utility for predicting recurrent failure. The reported numbers are: median support sizes of 20 and 19 features for the two tasks, with recurrent-failure AUROCs of 0.935 and 0.958, compared with 0.965 and 0.964 using full erosion. The gap is modest, which means a very small recurrent support preserves most of the audit signal.

The most useful takeaway is not the AUROC itself but the decomposition. The experiments show that recurrent representation change does not map one-to-one to failure. Some recurrent changes alter the readout score without crossing the threshold; others only become predictive once the clean margin is taken into account. That moves SAE analysis from 'which features changed' to 'how changed features interact with a readout to produce or fail to produce a behavioral error.'c:T456,This is a skim paper, but the method deserves careful attention. After that, read the experiments on the two frozen safety readouts, especially the support sizes, held-out logit reconstruction, and failure AUROC when combined with the clean margin.

If you only have about 20 minutes, read the method section, Figure 1, and the main results tables on held-out reproducibility and AUROC. If you want to go deeper, inspect the supplementary details on probe scaling, operator-level averaging, and the full-erosion baseline. Those choices determine whether RSS is isolating genuinely stable mechanism-related change or just a byproduct of preprocessing. The right framing while reading is that the paper is not claiming SAE features are causal atoms; it is proposing a stricter reproducibility filter for SAE-based analysis.d:T5fa,The key methodological move is preregistration plus a full audit trail. The authors froze the protocol in advance, including acceptance thresholds: same-window repeat rankings had to reach Spearman 0.90, and byte-identical next-day replays had to reach 0.99 agreement. They then refused to repair the protocol after seeing the data. This is what gives the negative result force.

Several design choices matter. First, they built an append-only audit chain: request bodies, hashes, exclusions, valid task groups, replay pairs, and recorded metadata are all pinned and traceable. That rules out the usual explanation that the requests were not actually identical. Second, they decompose instability into distinct observable modes: within-window fluctuation, cross-day drift, and amplification caused by structured outputs such as rankings, where a tiny token-level change can invalidate or reorder an entire record. Third, they add prospective attribution experiments rather than stopping at a failed gate: same-day versus cross-day comparisons, four-provider comparisons, self-hosted controls, and constructed-error calibration. These help separate endpoint instability from task difficulty or judge incapacity. Fourth, the paper connects systems-level nondeterminism to measurement failure. Batch-dependent kernels, serving-time batching, and silent deployment changes were already known in systems work; the paper shows how these low-level effects become fatal once the output is used as a structured ranking or thresholded gate.e:T56e,The headline numbers are strong and easy to interpret. Across two campaigns, the audit covered 52,988 request attempts. The authors are careful not to treat this as the effective sample size; the actual analyses rest on 31 valid task groups, 100 replay pairs, and 10 constructed-error conditions. The first preregistered gate failed badly: same-window repeat rankings achieved only Spearman 0.400 against a required 0.90. The second gate also failed: byte-identical next-day replays agreed at 0.78 against a required 0.99.

Equally important is what did not fail. Requests were delivered successfully, schemas were valid, request-body hashes matched the frozen plan, and all recorded metadata fields remained constant. In other words, the engineering record looked clean while the measurement record was unstable. That is a major empirical point: standard API logs are not enough to establish reproducibility. The supplementary comparisons appear designed to answer whether the issue is specific to one provider or endemic to black-box shared serving, and whether self-hosting reduces the instability floor. The constructed-error calibration is also important because it asks a sharper question than average accuracy: even if the observer can detect obvious errors on average, is its variance low enough for threshold-based scientific use?
