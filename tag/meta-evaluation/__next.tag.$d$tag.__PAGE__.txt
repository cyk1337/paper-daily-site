9:T6e5,The setup is clean and practical. Starting from the 419 pairwise instruction-following items in LLMBar, the authors create three versions of each item: original English (EN), full Chinese translation (ZH), and a natural Chinese-English language-switched version (LS) that preserves technical terms and named entities when appropriate. The critical requirement is label-preserving translation: the transformation must not silently fix factual, mathematical, formatting, or instruction-following errors in the original responses. To support this, the paper uses gpt-4.1-mini for transformation and adds an automatic audit for empty fields, severe length shrinkage, and large numeric-token changes, followed by a sensitivity analysis.

The evaluation protocol goes beyond standard accuracy. For each item and language condition, the judge sees both original and swapped answer order, so position bias is not conflated with language bias. Outputs A/B/Tie are normalized back to the original response identities. On top of that, the paper introduces translation-equivalence tie probes: the gold answer in English is compared against its Chinese translation, where the intended judgment is Tie. If a judge systematically prefers one language in this setting, that reveals language preference rather than quality sensitivity.

The metric suite is broader than typical judge evaluations. In addition to strict accuracy and tie-half accuracy, the paper reports language-invariance flip rate, gold-correctness flip rate, position inconsistency, source-level robustness, language preference, uncertainty estimates, paired significance tests, and usage cost. The useful distinction here is between being wrong and being unstable under label-preserving language transformations.a:T54b,The study evaluates four API-accessible judges and collects 13,408 successful judgments over the full 419-item benchmark. That is not a massive scale, but it is sufficient for a focused meta-evaluation because each item is expanded systematically across language conditions, answer-order swaps, and tie probes.

The experimental questions are well chosen. First, does standard accuracy remain stable across EN, ZH, and LS conditions? Second, how often does the same source item trigger a preference flip under language switching? Third, in the gold-answer translation tie probes, does the judge incorrectly prefer English or Chinese instead of Tie? Fourth, do the conclusions remain after excluding the 19 high-risk translated items flagged by the audit?

For this kind of paper, the most important result is not a single accuracy number but the error structure. A judge can have acceptable average accuracy while still being unreliable under language switching. That matters in practice. If a judge performs well in English but flips frequently in Chinese or mixed-language settings, it is a poor choice for multilingual evaluation. If it systematically prefers English in translation-equivalent probes, then ranking and reward signals may inherit that bias. The paper’s main contribution is turning that concern into a measurable diagnostic framework.b:T4e0,This is a good skim paper; around 30 minutes is enough to extract the main value.
