a:T458,For pretraining research, the contribution is methodological but consequential. A large fraction of current analysis around tokenizers, multilingual transfer, shared semantic spaces, and internal abstraction relies on representation similarity metrics. If the metric itself carries a strong length bias, then conclusions about what pretraining has or has not learned can be systematically distorted. The paper is useful because it does not merely say cosine is imperfect in the abstract. It gives a concrete theoretical result: under anisotropy, mean-pooled cosine increases monotonically with sequence length. It then validates that prediction across synthetic data, code LLMs, multilingual settings, and CLIP-like representations. For foundation-model researchers, two lessons stand out. Length must be treated as an explicit confound in cross-representation analysis, especially when tokenization differs across languages or modalities. And representation geometry, especially anisotropy, is not just an interpretability curiosity; it directly shapes how we read empirical evidence about pretraining outcomes.b:T7a7,The paper has three layers of argument. First is the theory. Each token representation is modeled as a shared direction μ plus zero-mean noise ε_i, which is a standard approximation for anisotropic transformer representations. After mean pooling over n tokens, the shared component μ is preserved while the noise shrinks as 1/sqrt(n). Longer sequences therefore produce pooled vectors that lie closer to μ, and thus closer in cosine to any other pooled vector drawn from the same distribution. The authors derive an explicit high-dimensional approximation showing that the expected cosine between two mean-pooled sequences is a monotonic function of both lengths m and n. The key control variable is ρ = σ²d / ||μ||², the ratio of noise energy to shared-direction energy.

Second is the alternative metric. The paper argues for replacing mean-pooled cosine with linear CKA in these analyses. The reason is structural: CKA compares full token-level representation matrices rather than collapsing them through pooling, so the 1/sqrt(n) concentration effect does not arise. It is also invariant to invertible linear transformations of the column space, which makes it less sensitive to the shared-direction shift induced by anisotropy. The trade-off is that CKA requires aligned token positions or aligned subsets, so the authors use shared-surface-form alignment in practice.

Third is the empirical validation. The paper first uses synthetic high-dimensional Gaussian data to show that the artifact appears even without any model, language, or semantics, as long as there is anisotropy plus length variation. It then moves to real models and runs regression analyses to quantify how much of the observed cross-language proximity is explained by length ratio versus more content-related variables such as AST depth or shared-token fraction. This design is strong because it isolates the geometric artifact before attributing anything to model behavior.c:T6fd,The strongest empirical result comes from cross-language code representation analysis on HumanEvalPack. Across four code LLMs, length ratio alone explains R² = 0.52 to 0.75 of the variance in cross-language “Python proximity.” By contrast, AST depth and shared-token fraction add less than 3% explained variance beyond length. That is a sharp result: in these settings, the dominant signal behind apparent proximity to Python can be tokenization length rather than syntax or semantics.

When the metric is replaced with CKA, the explained variance attributable to length drops by 83%, and the length coefficient flips sign from β_len = +0.86 to -0.37. This is more than a mild correction. It suggests the original metric may not just exaggerate an effect; it may point in the wrong direction altogether. The paper also includes a positive control on Mistral-7B showing that genuine French-to-English representational routing can still be detected with a more appropriate metric. So the claim is not that cross-language convergence is fake, but that mean-pooled cosine cannot separate real convergence from a tokenizer-length confound.

The synthetic experiment is equally important. In 4096 dimensions, with vectors generated as h_i = μ + ε_i using ||μ|| = 10 and σ = 1, one sequence length fixed at 100 and the other varied by a random length ratio, mean-pooled cosine tracks the length ratio almost exactly, while CKA stays flat. This elevates the result from a model-specific observation to a general geometric phenomenon. The paper further notes that the effect size matches the anisotropy prediction: it is strongest in code LLMs, where sequences are short and anisotropy is high, and nearly absent in CLIP after the contrastive projection head, where anisotropy is reduced.d:T535,This is a skim paper, but it deserves a careful read of the theory and experiment design. Start with the introduction and mechanism section. The key idea to internalize is simple: under anisotropy, mean pooling converts sequence-length differences into cosine differences. You do not need to dwell on every algebraic step, but you should understand why the shared direction survives pooling, why the noise shrinks as 1/sqrt(n), and why ρ controls the artifact strength.

In the experiments, read them in order. First, the synthetic setup establishes that the effect exists even without model behavior. Second, the HumanEvalPack regressions show how dominant length ratio is relative to AST depth and shared-token fraction. Third, the CKA replacement and the Mistral-7B positive control show that the paper is not merely criticizing prior analyses; it also offers a workable alternative. If time is limited, 30 minutes is enough for the main theorem, Figure 1, and the core regression tables.
