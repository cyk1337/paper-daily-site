9:T650,The experimental design is simple and careful. The authors hold everything fixed and vary only the date string in the system prompt, sweeping every day from January 1 to December 31, 2024. They evaluate 9 recent models on 6 standard datasets spanning four task types: MCQA (MMLU, GPQA, ARC-Challenge), math reasoning (GSM8K), code generation (HumanEval), and machine translation (WMT English→German/Finnish/Czech). They explicitly verify that the MCQA questions are not time-dependent.

The setup is deterministic by construction, so the date effect can be isolated from known sources of instability such as hardware, batch size, and numerical precision. The task selection is also deliberate. MCQA tests a setting where the final decision often depends on a single answer token. GSM8K, HumanEval, and MT progressively increase the role of long autoregressive generation, execution-based evaluation, and full-output scoring. This lets the paper test whether a hidden prefix perturbation grows as generation length increases.

A useful mechanism proposed in the analysis is that longer generations give the date prefix repeated opportunities to bias intermediate token choices. Small perturbations then cascade through autoregressive decoding, producing larger score differences on reasoning, coding, and translation than on single-token multiple-choice decisions. The paper also checks common prompting strategies and finds that chain-of-thought and few-shot prompting do not remove the effect; chain-of-thought can even amplify it because it lengthens the trajectory over which the prefix perturbation propagates.a:T4d1,The evaluation covers Llama 3.1 Instruct 8B/70B, Gemma 3 Instruct 4B/27B, Qwen3 4B, Qwen3-Next 80B, Phi-4 14B, and GPT-OSS 20B/120B. Dates are swept across the full year of 2024. Metrics are accuracy and ECE for MCQA, accuracy for GSM8K, pass@1 for HumanEval, and BLEU/chrF for MT.

The effect sizes are large. On MCQA, changing only the date yields up to 6% accuracy variation and can reorder model rankings. On GSM8K, the average worst-to-best delta is reported as 7.75%, with some models reaching 14%. On HumanEval, the average pass@1 delta is 4.81%, with the most affected model reaching 7.32%. On MT, the average variation reaches 1.88 BLEU and 1.33 chrF, with a maximum BLEU swing of 2.84. These are not small numbers relative to typical benchmark improvements.

The paper also compares the date effect against other known instability sources and finds it larger than batch-size and precision effects. Model scale does not eliminate the problem. The correlation analysis further shows that there is no simple monotonic trend over the year and no stable set of universally good or bad dates, which suggests a complex interaction between hidden prompt metadata and internal model dynamics rather than a trivial seasonal artifact.b:T48b,The study attempted 10 open model variants across five families and a scale range from 8B to 675B parameters. The authors froze the protocol in advance, including acceptance thresholds: same-window repeat rankings had to reach Spearman 0.90, and byte-identical next-day replays had to reach 0.99 agreement. They then refused to repair the protocol after seeing the data. This is what gives the negative result force.

Several design choices matter. First, they built an append-only audit chain: request bodies, hashes, exclusions, valid task groups, replay pairs, and recorded metadata are all pinned and traceable. That rules out the usual explanation that the requests were not actually identical. Second, they decompose instability into distinct observable modes: within-window fluctuation, cross-day drift, and amplification caused by structured outputs such as rankings, where a tiny token-level change can invalidate or reorder an entire record. Third, they add prospective attribution experiments rather than stopping at a failed gate: same-day versus cross-day comparisons, four-provider comparisons, self-hosted controls, and constructed-error calibration. These help separate endpoint instability from task difficulty or judge incapacity. Fourth, the paper connects systems-level nondeterminism to measurement failure. Batch-dependent kernels, serving-time batching, and silent deployment changes were already known in systems work; the paper shows how these low-level effects become fatal once the output is used as a structured ranking or thresholded gate.f:T56e,The headline numbers are strong and easy to interpret. Across two campaigns, the audit covered 52,988 request attempts. The authors are careful not to treat this as the effective sample size; the actual analyses rest on 31 valid task groups, 100 replay pairs, and 10 constructed-error conditions. The first preregistered gate failed badly: same-window repeat rankings achieved only Spearman 0.400 against a required 0.90. The second gate also failed: byte-identical next-day replays agreed at 0.78 against a required 0.99.

Equally important is what did not fail. Requests were delivered successfully, schemas were valid, request-body hashes matched the frozen plan, and all recorded metadata fields remained constant. In other words, the engineering record looked clean while the measurement record was unstable. That is a major empirical point: standard API logs are not enough to establish reproducibility. The supplementary comparisons appear designed to answer whether the issue is specific to one provider or endemic to black-box shared serving, and whether self-hosting reduces the instability floor. The constructed-error calibration is also important because it asks a sharper question than average accuracy: even if the observer can detect obvious errors on average, is its variance low enough for threshold-based scientific use?
