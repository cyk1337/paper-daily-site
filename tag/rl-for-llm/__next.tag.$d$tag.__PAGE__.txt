9:T481,The framework treats per-problem failure probability of the policy as a random variable over sampled problem instances. By the Hausdorff moment theorem, a distribution supported on [0,1] is uniquely determined by its full sequence of moments, so single-moment objectives capture only partial information about policy behavior. Multi-Moment Policy Optimization (MMPO) jointly minimizes multiple moments of the failure distribution, an objective that maps operationally to minimizing the expected truncated number of rollouts needed to obtain a first correct response, naturally balancing average performance and attention to high-failure hard problems. A generalized moment transformation framework further unifies a broader family of policy optimization objectives—including MaxRL, which is interpreted as optimizing moments of a transformed rather than raw failure distribution—and proves that this family of objectives is strictly Schur-convex, meaning optimization explicitly pushes for more balanced success rates across problems. The practical training surrogate is compatible with existing on-policy RLVR pipelines without major modifications.a:T4d7,SocraticPO turns a single-turn RL rollout into an interactive multi-round trajectory. The student answers first; a verifier checks correctness; if wrong, a teacher reads the student attempt (and optionally side information such as reference solutions) and produces concise Socratic-style guidance (diagnosis + hint, not a full solution). The guidance is appended to the context and the student continues; this can repeat for multiple rounds. Training remains standard expected-reward policy gradient (compatible with Reinforce++/GRPO-style implementations); the teacher is not distilled and no teacher logits/distribution matching are required, enabling black-box teachers.
The key anti-exploitation mechanism is reward decay: if the student only becomes correct after r rounds of teacher intervention, the reward is reduced as a monotone, saturating function of r. This makes “solve correctly with less help, earlier” the optimized behavior and discourages policies that intentionally wait for hints. The paper also analyzes properties of the resulting normalized advantages (non-negativity, monotonicity, saturation) to argue the shaping does not destabilize learning by flipping correct trajectories into negative-advantage samples.b:T79e,The first component is SVEB, a benchmark for state-value estimation. For a given partial sequence, the authors approximate the ground-truth value by large-scale Monte Carlo continuation: sample many suffix rollouts from the same prefix and average the final rewards. This gives a reference estimate for V^pi(s), against which different estimators can be compared directly. Using SVEB, they show that PPO critics in LLM RL tend to regress toward the group-average baseline rather than learning informative token-level values.

The second component is Numca, a domain-specific heuristic for mathematical reasoning. The paper reframes math problem solving as sparse-reward goal reaching and observes that numerical spans in intermediate reasoning often act as natural milestones. Numca groups disjoint rollouts using these numeric anchors and uses them to estimate future return from intermediate states. Conceptually, it borrows the spirit of hindsight-style relabeling, but adapted to text reasoning through easily parsable numbers. It is simple and cheap, but clearly tied to domains where numbers are meaningful intermediate structure.

The third component is Hista, the general solution. Hista represents each generation step with the model's hidden state and estimates the value of a state by probability-weighted averaging of returns from semantically similar states found in disjoint rollouts. It is a non-parametric estimator built on the intuition that similar hidden states should imply similar future rewards. Importantly, it requires neither extra rollouts nor additional critic training. The paper also provides a theoretical result showing that, under its assumptions, Hista yields lower estimation error than the group-average baseline. Compared with PPO critics, Hista avoids the difficulty of fitting token-level values from sparse terminal rewards. The theory starts from a simplified policy-gradient objective without clipping or importance weighting and studies how predictive entropy changes after an update. The key result is that entropy change is negatively related to advantage, and the effect is amplified when the model is already highly confident. Intuitively, positive-advantage samples reinforce selected tokens, sharpen the distribution, and reduce entropy; negative-advantage samples do the opposite and tend to flatten the distribution. In high-confidence regions, this sharpening or flattening effect becomes stronger, which explains why entropy collapse can become self-reinforcing once the policy has already narrowed.

Entrocraft uses this result operationally. Instead of adding entropy regularization to the loss, it performs rejection sampling on rollout samples before the policy update. If the observed entropy is below the target schedule, it preferentially keeps samples whose advantages push entropy upward and filters out samples that would reduce entropy further. If entropy is above target, it does the reverse. In effect, it biases the advantage distribution so that the resulting gradient steers the policy toward the desired entropy level. Because the intervention happens directly at the sample-distribution level, the controller reacts quickly, often within a few update steps, rather than relying on a weakly coupled regularization term. The paper emphasizes two practical properties: it is drop-in for existing RL pipelines, and it is advantage-estimator-agnostic.

A notable aspect is that the paper is about crafting an entropy curve, not merely preserving entropy. In a transformer, attention is computed from pre-softmax scores H. AtMan adds a mask to H before the softmax. Compared with deleting tokens, perturbing embeddings, or masking inputs directly, this is a relatively controlled intervention: the token sequence stays unchanged, and the manipulation acts through attention allocation rather than through a large input-distribution shift.

The mask is applied only to CoT tokens, not to prompt tokens or final answer tokens. This design follows the training objective: the prompt is fixed and not under model control, while the final answer should depend on the generated reasoning trace. The mask is initialized with a negative constant c = -0.4 over CoT-related entries, which uniformly suppresses attention to reasoning tokens. Then, with model parameters frozen, the mask is optimized to restore the likelihood of the correct answer under teacher forcing. Intuitively, if some reasoning tokens are important for producing the answer, the optimization must relax suppression on those positions. The learned mask values are then used as a proxy for token saliency.

After normalization, the mask values over the CoT region are aggregated into a saliency reward. This reward is combined with standard outcome-based reward inside GRPO. The novelty is therefore not in the RL algorithm itself, but in reward construction: the model is encouraged not simply to be correct, and not simply to produce longer reasoning, but to produce reasoning whose tokens measurably support the answer under attention-based intervention.

Relative to process supervision, the method avoids manual step annotations. Relative to pure outcome RL, it distinguishes between correct trajectories that are more or less dependent on their own reasoning text. The main limitation is also clear: attention saliency is only a proxy. It does not fully capture all causal pathways in the residual stream, MLP computations, or other internal mechanisms, so it should not be equated with full faithfulness.14:T564,Experiments are run on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, with GSM8K and MMLU as the main evaluation tasks.
