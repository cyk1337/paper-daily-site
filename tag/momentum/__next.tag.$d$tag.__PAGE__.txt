9:T70a,The paper analyzes discrete heavy-ball momentum dynamics in a river-valley model of the loss landscape. The geometry is decomposed into a low-loss, low-curvature, slowly bending river manifold and sharp orthogonal valley directions. The optimization challenge is not merely to descend, but to move quickly along the river without letting oscillations in the sharp directions destabilize the trajectory.

The main result is that momentum enlarges the largest tolerable learning rate by a leading factor of roughly (1+β)/(1-β) relative to vanilla GD. This factor is classical for quadratic objectives, but the nontrivial part here is showing that it still governs the leading stability threshold in a non-convex setting where the river and valley subspaces rotate and interact over time. Because of that rotation, gradient and momentum components leak across subspaces, so one cannot reuse a fixed-eigenspace argument.

To handle this, the authors develop a new inductive analysis that tracks both gradient and momentum projections onto river and valley directions, while controlling their mutual interference across iterations. A second key conclusion is interpretive: for a flat and slowly spinning river, momentum itself does not substantially change the intrinsic speed along the river. The acceleration mainly comes from enabling a much larger stable learning rate. In that sense, momentum acts more as a stabilizer that permits aggressive step sizes than as a direct source of extra river-direction acceleration.

The paper also improves the largest tolerable learning-rate bound for vanilla GD in the same river-valley framework. That side result suggests the proof machinery is not narrowly tailored to momentum alone, but strengthens the analytical understanding of the landscape model itself.a:T5fb,From the available full-text content, this is primarily a theory paper, with experiments serving to validate the mechanism rather than to establish a new large-scale training recipe. Gradients are modeled as a coherent low-rank signal plus a bounded-variance, mean-zero perturbation that is orthogonal to the signal. This is a deliberate abstraction: it captures the idea that some directions persist across steps and should accumulate, while other components behave like high-frequency noise.

The paper studies three pipelines. Pre-polar is standard Muon: first form the momentum buffer M_t = beta M_{t-1} + (1-beta) G_t, then apply the polar factor O(M_t). Post-polar is the Orthogonal-SGDM style variant: first orthogonalize each step O(G_t), then apply momentum. Polar-only removes momentum entirely. The first main result is that momentum acts as a spectral low-pass filter on the raw gradient stream. Because the coherent signal persists over time, it survives temporal averaging; the zero-mean perturbation is attenuated. This enlarges the gap between the r-th and (r+1)-th singular values and improves alignment of the top-r singular subspaces of M_t with those of the underlying signal. The second main result is about ordering. The polar factor preserves singular directions but replaces singular values by ones. If it is applied directly to a noisy gradient, it erases the magnitude structure that distinguishes signal from perturbation. Momentum applied afterward can only smooth already distorted directions; it cannot recreate the lost spectral separation. Hence pre-polar dominates post-polar for structural reasons, not as an implementation accident.

A useful aspect of the analysis is that it does not stop at the vague statement “momentum reduces noise.” It specifies what kind of noise reduction matters for Muon: creating a matrix whose spectrum is sufficiently separated so that the subsequent orthogonalization acts on stable leading subspaces.
