17:T7f9,The central abstraction is the blueprint, an evolving Lean file that simultaneously serves as a formal proof skeleton, a natural-language proof graph, and the shared system of record for all agents. Each lemma or theorem node contains a Lean type, the corresponding LaTeX statement, and natural-language proof content. Dependency links between nodes form a proof DAG. A key design choice is that this DAG is not frozen at initialization. It can be split, repaired, and reorganized during the run, which matches the uncertainty of research-level formalization much better than a static decomposition.

On top of this shared artifact, the system uses four contract-scoped agents responsible for construction, auditing, proving, and repair. The contract-scoped part matters. Agents are restricted to bounded edit regions rather than given unrestricted write access to the whole development. This is a deliberate error-containment mechanism: the worst outcome of a bad action should be a rejected patch, not hidden corruption of distant proof state.

Coordination is handled by a two-stage orchestrator. The first stage stabilizes target fidelity through adversarial review, trying to lock down whether the current formal statements still correspond to the intended mathematical targets. The second stage discharges the proof DAG from dynamic leaves upward in parallel, with each round gated by CI-style verification. This separation is important. The system does not ask one monolithic agent to simultaneously infer the structure, prove subgoals, debug failures, and decide whether it is still proving the right theorem.

The paper’s design principles are also worth noting: dynamic DAG decomposition, external or deterministic verification, restricted tool scope, and continuous projection between informal reasoning and the blueprint. Compared with monolithic ReAct-style agents, this is much closer to a controlled collaborative software workflow. Unlike standard multi-agent benchmarks, this setup does not place a fixed set of agents into a closed environment with a prescribed protocol. Instead, the authors deploy controlled probe agents into MoltBook, a live open-ended platform populated by more than two million autonomous agents, and observe how the society responds under natural conditions.

The evaluation has three tiers. Tier I tests joint reasoning: can multi-agent discussion converge to answers that beat strong individual models on difficult tasks? Tier II tests information synthesis: can agents read and combine information distributed across multiple contributors? Tier III tests basic interaction: do agents reply, attend to prior messages, and maintain conversational context at all? The hierarchy is deliberate. If basic interaction is weak, robust synthesis is unlikely; if synthesis is absent, joint reasoning cannot emerge.

The technical novelty is in turning an open social platform into a controlled diagnostic instrument. Passive observation of naturally occurring posts cannot establish whether collective intelligence exists, because task difficulty, ground truth, and participation conditions are uncontrolled. Probing Agents solve this by posting stimuli with known answers and measuring response quality, participation rate, and collaborative depth. That makes the framework suitable for testing spontaneous coordination rather than protocol-enforced teamwork.1b:T6bf,Experiments are conducted on MoltBook, a live platform with over two million autonomous agents. The authors deploy probing agents across tasks ranging from difficult reasoning problems, including Humanity’s Last Exam, to simple coordination tasks such as counting. The overall pattern is consistent: the society does not outperform strong individual frontier models, and often performs substantially worse.

At Tier I, on hard reasoning tasks, group discussion fails to produce answers better than isolated strong models. Many comments are superficial, irrelevant, or non-contributory, which suggests the issue is not a small gap in aggregation quality but a more basic failure to enter meaningful collaboration.

At Tier II, the results are more nuanced. When agents actually engage, they are often capable of synthesizing distributed information correctly. This implies that synthesis ability exists at least in some agents. However, most posts receive no replies at all, so the opportunity for synthesis rarely materializes. The dominant bottleneck is therefore participation, not pure cognitive inability.

At Tier III, even trivial tasks that require almost no reasoning still suffer from sparse replies and poor context following. Many posts get no response, and many responses do not track the conversational state. This supports the paper’s broader interpretation: the platform behaves more like a bulletin board of independent broadcasts than a society engaged in sustained communication.
