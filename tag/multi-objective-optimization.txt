18:T718,STAGE is not a new reward model and not a new PPO or DPO loss. It is an active-set controller wrapped around PPO. The method has four main components. First, cumulative retention: at stage k, only the active set P_k contributes to the scalar reward, but once an objective is admitted it remains active in all later stages. This differs from strict sequential curricula that drop old objectives when moving to a new one. Second, probing-based ordering: before the main run, the method performs a short probing phase with all preference dimensions active. It measures early reward gain and volatility for each dimension, then defines a difficulty score that favors low gain and high variance, producing a hard-to-easy admission order. In the paper’s example, creativity, reasoning quality, and numerical sensitivity fall into the harder cluster, while helpfulness, clarification, QA behavior, and ethical compliance are easier. Third, stability-guided admission: within each stage, the controller tracks the batch-mean normalized reward for every active objective and its running maximum within that stage. The gap between current reward and stage-best reward defines a deviation signal, which is then aggregated over a window. A new objective is admitted only when recent deviations are small enough, or when a patience budget is exhausted. The goal is to avoid introducing a new conflicting objective before the current active set has stabilized. Fourth, adaptive weighting: within the active set, weaker objectives receive more weight so that easy or already-strong dimensions do not dominate the PPO reward. The key distinction from reward soups, Pareto-style methods, or gradient surgery is that STAGE mainly controls temporal objective admission rather than only changing scalarization or gradient combination.19:T567,The experiments use two backbones, Qwen3-0.6B and Llama3-8B-Instruct, with 15 preference dimensions during training and 16 held-out automatic benchmarks for evaluation. The provided text does not include the full numeric tables, but the paper reports that STAGE improves the automatic-evaluation average over simultaneous training and shared-budget adapted baselines. The ablations are more informative than the headline: removing cumulative retention, removing the reward-deviation gate, or replacing the probing-derived order all hurts performance, suggesting that the method’s gains do not come from a single cosmetic component. The paper also analyzes active-set growth dynamics and the efficiency-stability trade-off controlled by patience. Short patience admits new objectives faster but increases interference before earlier objectives settle; long patience is more stable but less efficient. That result is expected, but it supports the controller design. The main design has three components. First, dual-track cleaning combines domain-aware heuristic rules with model-driven document understanding. Instead of applying uniform thresholds, the rules are calibrated per domain so that math, code, and other structurally unusual but useful content are not removed simply because they violate generic web-text statistics. A data understanding model annotates documents with multi-aspect writing quality, content value, and domain tags. Second, hybrid deduplication combines n-gram-style filtering with semantic deduplication. The paper highlights two hard cases: templatized clusters, where pages share a structural skeleton but differ in local parameters, and redundant crawling cases that fall just below an LSH threshold. CuraWeb uses a soft semantic deduplication strategy based on weighted voting over multiple similarity edges. A document is removed only when its accumulated penalty exceeds a threshold, which reduces over-filtering compared with hard similarity cutoffs. Third, multi-objective sampling combines writing quality, content value, and diversity. Writing quality is aggregated with a harmonic mean to penalize severe defects on any single dimension. Content value is summed linearly to reward expertise and information density. A diversity score is added to preserve domain coverage, and Power Sampling increases the probability of selecting high-value documents without collapsing the sample into a narrow high-score slice.1c:T798,The proposed method, Pareto LoRA, treats text loss and image loss as two coupled objectives and seeks a shared descent direction instead of combining them with a fixed scalar weight. Let g_txt and g_img be the gradients of the two modality-specific losses with respect to the trainable LoRA parameters. The method searches for the minimum-norm vector in the convex hull of these gradients. By the MGDA result, this vector either corresponds to a Pareto-stationary point or provides a common descent direction that improves both objectives. Because there are only two objectives, the optimal mixing coefficient has a closed-form solution, which keeps the additional optimization overhead modest.

An important detail is that the method is not applied blindly at every step. The paper first diagnoses the relationship between modality-specific gradients using two signals: cosine similarity for directional conflict and gradient magnitude ratio for dominance. This is a sensible design choice. If the gradients are already aligned and similarly scaled, intervention is unnecessary. The problematic cases are those where one modality overwhelms the update or where the two objectives pull in conflicting directions. This is precisely where fixed loss-level weighting is too crude, because it cannot adapt to layer-wise, stage-wise, or batch-wise changes in gradient geometry.

The LoRA-specific angle also matters. Since LoRA updates only low-rank adapters, adaptation capacity is limited. If the shared update direction is persistently driven by language gradients, the vision side is more likely to be underfit. Pareto LoRA can be interpreted as using that limited adaptation budget more carefully by steering each update toward a more balanced direction.
