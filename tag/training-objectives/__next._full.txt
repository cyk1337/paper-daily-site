19:T89c,The paper has two main components.

First, it gives a constructive impossibility result in linear-Gaussian dynamics. The state is decomposed into a system variable s and an environment variable e. A one-dimensional linear encoder y_t = w^T x_t is paired with the optimal linear predictor for y_{t+1} from y_t. The authors derive the stationary covariance through the discrete Lyapunov equation, obtain closed forms for upper-triangular dynamics, and compare the predictive risk of three encoder families: the NZ projector that keeps only the system coordinate, a pure-environment encoder, and general mixed encoders. The key result is stronger than a simple counterexample: the causal encoder can be strictly suboptimal, the environment-only encoder can outperform it, and the global optimum can lie in the interior, meaning the best predictive representation is a mixture tilted toward the more predictable direction.

The supplementary proof gives an explicit parameter setting: a_s=0.05, a_e=0.98, c=-0.90, q_s=0.05, q_e=0.10. Under this setup, the NZ encoder has predictive risk about 0.174, the environment encoder 0.100, and the globally optimal mixed encoder about 0.074 at an angle near 43.7 degrees. The interior optimum is also shown to be stable under small perturbations, so the phenomenon is not a knife-edge artifact.

Second, the paper scales the argument beyond the linear proof by running a large neural sweep over 2,695 network configurations trained on predictive tasks over linear-Gaussian dynamics. The authors define causal fidelity as the fraction of encoder sensitivity allocated to system degrees of freedom. This lets them quantify how much of the learned representation is actually devoted to the intended causal variables. The empirical result is that predictive training usually does not recover them.

Mechanistically, the paper is clear: predictive objectives reward temporal persistence and low-noise structure, not causal relevance. Slow, stable, low-entropy modes dominate one-step prediction, so the encoder spends capacity on them. If the true system variables are faster, noisier, or partially driven by the environment, pure prediction has no reason to isolate them.1a:T57b,The empirical section is centered on large-scale neural evidence rather than a broad benchmark suite.

1. Explicit analytic counterexample: in the 2D linear-Gaussian construction, the causal NZ encoder is strictly worse than both a pure-environment encoder and an interior mixed encoder. For the reported parameters, R(w_NZ) is about 0.174, R(w_env) is 0.100, and the global optimum reaches about 0.074 at roughly 43.7 degrees.

2. Neural sweep: the authors train 2,695 neural-network configurations on predictive representation learning tasks over linear-Gaussian dynamics. The mean causal fidelity is only 0.49, and only 2.5% of configurations exceed 0.70. So even when prediction works, the learned representation usually does not concentrate on the system variables.

3. Dimensional scaling: the failure worsens sharply with dimension. At N=100, the optimal encoder becomes nearly causally blind, with fidelity around 1e-8, while still achieving 92% lower prediction error than the causal representation. This is the most important quantitative result in the paper, because it shows that the wrong representation is not merely competitive; it is actively preferred by the predictive objective.

4. Robustness: the supplementary analysis argues that the interior optimum persists in an open neighborhood of parameters, so the gap is structurally stable rather than a single pathological construction.1b:T49a,Start with the problem setup and the definition of causal fidelity. Without that, the theorem can look like a narrow linear-algebra statement when it is really about objective mismatch.

Then read the main theorem and jump quickly to the supplementary proof in Section S1. The most useful parts are:
1. the stationary covariance derivation via the discrete Lyapunov equation;
2. the comparison between the NZ encoder, the environment encoder, and the interior optimum;
3. the higher-dimensional embedding argument showing the result is not confined to 2D.

If time is limited, focus on three items: the impossibility theorem, the 2,695-model neural sweep, and the dimensional-scaling result where fidelity collapses while predictive error improves. That is enough to understand the paper’s main contribution in about 30–45 minutes. A full pass through the proof will take longer.

One caution is important for LLM readers: the analysis is built around latent self-prediction in linear-Gaussian systems, not autoregressive next-token language modeling.
