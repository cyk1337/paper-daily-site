9:T713,The methodology is built around converging evidence from multiple interpretability pipelines rather than a single localization tool. First, gradient-based attribution is used to identify which mid-to-upper-layer components are most sensitive to temporal-preference judgments. Second, activation patching and attribution patching test causal necessity by swapping activations between prompts with different time-horizon conditions and measuring whether the model’s choice changes. Third, linear probes are trained on internal states to test whether horizon information is decodable and how it is geometrically organized in the residual stream. Fourth, concept-direction style analysis and intervention, including CAA-like steering, is used to verify whether moving activations along a learned direction can systematically shift the model toward shorter-term or longer-term preferences.

The paper’s strongest mechanistic claim is a two-stage picture. Time horizon appears to be represented as a non-linear manifold in the residual stream at localized mid/high layers. Then, around the user-to-assistant turn boundary tokens, attention collapses that continuous representation into a committed binary preference that drives the final answer. This is more interesting than a simple “there is a neuron for patience” story. It separates representation from commitment, and ties the commitment step to a concrete token boundary in the chat template.

Relative to prior work, the difference is clear. Earlier studies on LLM economic behavior mostly fit discounting curves at the output level. Mechanistic interpretability work, on the other hand, has focused more on binary contrastive concepts. This paper tries to handle a dimensional concept and connect localization, geometry, and steering in one pipeline.a:T669,The experiments are centered on consistency of mechanistic evidence rather than benchmark-style aggregate scores. The model under study is Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, chosen because its non-thinking-only mode keeps computation within a fixed prompt template, which makes token-aligned attribution and patching more tractable.

At the behavioral level, the authors fit intertemporal-choice behavior and compare it with human discounting. The main result is that the unintervened model discounts the future much less steeply than humans, so it behaves as if it is more patient. But this preference is not stable across contexts, which weakens any interpretation that the model has a coherent, globally consistent utility function over time. That instability is one of the paper’s central motivations for explicit control.

At the mechanistic level, several localization pipelines converge on a similar mid-to-upper-layer subgraph, with L24 attention highlighted as relevant for horizon inference. Probe-based analyses suggest that horizon information is present but not organized as a simple linear axis. Around the turn-boundary tokens, the representation appears to collapse, after which the model commits to a binary preference. Steering experiments then show that targeted interventions on these localized representations can reliably bias the model toward shorter-term or longer-term choices, including in more open-ended generation settings. Then read Section 5.2 carefully. Any residual-stream projections, probe visualizations, or attention maps here are central. After that, read Section 5.3 and Appendix AB for steering. The main thing to check is whether the intervention uses the localized concept direction in a principled way and whether the effect transfers beyond the narrow prompt format used for localization. Also look for side-effect analysis: does steering temporal preference degrade general helpfulness, formatting, or unrelated reasoning?
