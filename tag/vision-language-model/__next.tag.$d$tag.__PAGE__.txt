9:T9bd,Libra 的核心设计是 decoupled vision-language system：一套 vision system，一套 language system，中间用 cross-modal bridges 连接。关键不只是双塔，而是在每层内部把 self-modal modeling 和 cross-modal interaction 分成两种计算模式。

第一是 switch attention。作者区分两种场景：模态内部建模时，用标准 attention；跨模态交互时，不直接让一种模态去对齐另一种模态的原始特征空间，而是先通过 cross-modal bridge 把 Q/K/V 映射到新的交互空间，再做注意力。这么做的动机很明确：如果直接共享同一注意力空间，视觉和语言会互相拉扯，尤其在数据不平衡时更容易让弱势模态被强势模态同化。bridge 相当于给跨模态交互加了一个受控接口，保留各自表示的自治性。

第二是 switch FFN。它对应不同任务场景下的后续变换分流，用来进一步区分理解与生成、以及自模态与跨模态信息融合后的处理方式。虽然从摘要和引言看，具体实现细节需要结合方法章节确认，但设计意图很清楚：attention 负责信息路由，FFN 负责场景化变换，二者共同完成“共享框架下的计算解耦”。

在实例化上，作者给了两个版本。Libra-1 面向 understanding-only image-to-text。这里有两个值得注意的点：一是 supervision 采用 unified discrete autoregression，把视觉和语言都放进离散 AR 框架里，优先换取训练稳定性；二是 vision input 采用 hybrid tokenization，把连续视觉信号和离散 ID 结合起来，试图兼顾细粒度感知与离散序列训练的稳定性。这个选择说明作者并不完全相信纯离散视觉 token 足够表达感知细节。

Libra-2 扩展到 unified understanding + generation。按引言描述，它在 tokenization、representation、supervision 上又加了额外策略，目标是同时支持 image-to-text 和 text-to-image。这里最关键的不是“能不能统一”，而是统一后是否还能维持两边能力不互相伤害。Libra 的回答是通过结构分流降低干扰，而不是完全依赖数据配比去硬平衡。

The key idea is not merely having two towers, but introducing two computation modes inside the model: self-modal modeling and cross-modal interaction.

The first component is switch attention. For self-modal modeling, Libra uses standard attention. For cross-modal interaction, it does not directly align one modality into the raw feature space of the other. Instead, queries, keys, and values are mapped through a cross-modal bridge into a dedicated interaction space before attention is computed. The motivation is clear: direct sharing of the same attention space can cause one modality, usually language, to dominate the other under imbalanced data. The bridge acts as a controlled interface that preserves modality-specific representations while still allowing information exchange.

The second component is switch FFN. It further separates computation after attention depending on the scenario, helping distinguish self-modal processing from cross-modal fusion, and likely also understanding from generation. The exact implementation details need the method section, but the design intent is straightforward: attention handles routing, FFN handles scenario-specific transformation, and together they realize decoupled computation within a unified framework.

The paper instantiates the framework in two versions. Libra-1 targets understanding-only image-to-text modeling. Two details matter here. First, supervision uses unified discrete autoregression over both vision and language, prioritizing training stability. Second, vision inputs use hybrid tokenization, combining continuous visual signals with discrete IDs to balance fine-grained perception and stable sequence modeling. This suggests the authors do not fully trust purely discrete visual tokens to carry enough perceptual detail.

Libra-2 extends the framework to joint multimodal understanding and generation. According to the introduction, it adds further strategies in tokenization, representation, and supervision to support both image-to-text and text-to-image. The central claim is not simply that these tasks can be unified, but that they can be unified without one side damaging the other, thanks to structural decoupling rather than only careful data balancing.

Compared with typical unified MLLMs, Libra treats cross-modal interaction as an explicit computation mode with dedicated mappings, not as the default state of every layer. In effect, the model learns a task-dependent representation space: the same image can map to different embeddings depending on whether the instruction asks about category, color, relation, or some other attribute.

Training data is built as synthetic image-instruction-answer triplets. An LLM serves as the knowledge source to generate instruction-response pairs for images, and these are combined into supervision tuples. This is an important design choice. The text is not merely descriptive captioning; it encodes task intent. That makes the supervision closer to instruction following than standard image-text alignment. At the same time, because answers remain in the text-embedding space, the method can keep a simple contrastive-style alignment objective instead of adding a full generative head.

The loss follows the SigLIP-style sigmoid alignment objective rather than the standard CLIP softmax contrastive loss. The paper argues this gives better stability and performance for batchwise matching. The text encoder acts as a semantic anchor, while optimization focuses on the instruction-conditioned vision encoder. At inference time, no large LLM is needed: image plus instruction directly yields a task-relevant embedding that can support downstream matching or prediction.

The main distinction from LLaVA/Flamingo-like systems is where adaptation happens. Those systems mostly keep the vision tower generic and rely on a large decoder to integrate language and solve tasks. LIVE pushes task control into the encoder. First is task construction. The authors use graph coloring and Sudoku rendered as images. Both require reading structured visual information and then performing discrete reasoning on top of it. Model outputs are constrained into a perception-first, reasoning-second format, so perception can be directly scored by exact match against the oracle representation. This also enables counterfactual reasoning evaluation: feed the model the ground-truth perception and measure whether it can reason correctly once perception is no longer the bottleneck.

Perception improves as its weight increases, but reasoning degrades, yielding a smooth trade-off. To avoid manual tuning, they also test dynamic multi-task balancing methods that adaptively adjust perception vs reasoning weights.

For RL, the key concept is reward coupling. Standard outcome rewards only depend on final correctness, so they correlate strongly with reasoning correctness but much less with perception correctness. Reported correlations are r=0.65-1.00 for reasoning and only r=0.34-0.43 for perception in most settings. The authors then add explicit perception rewards, and also test surrogate rewards from prior work when ground-truth perception rewards are unavailable. Again, the intervention exposes a trade-off: stronger perception reward improves perception but can weaken reasoning.

Third is intervention-based validation. On the SFT side, they compare fixed reweighting and dynamic balancing. On the RL side, they compare oracle perception rewards and surrogate rewards. The point is not that one specific weighting scheme wins everywhere, but that training-signal allocation itself explains the asymmetry much better than a vague claim that the architecture is simply better at reasoning.16:T788,Experiments cover two open VLM families, Qwen3-VL and InternVL3.5, on graph coloring and Sudoku. Moreover, when conditioned on oracle perception, reasoning is often already fairly strong, which means end-to-end failures are still dominated by perception. This pattern is stable across both model families and both tasks.

Second, evidence for token imbalance in SFT. On Qwen3-VL for graph coloring, perception tokens account for only 2.5% of the sequence, but only 1.3% of the loss and 8.5% of the gradient norm. Increasing the perception loss weight raises perception accuracy and lowers reasoning accuracy, but end-to-end accuracy peaks at a modest nonzero upweighting. Adding perception reward shifts optimization toward perception; in most settings, end-to-end performance peaks at a nonzero perception-reward weight, with gains up to +6.0 points over standard RL. When oracle perception reward is unavailable, surrogate rewards can still help. The paper evaluates several existing surrogate designs and finds that reward-perception correlation is a useful predictor of whether a surrogate will improve perception. The strongest surrogate gives +3.2 points.
