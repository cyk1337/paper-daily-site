5:T513,For pretraining researchers, the main value is conceptual. The paper makes explicit that PEFT is not only about how many parameters are updated, but also about which coordinate system the optimizer sees. LoRA’s empirical success has encouraged the view that low rank is a natural adaptation prior, but GPart shows that low rank also imposes geometric distortion. That connects directly to intrinsic dimension, random subspace training, and continual adaptation. If a simple globally randomized subspace can match or beat LoRA-style methods at very low budgets, then the essential ingredient for adaptation may not be low-rank structure itself, but access to a sufficiently expressive low-dimensional subspace with cleaner geometry. Two implications matter. First, post-training interfaces should be evaluated not only by memory and throughput, but by how well their parameterization aligns with local geometry in pretrained weight space. Second, if random global subspaces already work well, pretrained models may have substantial adaptation redundancy, which is relevant to plasticity analysis, selective updates, and even mid-training or cooldown-stage interventions. In contribution scale, this is a well-motivated PEFT paper with stronger geometric reasoning than most purely empirical tweaks.6:T840,The design is simple and deliberate. In LoRA, each weight update is parameterized as BA, so optimization happens in factor space while the actual update lives in weight space. Uni-LoRA compresses the trainable state further into a d-dimensional vector and maps it into LoRA parameter space with a random partition matrix, but it still ends with the same bilinear BA map, so end-to-end isometry is still broken. GPart removes that intermediate factorization entirely. All adapted weight matrices are flattened and concatenated into a single global vector of length N. The method then defines a sparse N by d partition matrix P, implemented implicitly rather than materialized. A seed-dependent random permutation is applied to the N coordinates, and the permuted coordinates are split into d disjoint groups. Each original parameter belongs to exactly one group, and all parameters in the same group share one trainable scalar, normalized by group size. By construction, the columns of P are orthonormal, so P transpose times P equals the identity. This makes the mapping from the d-dimensional trainable vector to the N-dimensional weight update an isometric embedding. Intuitively, GPart performs a global random tied update across the model: parameters in the same group receive the same scalar update, but the tying is global rather than layer-local or low-rank-factor-specific. Several design choices matter. First, the partition is global rather than per-layer, so the subspace budget is allocated across the whole model instead of forcing a fixed rank per layer. Second, there is no LoRA rank hyperparameter; d alone controls subspace size. Third, both forward and backward passes can be implemented efficiently through the assignment map. Compared with VeRA, GPart does not freeze random bases and learn only scaling vectors; it directly defines a random subspace in full weight space. Compared with intrinsic-dimension training, it is designed to be storage-efficient and practical. Compared with Uni-LoRA, it removes the main source of geometric distortion, namely the bilinear map back to weight space.7:T683,From the provided text, the main empirical claim is that GPart is competitive across both encoder and decoder settings, outperforming Uni-LoRA and related PEFT baselines on natural language understanding and computer vision benchmarks at low parameter budgets, while remaining competitive on decoder-only models and mathematical reasoning tasks. The breadth of evaluation matters because the paper is arguing for a parameterization principle, not a task-specific trick. The most important thing to inspect is the budget-performance curve. Since the central claim is about extreme compression, the key question is how GPart compares with LoRA, VeRA, and Uni-LoRA as the trainable dimension d changes. If the gains are concentrated in the very low-budget regime, that would support the geometric argument; if the gap narrows at larger budgets, that would also make sense because parameterization distortion matters less in higher dimensions. The theory section proves isometry, and the experiments should ideally show corresponding practical effects such as more stable optimization, simpler model selection, and reduced sensitivity to rank choices. Two possible weaknesses should be checked carefully. First, random grouping may introduce seed variance; if performance depends heavily on the seed, the method is less attractive in practice. Second, global tied updates may hurt tasks that require fine local adaptation, especially in decoder-only generative settings.
