Second, the correlation varies sharply by task, from -0.04 on travel planning to 0.85 on tax preparation, showing that the overlap between being a strong solver and a strong assistant is task dependent. Third, assistance is not always beneficial. The paper reports that GPT-5-mini ranks first on average in both regimes, but in augmentation the unaided worker baseline, where GPT-3.5-Turbo works without any external help, achieves the second-best average rank. That means some assistance harms performance, likely because it is too complex, poorly calibrated, or mismatched to the worker. The task-level reversals are especially informative: for example, Claude-Opus-4.8 is among the strongest direct solvers on market trends analysis with mean rank 2.05, yet among the weakest assistants on the same task with 8.15, while GPT-4.1 shows the opposite profile on counseling.19:T78e,The core contribution is a corrected protocol for measuring cross-lingual action-policy retention. Under a fixed scaffold, models operate over a shared five-tool alphabet and emit executed action traces alongside answers. The study covers 8 models, 6 parallel benchmarks, 41 languages, and 2.38 million rollouts. A naive trace-similarity metric fails for five reasons: short traces look artificially similar, empty traces score perfectly, unrelated traces overlap by chance, same-language reruns are not identical, and without a within-language baseline the quantity is not identifiable.

The estimator addresses each issue explicitly. Every language-task cell is run twice with identical decoding settings and token budgets, changing only the serving seed. Same-language agreement, I_within, compares the two replicates in one language. Cross-language agreement, I_cross, compares traces across languages, also cross-seed. The normalized retention metric is \tilde{I}=I_cross/I_within, which measures how much of a model’s own reproducible policy survives a language change. Comparisons are length-matched in both directions, pairs are dropped if either trace is empty, and uncertainty is estimated with task-level bootstraps. This design makes language the only systematic difference while keeping decoding noise symmetric.

The paper then probes mechanism. The evidence points to an English pivot. Translate is the most frequently used tool across adapted benchmarks, and reasoning text is about 99% ASCII even for non-Latin inputs such as Devanagari. Removing the translation tool lowers agreement in proportion to a model’s prior reliance on translation; forcing translation helps in a pre-registered ordering across models. The implication is that many multilingual agents do not execute the same policy directly in the target language. They often map the task into an English-centered internal workflow and plan from there.1a:T697,The empirical scale is substantial: 8 models, 6 parallel benchmarks, 41 languages, and 2.38M rollouts. Three results stand out. First, under greedy decoding, all 24 main cells show a positive cross-lingual policy gap, and every bootstrap interval on the raw gap excludes zero. The effect is stable rather than anecdotal. Second, every correction increases the measured effect instead of shrinking it. That is important because it means the problem was previously hidden by bad measurement, not because it was weak. Third, after normalization, four otherwise very different models converge to nearly the same policy-retention value under greedy decoding: about 71–73%. The regularity holds at the cell level, not just in model averages, and model identity explains only about 5.7% of the variance.

The temperature ladder is also informative. As sampling temperature rises, cross-language agreement changes little, while same-language self-consistency drops sharply. Without normalization, sampling noise masks the true cross-lingual policy loss. On the mechanism side, Translate is the most-used tool in every adapted benchmark, and reasoning text is almost entirely ASCII, indicating that non-English tasks are often routed through an English internal channel. Removing translation hurts agreement roughly in proportion to how much a model already uses it; forcing translation helps in the pre-registered order predicted from those usage rates. - Verbalization-based: prompt the LLM to output a numeric confidence (with variants like CoT/self-probing/top-k verbalization).
- Sampling-based: sample multiple predictions and aggregate (e.g., consistency frequency; mean of sampled verbalized confidences).
- Logit-based: use token log-probabilities (e.g., probability of the class-label token) when the API exposes logprobs.
Main analysis: verbalization yields highly discrete confidence values due to strong preferences for certain numeric tokens, producing only a few distinct thresholds.
Evaluation pitfall: AUARC requires integrating a curve defined by discrete operating points. Linear interpolation effectively invents intermediate operating points, which can over-credit sparse methods; stepwise interpolation better matches the semantics of thresholding (metrics should not change until the threshold crosses an actually attainable confidence value). The authors advocate standardizing stepwise interpolation.
Proposed method: verbalization logprobs. Nodes are partial prefixes, complete paths are traces, and an inference algorithm specifies how to expand, score, stop, and aggregate under a budget. The work separates three regimes. Single-trajectory sequential scaling spends extra compute on one evolving solution path. Sampled inference generates completed candidates and then applies voting, reranking, verifier selection, judge selection, or minimum-Bayes-risk style reduction. Partial-state search allocates compute before completion, expanding and pruning unfinished prefixes. A useful distinction is made between evidence computed per trace and decisions made over a completed candidate pool. Causal stopping is modeled separately because it changes generation cost, not just selection behavior.
