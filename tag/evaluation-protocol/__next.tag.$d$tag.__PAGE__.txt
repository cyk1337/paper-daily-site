FEB-30 contains 30 fact-evolution trajectories spanning 6 diagnostic categories and 3 difficulty levels. The categories are designed to isolate failure modes that extrinsic QA-based evaluation cannot disentangle:
1. C1, LLM Compensation: the memory state is wrong, but the model may still answer correctly.
2. C2, Retrieval Bottleneck: the memory state is correct, but retrieval fails because of lexical mismatch or abstraction mismatch.
3. C3, Classification Accuracy: whether the system correctly classifies observations as create, update, reinforce, or conflict.
4. C4, Synthesis Quality: whether evidence from multiple artifacts is merged into the right fact structure.
5. C5, Temporal Correctness: whether the memory state tracks temporal evolution, retraction, and drift correctly.
6. C6, Multi-Author Attribution: whether source identity is handled correctly in corroboration, conflict handling, and confidence updates.

Difficulty is controlled in a structured way. L1 is close to surface matching, with target claims appearing almost verbatim. L2 embeds claims in more realistic document context. L3 requires inference from multiple statements and includes substantial distractor content.

The most important design choice is what the authors call paradigm inversion. Instead of starting from documents and then annotating facts, they first define the exact atomic claims that should hold at each state of a trajectory, then generate documents from those trajectories. The pipeline also uses multi-model rotation and three-judge verification to reduce generator-specific artifacts and improve factual consistency.

Evaluation is split into intrinsic and extrinsic metrics. Intrinsic evaluation directly compares the memory store against the state-specific ground truth, using metrics such as claim coverage, update detection, and false update rate. The first is a mechanistic diagnosis of what the authors call the consensus–termination gap. They generate one main reasoning trajectory per problem, freeze it, and then append probes offline to different prefixes of that same trajectory. This frozen-trajectory design is crucial because it ensures that different probing schedules and stopping rules observe identical underlying reasoning rather than perturbing it. On top of this substrate, they run three direct measurements.

First, wording sensitivity: for the same frozen prefix, they vary the wording of the probe and check whether the extracted current answer changes. If early prefixes are highly sensitive to probe wording, then the answer is being elicited by the query rather than read out from a settled internal state. Second, a hand-labeled taxonomy of wrong stops: among cases where the rule stops and the committed answer is wrong, they distinguish trajectories that had genuinely converged to a wrong answer from trajectories that had not converged at all and were merely forced into a temporary placeholder by the probe. Third, non-terminal commitment analysis: they measure how often a consensus stop commits to an answer that the original trajectory later abandons, and whether the truncated continuation would have corrected that answer.

The second component is a preregistered exhaustive sweep over a family of windowed consensus rules. The search space includes window size, agreement-share threshold, and operational knobs, for a total of 3,520 rules. These are evaluated on frozen trajectories from two models and three benchmarks with problem-grouped splits and pre-fixed acceptance gates. This is a strong design choice: instead of showcasing a few hand-picked operating points, the paper asks whether the rule family contains any robustly usable point at all. The non-terminal commitment analysis is even more telling: at a consensus rule that still saves 32% of tokens, one stop in nine commits an answer that the trajectory later leaves behind. The study covers 8 models, 6 parallel benchmarks, 41 languages, and 2.38 million rollouts. A naive trace-similarity metric fails for five reasons: short traces look artificially similar, empty traces score perfectly, unrelated traces overlap by chance, same-language reruns are not identical, and without a within-language baseline the quantity is not identifiable.

The estimator addresses each issue explicitly. Every language-task cell is run twice with identical decoding settings and token budgets, changing only the serving seed. Same-language agreement, I_within, compares the two replicates in one language. Cross-language agreement, I_cross, compares traces across languages, also cross-seed. The normalized retention metric is \tilde{I}=I_cross/I_within, which measures how much of a model’s own reproducible policy survives a language change. Comparisons are length-matched in both directions, pairs are dropped if either trace is empty, and uncertainty is estimated with task-level bootstraps. This design makes language the only systematic difference while keeping decoding noise symmetric.
