9:T618,The authors do not propose a new detector. Instead, they reuse an existing three-tier candidate-generation pipeline and evaluate eight fully specified cleaning policies on top of it. Tier 1 performs exact and near-exact matching after normalization of whitespace, casing, and LaTeX macros. Tier 2 performs lexical matching using 13-gram and 8-gram coverage plus a 128-permutation MinHash/LSH Jaccard estimate. Tier 3 performs semantic retrieval with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and cosine similarity. A subset of lexical and semantic candidate pairs is then adjudicated by an existing LLM judge into same, subsumes, variant, or different. The eight policies differ in how much they trust exact matches, lexical heuristics, semantic thresholds, and LLM-judge outputs.

The strongest part of the paper is the evaluation design. The authors build a new independently and blindly dual-reviewed reference set of 594 training-benchmark pairs, split in advance into calibration and held-out partitions. The held-out split is disjoint not only in benchmark items but also in training-example clusters, so policy tuning is tested on genuinely unseen corpora and questions. They also sample 80 candidate-bearing training items and review every candidate edge attached to each item. That enables item-level false-removal measurement: a training item counts as wrongly removed only if none of its candidate edges is truly contaminating. This is more faithful than pair-level precision, which can overstate conservativeness when one item has both true and false candidate matches.a:T49f,The audit covers 16 open RLVR math corpora and 6 benchmarks: MATH-500, GSM8K test, AIME24, AIME25, AIME26, and AMC23. After normalization and corpus-specific deduplication, the study analyzes 1,673,514 corpus-specific training items corresponding to 1,197,691 unique underlying problem texts. Evaluation uses 594 manually reviewed pairs plus 80 fully resolved item-level samples.

The central empirical result is clear: the common heuristic “remove if semantic similarity exceeds a fixed threshold” becomes unreliable as the threshold is relaxed. Candidate-frame recall improves, but manual review shows that at permissive thresholds, most sampled removals are false removals. In other words, semantic retrieval is useful for candidate generation, but weak as a direct removal rule. The paper also reports the cost of each policy in retained corpus size and source-composition shift, showing that cleaning decisions affect not only how much data remains but also which sources remain. Detection (scalable and shard-invariant): documents are segmented at natural boundaries; each segment is normalized (formatting/casing/whitespace etc., per the paper’s rules) and exact-hashed; distributed key–value aggregation computes global occurrence counts for each hash, ensuring cross-shard visibility.

The evaluation is organized as a progression of bias complexity: ImageNet for overall recognition, CelebA for a single spurious attribute, and UrbanCars for two simultaneous spurious attributes. The paper formalizes this through group structure induced by labels and spurious attributes, with the number of groups growing combinatorially as more attributes are involved. That framing matters because it explains why multi-attribute bias is qualitatively harder, not just a slightly noisier benchmark.

The analysis decomposes several design factors: model size, training data size, whether the training data is curated or unfiltered, patch size, and architecture family. A useful part of the paper is that it does not rely only on global correlations. It also performs controlled comparisons where one factor changes while others are kept as comparable as possible within a family. That reduces the risk of attributing gains from better data or newer architectures to scale alone.

Three findings stand out. First, model scaling remains effective for standard recognition but loses predictive power for robustness under complex bias. Second, data properties are more stable: larger and better curated datasets retain meaningful association with worst-group performance even in the harder settings. Third, input granularity matters: coarser visual patches hurt worst-group accuracy, suggesting that fine-grained visual tokens help the model separate causal object evidence from background shortcuts.

The mechanism proposed by the paper is sensible. Contrastive VLM training benefits from frequent image-text co-occurrences, which are often shortcuts. More capacity helps fit those regularities, but does not force the model to rely on causally relevant features. Data curation changes the shortcut distribution directly, and finer patching preserves local evidence that may be necessary to resist background or co-occurrence bias.11:T5d8,The experimental coverage is the backbone of the paper: 194 public VLMs from 16 families, parameter counts from 63M to 3.6B, and 24 training data sources ranging from 13M to 12.8B samples. The most important numbers are straightforward. The Spearman correlation between model scale and performance drops from 0.68 on ImageNet to 0.48 on CelebA and to 0.05 on UrbanCars. In controlled comparisons where model size is the main varying factor, scaling improves ImageNet accuracy by about +2.6% on average, but reduces UrbanCars worst-group accuracy by -4.2%. So larger models can become better at exploiting shortcuts rather than better at avoiding them. Data-related factors are more stable: training data size still shows a correlation of 0.41 with robustness on the multi-attribute benchmark, and curated datasets outperform size-matched unfiltered alternatives by up to 25% in worst-group accuracy.
