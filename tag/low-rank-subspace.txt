17:T70d,The method, TILR (Trajectory-Invariant Latent Refinement), follows a simple principle: identify a stable low-rank subspace first, then constrain refinement to that subspace. The authors collect hidden-state differences between stronger and weaker reasoning trajectories and study their structure across inputs, paraphrases, perturbations, and reference checkpoint choices. Empirically, these contrastive differences are highly concentrated spectrally: a small number of directions explain most of the variation between strong and weak trajectories. TILR uses this observation to estimate an invariant subspace that captures reasoning-associated directions that persist across semantically equivalent inputs and across reference settings. Once this subspace is learned, the usual contrastive latent update is projected into it, filtering out unstable components that are specific to a particular phrasing or trajectory realization. A second component, the adaptive alignment gate, modulates intervention strength based on how well the current trajectory aligns with the learned invariant structure. When alignment is high, the intervention is trusted more; when alignment is weak, the method suppresses the update to avoid injecting noisy or mismatched steering signals. This is the main conceptual difference from prior latent refinement methods: the paper rejects the assumption that the full strong-minus-weak hidden-state difference is uniformly meaningful. It treats only the low-rank, cross-condition stable component as reliable. The paper also introduces trajectory-level analyses beyond final accuracy, including paraphrase consistency, latent trajectory variance, and checkpoint-pair sensitivity, to test whether the identified directions are functionally relevant rather than merely descriptive.18:T485,Several experimental findings are clear from the full text excerpt. First, the difference between stronger and weaker reasoning trajectories exhibits a pronounced low-rank structure: a small number of directions explain most of the variation. This supports the invariant-subspace premise. Second, across six benchmarks, subspace-constrained interventions improve reasoning consistency and trajectory stability while largely preserving task accuracy. Third, the paper explicitly evaluates three robustness axes: consistency under paraphrases, variance under trajectory perturbations, and sensitivity to the chosen reference checkpoint pair. Unconstrained latent steering is fragile on all three, whereas TILR reduces that fragility. The provided text does not include the full result tables, benchmark names, model sizes, or exact gains, so it would be unsafe to invent numbers. The experimental point is not primarily a large accuracy jump. It is to show that the low-rank invariant directions are functionally meaningful: when interventions are restricted to them, trajectories become more stable and more consistent without materially harming correctness.19:T580,Read this as a geometry-and-mechanism paper, not as a plug-and-play training recipe. Then focus on the method section that constructs the invariant subspace. Two deeper follow-up questions are worth keeping in mind: whether the low-rank structure is specific to latent reasoning or a generic property of hidden-state differences between stronger and weaker models, and whether the learned invariant subspace can be turned into a training-time regularizer, a distillation target, or an architectural prior for recurrent latent reasoning blocks.1a:T679,For each behavior, the authors build a matched contrastive dataset with positive examples that elicit the behavior and negative examples that suppress it. At a chosen layer of a decoder-only Transformer, they take the last-token residual-stream activation and compute paired contrast vectors between positive and negative examples. These vectors are centered and decomposed with SVD. Instead of representing a behavior by a single steering direction, the method represents it as a top-k low-rank subspace.

A key step is removing decision-aligned variance. The leading singular components can reflect the model’s final positive-vs-negative decision rather than behavior-specific structure. The paper defines a scalar decision margin using the log-probability difference of label strings, identifies components aligned with this margin, and separates them from the behavior subspace analysis. This is meant to avoid conflating decision readout with the internal representation of the behavior.

The diagnostic then studies two quantities. The first is geometric overlap between behavior subspaces, measured through projection-style or principal-angle-style comparisons. The second is decision coupling, which measures how strongly a behavior subspace is connected to the model’s decision direction. Projection-based interventions are applied to a source behavior subspace, and the induced changes are measured on target behaviors. Overlap explains whether an intervention can reach another behavior; decision coupling explains whether that shared representation actually affects the output. 1) Key observation (linear-layer gradients lie in activation span): for a linear layer, the weight gradient is G_W = X^T G_out, hence it lies in the span/column space of input activations X. Therefore, if activations are approximated in a rank-r subspace, gradients admit an optimal rank-r representation in the same subspace.
2) Compress activations while keeping forward exact: OASIS does not modify forward-pass computation. It only projects and stores intermediate activations for backward:
- Store \tilde X_t = X_t U_t (N×r) instead of X_t (N×d).
- Perform gradient/optimizer computations in the low-rank space and project updates back via U_t.
3) Joint low-rank gradients and optimizer states: since gradients are kept as (r×m), Adam’s first/second moments can be stored and updated in the same low-rank space, reducing memory from O(dm) to O(rm) per layer.
4) Online subspace learning (core novelty): U_t cannot be fixed because activation distributions drift as parameters change. The paper uses Oja’s rule (streaming PCA) to update U_t incrementally without repeated exact eigendecomposition.
5) Projection-aware state updates: when U_t changes, low-rank momentum/variance states must be transformed to remain consistent with the new basis; otherwise optimization becomes unstable. Following LDAdam, they apply projection-aware alignment of optimizer states after subspace updates.
