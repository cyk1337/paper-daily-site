9:T6b8,The method has two main components. The first is a behavioral diversity metric. The authors adapt Hierarchic Social Entropy (HSE), originally proposed for robot societies, to language-model societies. Instead of comparing parameters or hidden states, they derive behavioral vectors from model outputs, compute pairwise similarities, build a hierarchical clustering over actors, and integrate entropy across clustering scales. This gives a diversity score that reflects both the number of distinct behavioral clusters and how balanced they are. The design choice is important: routing operates over input-output behavior, so output-level differentiation is more relevant than architectural or parametric differences.

The second component is a perturbation-based robustness metric for routers. For each query, the authors generate semantically related variants under a five-level perturbation taxonomy and measure whether the router assigns these variants to the same actor. This isolates assignment stability from task accuracy. A router may achieve good utility by exploiting superficial prompt cues, yet fail to produce stable specialization because small wording changes alter the selected actor.

A notable part of the setup is the comparison between real-world model pools and synthetic specialist societies. The synthetic societies are constructed to vary role structure and expertise overlap, which lets the authors test whether HSE tracks genuine behavioral differentiation rather than arbitrary output variation. They also study HSE as a function of society size, effectively using it as a coreset signal: once HSE saturates, adding more actors mostly adds redundancy rather than new routing-relevant structure.a:T62e,Experiments are run on two routing benchmarks, EmbedLLM and RouterBench. The main findings are clear. First, synthetic specialist societies achieve substantially higher HSE than real-world model pools of comparable size. This suggests that deployed model pools are often less behaviorally diverse than people assume; many models differ in quality or cost, but not enough in behavior to support rich specialization.

Second, HSE shows strong diminishing returns with society size. The paper reports that fewer than ten agents recover most available diversity on EmbedLLM, and around four agents do so on RouterBench. This is practically useful: beyond a small curated subset, larger pools may mostly increase routing complexity without adding meaningful behavioral coverage.

Third, higher diversity does not imply higher routing robustness. KNN routers achieve their best accuracy on specialist societies but their worst robustness, while prompted routing remains comparatively stable across HSE levels and perturbation types. This is an important separation: diversity, utility, and robustness are not aligned by default.

The most useful experimental takeaway is relational rather than leaderboard-driven. A system can have high routing accuracy over a redundant society, and a highly diverse society can still fail to realize specialization if the router is unstable.
