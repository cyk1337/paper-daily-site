5:T8d9,The method, Bidirectional Evolutionary Search (BES), has two tightly coupled components.

The first is bidirectional search. The forward side generates candidate solutions in the usual search sense. The backward side starts from the goal or terminal condition and decomposes it into finer-grained sub-goals, which provide denser intermediate verification signals. The motivation is straightforward: if the verifier only gives a terminal binary reward, search has little guidance on whether an unfinished trajectory is structurally promising. Backward decomposition turns a sparse end reward into a set of local constraints, making partial trajectories more comparable.

The second is the use of evolutionary operators. The authors argue that expansion-only search is fundamentally limited because every new candidate must be produced by extending an existing prefix. That keeps search near the model’s own continuation distribution. BES introduces four operators: combination, translocation, deletion, and crossover. The recombination-style operators merge useful segments from different trajectories, while deletion removes the least reliable segment from a candidate. This means new candidates no longer need to arise from a single left-to-right rollout; they can be assembled through discrete structural edits over existing traces. The paper also provides a theoretical argument that expansion-only search is confined to a relatively narrow entropy shell, whereas evolutionary operators can escape that region. It is not a full optimality result, but it gives a clear mechanism for why recombination may outperform pure tree expansion.

In implementation terms, BES treats the LLM as a proposal and mutation engine. The model generates trajectories, rewrites segments, decomposes goals, and helps score local structure, while the search framework maintains the candidate pool and applies bidirectional and evolutionary operations. Compared with Tree-of-Thought, beam search, or MCTS, the key difference is that BES allows cross-branch recombination rather than only branch-wise expansion. Compared with coding-oriented evolutionary systems such as OpenEvolve, BES adds backward goal decomposition so that verification is not only applied at the terminal state.6:T762,The experiments are split into post-training and inference settings. The most important empirical claim is not a small average gain on easy benchmarks, but a failure-mode difference on hard reasoning tasks: baseline methods often cannot find enough high-quality samples to improve the model at all, and sometimes even degrade it, whereas BES continues to surface usable trajectories.

In the post-training setup, the paper studies logical reasoning and multi-hop reasoning tasks where GRPO, MaxRL, and Tree-GRPO struggle because positive samples are too rare under the base policy. The central result is that BES substantially increases the discovery rate of high-scoring trajectories, which in turn makes downstream RLVR updates effective. The key thing to inspect is not only final benchmark scores, but the regime where baselines barely move or regress while BES still produces measurable improvement.

In inference, BES is evaluated on three open problem-solving benchmarks against open-source frameworks including OpenEvolve, GEPA, and ShinkaEvolve. The reported outcome is that BES finds more stable and higher-quality solutions. The paper also includes component ablations showing that both the bidirectional design and the evolutionary operators matter. Removing backward decomposition makes search rely too heavily on terminal verification. Removing recombination operators makes search more likely to stay trapped in the model’s existing modes. The case studies are useful because they show that some successful solutions are not obtained by gradually extending a single trajectory, but by recombining two incomplete trajectories that each contain locally correct pieces.

If reading more deeply, focus on two issues: how dependent the method is on verifier quality, and whether the generated trajectories introduce distribution shift during post-training by favoring unnatural but verifiable reasoning traces.8:T86e,The central idea is to replace the usual frontier state with a persistent pool of historical prefixes. Each round still expands only a fixed budget of N new prefixes, so after t rounds the pool size is at most Nt. This keeps memory growth linear rather than storing the full exponential tree. The key change is that a prefix that was not selected in one round is not permanently discarded; it can be reconsidered later.

The paper instantiates this idea in two ways.

The first is Subpool Selection. A naive global top-k over the full history would still be dominated by a few over-scored but ultimately wrong prefixes. Uniform sampling from history would waste tokens on stale or low-quality states. Subpool Selection instead samples a random historical subpool and then applies PRM-based ranking within that shortlist. This preserves PRM guidance while giving under-scored prefixes a chance to re-enter expansion. In effect, it adds controlled stochasticity on top of greedy PRM ranking to break long-lived blocking by falsely high-scored nodes.

The second is Power Backtrack SMC. This extends SMC-style sequential sampling from the current frontier to a persistent historical pool. Prefixes are sampled from a mixture over historical states, with powered PRM scores controlling how sharp the proposal distribution is, and mixture-corrected importance weights compensating for the fact that candidates come from multiple historical proposal sources. Compared with standard frontier-only SMC, this version can jump back to earlier states and recover from bad pruning decisions.

A strong part of the paper is its treatment of target mismatch. The ideal quantity is the true marginal probability that a prefix can still be completed into a correct final answer under the deployed generator. Real PRMs are usually trained on binary supervision or stronger rollout policies, so their scores are only proxies. They may be uncalibrated and may not match the continuation behavior of the base LLM used at inference. The paper’s main mechanism claim is that the real brittleness comes from combining this imperfect proxy with irreversible frontier-only pruning.9:T5e3,The experiments focus on mathematical reasoning and evaluate the accuracy–token trade-off rather than only final accuracy at a fixed sample count. The proposed methods are compared against strong baselines from two families: independent full-sample methods such as Best-of-N and self-consistency, and PRM-guided frontier-only methods such as beam search, tree search, and SMC variants. The qualitative result is consistent: at the same token budget, Subpool Selection and Power Backtrack SMC usually achieve higher accuracy; or, for the same target accuracy, they require fewer generated tokens.

The gains are most visible when PRM scores are noisy or only weakly correlated with eventual correctness, because historical revisiting can repair early mistakes. The ablations carry three main messages. First, persistence alone is not enough: keeping history but using overly greedy global selection gives limited benefit. Second, too much randomness also hurts, so subpool size, power temperature, and resampling policy all matter as exploration–exploitation controls. Third, the improvements appear across model scales, suggesting the effect is not tied to a single base model or PRM.

The second key design is search–evaluation co-training. The motivation is to reduce evaluator–policy mismatch. A frozen evaluator may become stale as the policy changes its search behavior, while likelihood-based evaluators can be inconsistent with the policy’s own generation dynamics under partial prefixes. Jointly training search and evaluation is meant to keep the process reward adaptive to the current policy distribution.
