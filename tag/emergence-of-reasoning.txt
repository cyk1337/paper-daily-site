1a:T53f,The paper introduces StALT, short for Spatiotemporal Amplitude of Latent Transition. The intuition is that genuine reasoning should not look like a smooth, uniform hidden-state drift. It should produce stronger temporal transitions during decoding, and those transitions should be concentrated in a subset of layers rather than spread evenly across the network. The authors therefore define two local variation measures. The first, Δ_time, measures hidden-state change between adjacent decoding steps at a fixed layer. The second, Δ_layer, measures hidden-state change between adjacent layers at a fixed generation step. Together they form a token-by-layer grid of local dynamics. The empirical observation is that successful reasoning trajectories tend to show broader temporal fluctuations and sharper layer-local concentration. StALT operationalizes this by combining token-wise temporal amplitude with dynamic layer-local saliency into a single score. The method is training-free: it does not require fitting an auxiliary classifier, and it avoids collapsing all layers into a single averaged trajectory. Relative to prior hidden-state probes, the key difference is preserving local structure in both time and depth, and interpreting reasoning success as a specific spatiotemporal pattern rather than just larger norms or longer outputs.1b:T45a,The experiments use Qwen3-0.6B as a representative reasoning model and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as a non-reasoning baseline. The main result is that correct trajectories exhibit higher StALT than incorrect ones on reasoning-heavy tasks, and this contrast is stronger in reasoning models than in non-reasoning baselines. The pattern weakens in knowledge-dominant domains, which supports the claim that StALT is tracking deliberative computation rather than generic generation activity or factual recall. The paper also includes intervention studies showing that when reasoning behavior is systematically modulated, StALT changes in the expected direction.
