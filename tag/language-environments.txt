18:T845,SDP decomposes each decision step into four operators: Propose, Realize, Validate, and Replan. In Propose, the model does not immediately choose an action. It first generates a natural-language predicate describing the condition that should hold after the next action. This predicate is intended to be checkable against the next observation, not merely a vague plan. In Realize, the agent selects an action intended to make the predicate true. In Validate, the new observation is checked against the predicate. If the predicate holds, it becomes a certified state in the trajectory. If not, the process enters Replan, where the agent revises its next predicate or action.

The key idea is not simply adding another reasoning step. The important move is forcing intent into an explicit, falsifiable form that the environment can arbitrate. Prior agent frameworks often generate intermediate text—thoughts, plans, reflections, or internal world descriptions—but those texts are usually not treated as verifiable state propositions. As a result, they do not induce certified state transitions. SDP is designed precisely to fill that missing interface layer.

The theoretical motivation is that useful state abstractions in language environments are goal-dependent. The environment cannot provide a universal state space in advance, because what counts as a useful state depends on the task. The agent therefore has to construct the relevant state abstraction online. In that sense, SDP is positioned as a runtime layer that makes MDP-style reasoning possible, rather than as a replacement for planning or policy learning.

Two practical issues are central to whether the method works. First, predicate granularity matters: predicates that are too coarse are hard to validate, while predicates that are too fine make trajectories long and expensive. Second, validator quality is critical. If validation is noisy or biased, the certified trajectory becomes unreliable. The paper explicitly discusses validator auditing, which suggests the authors treat this as a first-order issue rather than an implementation detail.19:T695,Empirically, the paper reports the best training-free performance on five benchmarks, with the advantage increasing as task horizon grows. That trend matches the motivation: on short tasks, agents can often get away without explicit state certification, but on long-horizon tasks, errors compound and unverified assumptions become costly. The paper also includes operator-level ablations to isolate the contributions of Propose, Validate, and Replan. The point of these ablations is to show that the gains do not come merely from generating more text, but from structuring intermediate reasoning as verifiable state transitions.

A second contribution of the experiments is diagnostic rather than purely performance-based. Certified trajectories enable analyses that are difficult to obtain from standard language-agent traces: failure localization, partial-progress tracking, cascade recording, and validator auditing. Failure localization identifies where the first broken predicate appears. Partial-progress tracking distinguishes total failure from trajectories that completed some verified subgoals. Cascade recording shows how one failed validation can propagate into later mistakes. Validator auditing checks whether the validation mechanism itself is trustworthy.

It is also important to inspect whether the validator relies on extra model calls and how much inference overhead the framework introduces.
