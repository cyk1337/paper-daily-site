17:T455,The central method is a belief-rollout diagnostic. Instead of training a separate transition model, the paper defines the agent’s world model at inference time as an elicited structured belief trajectory. For the same task, environment, and model, only the harness is varied. The model is asked to produce a K-step rollout containing predicted progress, active constraints, risk state, recoverability, uncertainty, likely failure mode, future success probability, and the recommended next action. Cross-harness belief divergence is then computed over these structured trajectories. The paper further decomposes the divergence into an arrival term and a growth term. The arrival term captures immediate interface-level shifts, such as blocked or rewritten actions. The growth term captures horizon-dependent drift in fields such as risk, failure mode, uncertainty, and success estimates. The proposed BIWM protocol is training-free: it canonicalizes beliefs, preserves blocked and repaired branches, records verification masks, executes risky branches in shadow mode, and aligns beliefs across harness views.18:T82f,The work consists of two benchmark components plus a unified execution setup. Stat-Closed contains 404 questions spanning 18 statistical topics, with multiple formats: multiple choice, fill-in-the-blank, decision-making, and practical application. The practical-application subset is the key design choice: each instance is a triplet of statistical question, dataset, and ground-truth function parameters, so the model must interact with the provided toolkit rather than answer purely in prose.

The toolkit, SAToolKit, exposes 35 callable statistical functions covering descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, regression, causal inference, survival analysis, high-dimensional analysis, and more. This lets the benchmark evaluate three distinct abilities in a controlled way: selecting the right procedure, specifying valid parameters, and interpreting the returned results.

For data construction, the authors aggregate material from textbooks, prior benchmarks, and public exam-style resources. To mitigate contamination, they use a modify-and-verify pipeline: questions answered correctly by Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct are flagged as potentially leaked, then manually perturbed by rewriting stems, changing distractors, shuffling options, or inverting conditions. Only items that at least one model subsequently fails, or that experts explicitly validate as high quality, are retained. This is not a formal guarantee against leakage, but it is stronger than naive collection from public sources.

Stat-Open contains 30 open-ended tasks adapted from mathematical modeling competitions such as MCM/ICM, CUMCM, and MAS. These require end-to-end data cleaning, exploratory analysis, model building, and structured report generation. Since there is no single gold answer, the authors use structured outputs and an LLM-as-Judge protocol, with claimed human alignment validation. On the execution side, Stat-Closed is run mainly through LangChain MCP with SAToolKit, and the paper also compares several agent frameworks including Qwen Agent, CrewAI, AutoGen, and Smolagents.19:T488,The headline numbers are straightforward. On Stat-Closed, GPT-5.1 reaches only 68.6% accuracy, while the strongest open model reported, Qwen2.5-72B, gets 60.6%. On Stat-Open, the best agent framework achieves an average score of 61.86. The paper also reports a Friedman test with p = 2.87 × 10^-9, indicating that the performance differences across systems are statistically significant.

More interesting than the absolute numbers is the structure of the evaluation. The benchmark separates easier knowledge-centric formats from practical tool-based tasks and then from open-ended modeling. That layered design helps identify where models fail: conceptual recall, procedural execution, or long-horizon analytical planning. The comparison across agent frameworks is also informative, because it shows that the same underlying model can move noticeably depending on orchestration and tool-calling setup. If the full tables include per-topic or per-format breakdowns, those are worth checking carefully—topics like causal inference, survival analysis, and high-dimensional statistics are likely where conceptual and operational weaknesses become most visible.1a:T423,The KAPRO (Knowing-Acting Quadrant Probe) framework splits evaluation along two independent axes: (1) Knowing, where execution is blocked and the model is forced to make per-substep judgments about whether external tool support is required, measuring explicit awareness of capability boundaries; (2) Acting, where the model's spontaneous tool use is observed in a standard unconstrained tool environment. Crossing these two axes produces a four-quadrant mapping that quantifies cognitive-behavioral alignment, separating 'unknown unknowns' from 'knowing but acting wrong' failures. The accompanying KAware dataset partitions tasks into three controlled subspaces: purely external-dependent tasks requiring real-time or private information outside parametric coverage, purely internal-solvable tasks within parametric capability (translation, summarization, commonsense reasoning), and hybrid tasks requiring selective tool use across subtasks. Task construction controls for tool count, reasoning hop count, and parallelism to eliminate confounding variables.1b:T4ac,SentinelBench reframes a class of tasks from continuous action to sustained attention. Environments evolve over time without the agent’s intervention; the agent should detect when a condition becomes true and then act promptly.

The benchmark includes 10 synthetic but high-fidelity web apps (React frontend, FastAPI backend, SQLite; single authenticated user assumption) and 100 tasks defined as time-stamped event replays (e.g., monitor a jobs page; if a new posting mentions Kubernetes, apply and notify).

Metrics go beyond task completion:
- Reaction time: how quickly the agent finishes after the trigger condition is met.
- Resource utilization: token usage (self-reported by cooperative agents) and implied cost.

To isolate “waiting” as an experimental factor, they compare two harnesses:
- sleep(time): unconditional yielding.
- wait_for(condition, timeout): a structured waiting primitive that heuristically detects page-content changes.

Key trade-offs: synthetic environments buy controllability and reproducibility of temporal events, but reduce ecological validity vs real websites. Starting from a longer candidate list, the authors iteratively trimmed and merged fields during pilot scoring until they reached a schema that could be applied in about an hour per paper using only the paper and public repository, without rerunning models. The final schema has five fields:
1) benchmark identity: benchmark name, version or commit, subset, and grader/evaluator version;
2) harness specification: scaffold name and version, system prompt (verbatim or hash), tool inventory, stopping rule, and environment identifier; notably, the environment should be content-addressed, such as a Docker digest, rather than a mutable tag;
3) inference settings: model name, inference engine, sampling configuration, seed or explicit statement that no seed is exposed, and run date; the run date matters because closed API aliases can silently change over time;
4) cost reporting: budget, token usage, or cost information;
5) failure breakdown: not just the final score, but what kinds of failures occurred.

A key design choice is that the schema measures disclosure adequacy, not experimental correctness. A score of 1.0 means a competent reader could attempt a faithful re-execution, not that the evaluation design was ideal. The paper also spends useful effort on boundary cases: whether a reference harness and a proposed scaffold should be treated differently, whether prompts must be fully exposed, and why environment digests are preferable to repo tags.
