a:T48e,SenWorld is a physically grounded, deterministic, event-sourced digital twin. Personas, smartphones, external services, and physical context evolve together in one simulated world. Several design choices matter. Real-world constraints are injected through maps, weather, holidays, and network data, so movement, communication, and device state changes are not purely fictional. The system is deterministic, which supports reproducibility. It is also event-sourced, meaning state transitions are recorded as events and full-system snapshots can be exported at each simulated frame. This allows evaluation cases to be tied to exact moments in a longitudinal trajectory. Ground truth is represented as a pointer into the snapshot, such as a source table plus a unique record ID, rather than a natural-language answer. The paper also introduces a snapshot-centered projection mechanism: one world snapshot can be deterministically transformed into multiple downstream testing artifacts. Compared with prior state-grounded evaluation setups, the key difference is that SenWorld targets a richer smartphone ecosystem and avoids LLM-based judging in the final scoring loop.b:T41f,The empirical section focuses on three questions. First, distributional fidelity: the authors simulate full-day trajectories for 16 personas in Beijing and compare generated data against a held-out real-user benchmark over six shared categories. The reported conclusion is that the synthetic data tracks the real benchmark reasonably well at the distribution level. Second, emergent behavior: instead of hand-authoring interaction scripts for each persona, the framework lets behavior arise from persona settings, schedules, environment, and device dynamics, producing nontrivial links across contacts, locations, communication logs, and app state. Third, practical utility: the generated evaluation cases are used to diagnose defects in a production smartphone assistant, and the paper shows that these cases can surface concrete failures and help localize them. This is not a model-scaling paper, so readers should not expect parameter-count or training-token tables. The experimental value lies in the realism checks, case studies, and defect analysis.c:T5cc,The core idea is to benchmark agents under a much broader operational scope. The environment is expanded along three axes: long-horizon event histories, interdependent backend services, and multi-device interaction through both GUI and CLI. Unlike many prior benchmarks that isolate a single app or a single-turn task, Claw-Anything embeds each task inside an evolving, persona-grounded digital world.

The environment is built through an automated synthesis pipeline. Starting from a minimal persona seed, an LLM-based simulator performs multi-round event injection, incrementally adding everyday events and updating both persistent world state and dynamic service traces such as email, calendars, and social platforms. As the process continues, the event history grows, the persona becomes more specific, and the environment accumulates realistic noise, including irrelevant and contradictory events. The next event is then turned into a grounded task with an executable verifier, so evaluation becomes a question of whether the agent can perform the appropriate next action in an evolving digital life.

Two design choices are especially notable. First, tasks emerge from the simulated world rather than being written independently of it, which makes temporal dependencies and hidden assumptions more natural. Second, the benchmark includes proactive assistance, so agents are evaluated not only on responding to explicit requests but also on inferring useful actions from context.d:T4c5,The main empirical result is straightforward: GPT-5.5 reaches only 34.5% pass@1 on Claw-Anything, much lower than the performance many models report on earlier benchmarks. The intended conclusion is that prior evaluations underrepresent the difficulty of integrating long histories, coordinating across services, and acting across devices. For a benchmark paper, that is the key result.

In terms of scale, the paper builds 200 human-verified evaluation tasks and 2,000 training environments. More importantly, it shows that these environments are useful beyond evaluation. Fine-tuning Qwen3.5-27B on 1,500 successful trajectories derived from the training environments improves success by 23.7%. That suggests the synthetic pipeline is not merely generating harder tests; it can also produce supervision that helps close part of the capability gap.

From a research perspective, the most informative numbers are not leaderboard rankings but the size of the drop under broader context and the amount recovered through fine-tuning. The first indicates strong sensitivity to environment complexity.
