9:T4be,The method is called C3. For each task instance, the authors generate multiple context perturbations that preserve the task semantics while changing the surrounding framing. They then compare the model’s output distribution under the original prompt and under the perturbed prompts. The magnitude of that distributional shift becomes the instance-level C3 score: smaller shifts indicate higher credibility. A notable strength is that C3 is not tied to one output format. The paper adapts the comparison procedure across multiple-choice, short-answer, long-form factuality, and code generation settings, using task-appropriate estimators of output-distribution change. The motivation section uses a logical-semantics framing: if a model stably supports a proposition, semantically irrelevant contextual changes should not move its induced answer distribution much. This is not a mechanistic theory of LLM internals, but it is a coherent behavioral rationale. Relative to self-consistency, C3 measures stability across prompts rather than repeated samples from one prompt. Relative to paraphrase consistency, it tries to avoid changing the question itself, reducing false inconsistency caused by altered semantics.a:T518,The evaluation spans 26 models and 6 benchmarks, with 16 models in the main analysis and an additional 10-model case study. The task mix includes reasoning, factuality, and code generation. The main empirical findings are threefold. At the instance level, smaller cross-contextual shifts are associated with higher correctness or factuality. At the model level, aggregated C3 scores produce a robustness profile that distinguishes models that are merely benchmark-competent from those that remain behaviorally stable under semantically neutral perturbations. At the method level, the signal appears robust across perturbation sources and across different estimators of distributional difference. The paper also argues that C3 remains informative when standard benchmarks are less discriminative because of saturation or contamination, since it does not rely directly on answer-key scoring. For a closer read, the key numbers to inspect are the correlation between C3 and accuracy/factuality on each task, whether the effect holds for both open and closed models, which error types show the largest shifts, and how C3 compares with self-consistency, paraphrase consistency, and calibration-style baselines.
