c:T5da,The method combines activation patching and attention analysis across 29 languages, five open-source model families, and six checkpoints. The experimental design is deliberately controlled. The authors build verb inventories from morphological resources such as UniMorph, then create zero-shot prompts with a common template like “Conjugation of the verb X in present tense: pronoun conjugation,” adapted to each language. This is less natural than free-form text, but it preserves token-position alignment between clean and corrupted prompts, which is important for causal patching. They distinguish two recovery metrics: a broader target-form recovery metric, which asks whether patching a head helps restore the context-appropriate target token, and a stricter minimal-pair inflection recovery metric, which isolates restoration of the inflectional contrast itself. For each language, they identify the attention heads causally implicated in agreement, then compare these head-level signatures across languages. The key claim is not just that overlap exists, but that overlap varies systematically with overt inflection. Languages with clear person/number marking show stronger circuit similarity. English behaves as a bridge case: its third-person singular cases align more with strongly inflecting languages, while its non-inflecting cases do not.
