1b:T806,The method has two main components. The first is DVBP. The authors estimate the input activation covariance of each layer in an online manner, avoiding the need to store all activations. They then eigendecompose the covariance matrix and use random matrix theory, specifically the Marchenko-Pastur law, to identify the noise-dominated part of the spectrum. Those spectral components are filtered out, producing a denoised covariance matrix. Variance scores are then computed from this denoised covariance and used to rank neurons. The key point is that the original VBP relies on diagonal variance terms, but those diagonals are themselves corrupted by global spectral noise; denoising at the spectrum level leads to more stable rankings, especially under aggressive pruning.

The second component is OB²C. Starting from the Optimal Brain Compression reconstruction objective, the authors incorporate the mean-shift bias compensation directly into the objective instead of treating it as a separate post-hoc fix. With this formulation, the layer-wise Hessian reduces exactly to the input activation covariance matrix. This matters because it turns a generic second-order reconstruction problem into one that can be solved from calibration activations alone. Given a pruning mask, the method then solves for the optimal update of both the remaining weights and the bias so that the output reconstruction error is minimized after low-variance neurons are removed. Compared with original VBP, the difference is not just a better bias correction; the remaining weights are also allowed to absorb information from the pruned features. Compared with standard OBC, the explicit bias term makes the formulation better suited to the case where low-variance features behave like near-constants.

Operationally, the full pipeline is: use denoised covariance for more reliable neuron ranking, then use the same covariance for second-order recovery. The design is fairly clean, and the reuse of one statistical object across both stages is the main technical appeal.1c:T635,The experiments cover DeiT, Swin, and ConvNeXt backbones across Tiny, Small, and Base scales. The main comparison is against the original VBP under different structured pruning ratios. The headline result is that the gain widens as pruning becomes more aggressive. At 50% structural reduction, Tiny models retain roughly 80% Top-1 accuracy, while Small and Base models stay above 90%; relative to VBP, the improvement reaches up to 7.33% on Swin-S and 29.46% on ConvNeXt-T. These numbers suggest that the method mainly helps in the high-compression regime, which is consistent with the paper’s diagnosis that VBP fails because of noisy ranking and insufficient compensation.

The most informative ablations are of two kinds. First, whether spectral denoising alone improves neuron ranking. The paper shows that denoised variance by itself already outperforms raw variance at high pruning ratios, indicating that covariance noise is not a minor issue but directly affects pruning decisions. Second, whether OB²C improves over mean-shift-only compensation. The results show that bias correction alone is insufficient; jointly updating the remaining weights reduces reconstruction error and improves final accuracy. If the paper includes layer-wise or module-wise analyses, the gains are likely more visible in MLP/channel pruning settings, where the low-variance-as-near-constant assumption is most plausible.

A clear limitation is that all experiments are on vision models rather than LLMs. So the transferable value is mostly in the method, not in the empirical conclusions themselves.1d:T53f,The method is a training-free loop: stochastic concept mining → attribution-based ranking → circuit intervention.
1) Stochastic concept mining: under the linear representation hypothesis, hidden states are treated as linear mixtures of concept directions. Instead of learning a dictionary/SAE, the authors sample pseudo-concept vectors. A key design choice is to sample/search in the per-head low-dimensional subspaces induced by MHSA projections (d_head), which empirically increases “concept hit” probability and reduces entanglement.
2) Vulnerability explanation via attribution: they propose a gradient-based attribution score that combines (a) whether an attention head focuses on text patches (focus) and (b) whether the sampled concept direction aligns with lexical semantics (direction). This yields a normalized score to identify TA-vulnerable concepts and the hosting heads/modules.
3) Mechanistic intervention: once harmful circuits are identified, they apply attention reweighting or ablation of selected heads/paths at test time to suppress lexical-dominated routes and recover visual-semantic behavior.
