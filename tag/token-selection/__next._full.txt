18:T83e,The method, AViTS, stands for Adaptive Spatiotemporal Token Selection. At resolution transition points, it assigns each latent token a joint importance score and uses that score to decide which tokens are upsampled earlier.

The first component is spatial importance. Instead of relying only on local latent structure or one-step statistics, AViTS explicitly uses latent-text attention. The intuition is straightforward: if a visual token consistently responds strongly to the text condition at low resolution, it is more likely to correspond to prompt-relevant objects, attributes, or edit targets, and should therefore receive high-resolution compute earlier. The paper suggests that this signal is aggregated rather than taken from a single layer or step, which makes it more stable.

The second component is temporal importance. The paper measures token-level step variance, namely how much a token representation changes across diffusion steps. Tokens with large variation are still evolving and likely need further refinement; tokens with small variation are closer to convergence and can be deferred. This complements attention-based importance because some regions may not be the strongest text-aligned areas but still require continued updates for editing fidelity or fine-detail recovery.

The third component is fusion and selective upsampling. AViTS combines the spatial and temporal signals into a unified spatiotemporal importance score, then performs importance-aware selective upsampling: high-importance tokens are promoted to high resolution earlier, while low-importance tokens are delayed. This differs from prior partial upsampling methods that mostly depend on local structure heuristics, edge cues, uncertainty proxies, or single-step latent statistics. AViTS jointly models conditional semantics and cross-step dynamics.

Importantly, the framework is presented as an add-on policy for dynamic-resolution DiTs rather than a backbone redesign. It is reported to work with FLUX, Qwen-Image-Edit, and FLUX.1-Kontext-dev, and to compose with distillation, quantization, and feature caching.19:T618,The main experimental story is the efficiency-quality trade-off rather than a single headline metric. The paper reports up to 6.34x acceleration on FLUX, nearly 9x FLOPs reduction on Qwen-Image-Edit and FLUX.1-Kontext-dev, and up to 14.76x acceleration when AViTS is combined with distillation. The claim is that these gains come with limited degradation in generation and editing quality.

Two empirical parts matter most. First, the paper provides the analysis that motivates the method. On both FLUX.1-dev and Qwen-Image-Edit, attention-based spatial importance and step-variance-based temporal importance show strongly long-tailed distributions, indicating that only a small subset of tokens are truly critical. The visualizations further suggest that attention highlights instruction-relevant semantic regions, while high step variance concentrates on areas requiring fine-grained modeling or modification.

Second, the method appears to include ablations comparing spatial-only, temporal-only, and fused scoring. Even without all table values in the prompt, the paper’s narrative makes clear that the joint score is more reliable than either signal alone. Another useful result is composability: AViTS works not only for text-to-image generation but also for image editing, and it stacks with distillation, quantization, and feature caching. That consistency is more interesting than the raw speedup number, because it suggests the method is exploiting a fairly general structure in token importance rather than overfitting to one model or one sampler.1a:T405,For a quick read, start with the introduction and the empirical motivation around token-importance heterogeneity.
