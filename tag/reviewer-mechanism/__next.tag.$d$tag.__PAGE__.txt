9:T5f8,The paper is a protocol study rather than a new model proposal. It keeps the actor family fixed and compares several inference-time designs on the same benchmark: a matched single-agent iterative baseline, a broadcast-style peer discussion protocol, and a hierarchical planner-executor-reviewer protocol, abbreviated as PER. The main methodological contribution is the evaluation decomposition. Instead of reporting only final accuracy, the authors use verifier-grounded analysis to measure whether the reviewer produced useful critique, whether the next candidate answer changed in response, and whether that change repaired the solution.

This leads to three central diagnostics: NeglectRate, which measures how often useful critique is ignored; CouplingRate, which measures whether the next answer actually changes after useful critique; and ReviewerGuidedRepairRate, which measures whether that change leads to a correct repair. This decomposition is the paper’s strongest idea. It turns a protocol comparison into a process analysis.

The authors also test several within-PER interventions. ACK-required is a compliance-style control that forces explicit acknowledgment of reviewer feedback. EMB injects reviewer guidance directly into the solver’s working context to reduce interface friction. A deeper-reflection variant is also explored. These interventions recover only part of the gap between PER and broadcast, suggesting that the issue is not simply reviewer quality but the structure of feedback routing itself.a:T57b,The experiments are run on 4,181 verifier-grounded Omni-MATH problems using matched gpt-oss-120b actors. The tiered benchmark is important because easy math benchmarks are too close to ceiling to separate protocols cleanly. The main findings are clear. First, on tiers 1 and 2, collaboration adds little, at most around 2 percentage points over the matched single-agent baseline. Second, from tier 4 onward, collaboration starts to matter, and gains reach roughly 10 to 20 points on tiers 6 to 9. Third, in that regime, broadcast outperforms PER in final accuracy.

The most informative numbers are the process metrics. PER’s reviewer has higher precision than broadcast’s reviewer, 0.861 versus 0.644. But when the reviewer gives critique that the evaluator later confirms as useful, PER changes its carried-forward candidate only 33.6 percent of the time, while broadcast does so 93.5 percent of the time. ReviewerGuidedRepairRate is 0.051 for PER versus 0.286 for broadcast. These numbers strongly support the paper’s central claim: reviewer precision alone does not explain protocol quality if critique uptake is weak.

The appendix-level evidence also matters. A reduced replication with Gemma 3 actors changes the overall ranking of protocols, but the precision-versus-uptake separation remains.
