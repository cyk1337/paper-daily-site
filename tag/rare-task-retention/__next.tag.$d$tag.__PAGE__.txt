a:T61b,The paper proceeds in three layers. First, it gives a phenomenological argument based on power-law scaling. The authors distinguish compute-optimal loss from asymptotic loss and argue that if the parameter exponent alpha exceeds the compute-optimal exponent gamma, then larger models should still achieve lower asymptotic loss. That suggests there are parts of the training distribution that smaller models fail to learn even with unlimited data under the same setup. The authors are careful not to present this as a strict theorem, but as motivation. Second, they build a controlled toy setting: a mixture of regression tasks with independently manipulated task frequency and task complexity, while ensuring that each individual task is learnable by the model family under study. This isolates mixture-learning failure from pure expressivity limits. In this setup, smaller models preferentially allocate neurons and updates to frequent or simple tasks, while rare and complex tasks fail to accumulate stable representations. Third, they validate the mechanism in real LLM pretraining using OLMo models from 4M to 4B parameters, pretrained on Dolma v1.7 with injected novel tasks at controlled frequencies. They then probe three linked factors: whether the tasks are learned, whether more task features are embedded in the representations, and whether gradient interference is reduced. A key claim is that larger models do not merely update more strongly on rare-task examples; they retain those updates better across subsequent batches dominated by common tasks.b:T55d,The experiments have two parts. In the toy mixture-of-regression setting, the authors vary task frequency and complexity and show that only larger models reliably learn low-frequency, high-complexity tasks. Smaller models remain near chance or substantially worse even after long training. More importantly, training-dynamics analysis shows that small models do move in the right direction when a rare-task batch appears, but those updates are quickly overwritten by later batches from common tasks. Larger models preserve more of that information until the next rare-task exposure, allowing accumulation across time. In the real-LLM section, the authors pretrain OLMo models ranging from 4M to 4B parameters on Dolma v1.7 with injected novel tasks at controlled frequencies. The result is qualitatively the same: only the larger OLMo models learn the infrequent and complex tasks. Additional analyses show that larger models encode more injected-task-related features in their internal representations and exhibit lower gradient interference, matching the toy-model story. The abstract truncates before giving full numeric details, so exact scores, token counts, and injected-task specifications need to be checked in Sections 4.2 to 4.4 and the appendix. Even so, the experimental design already makes the paper more informative than a generic “scaling helps” result.c:T492,This is a skim paper, but it deserves a careful skim.
