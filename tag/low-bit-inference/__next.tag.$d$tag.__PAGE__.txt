9:T87a,The paper proposes the Two Failure Modes Hypothesis and builds a mechanistic analysis pipeline around it.

1. Signal Degradation: the computational pattern remains largely intact. Key components still perform their intended role, but quantization error accumulates like noise and reduces information precision. In this regime, the correct answer is often still present in the output distribution, but with lower confidence.

2. Computation Collapse: quantization error becomes severe enough to break the functionality of critical components. Information is corrupted in early layers, so later layers operate on already damaged representations. The correct answer rank collapses, and outputs degenerate into frequent stop words or near-random guesses.

To validate this distinction, the paper goes beyond final accuracy and studies layer-wise information flow, causal pathways, component functionality, and representation-space structure. The main analysis is on Llama-3.1-8B, with validation on Qwen3-8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Gemma-2-9B-it. GPTQ is the primary PTQ baseline, with AWQ checked in the appendix. The task is factual recall using Pararel templates cast as next-token prediction, which gives fixed token positions and makes it easier to localize where knowledge signals are preserved or destroyed.

A strong design choice is the dataset partitioning. The authors split examples into a Robust Subset and a Failure Subset based on FP16 and 4-bit behavior, so they can isolate quantization-induced failures from examples the base model never knew. They then compare internal behavior across 4-bit and 2-bit on these subsets and show that 4-bit mostly preserves structure with reduced precision, while 2-bit causes structural breakdown.

The paper also includes mechanism-aware interventions. The conclusion is precise: signal degradation can be partially repaired with targeted, training-free methods, but computation collapse cannot be fixed by low-rank compensation or similar post hoc patches. Once the functional structure is broken, reconstruction through fine-tuning is required. That is a more useful statement than simply saying 2-bit is harder.a:T772,The experimental story is clean. The paper compares FP16, 8-bit, 4-bit, 3-bit, and 2-bit behavior on factual recall, then dissects the difference between 4-bit and 2-bit failure modes. The main model is Llama-3.1-8B, with Qwen3-8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Gemma-2-9B-it used for generalization. GPTQ is the main quantizer, and AWQ is used to test whether the conclusions depend on the quantization algorithm.

Two results stand out. First, there is a clear performance cliff: the move from FP16 to 4-bit causes gradual degradation and the model remains usable, while 2-bit drives accuracy essentially to zero. The paper argues this is not just lower precision, but a different failure regime. Second, at the output-distribution level, 4-bit mostly causes answer rank drop: the correct token often remains near the top, for example within the top few candidates. In contrast, 2-bit causes answer rank collapse: the correct answer falls to ranks in the thousands, and the model often emits high-frequency tokens such as “the” or “.”, indicating that the knowledge signal has already been destroyed.

The appendix reportedly shows similar trends on broader tasks such as MMLU and GSM8K, so the phenomenon is not limited to Pararel. The intervention experiments are especially useful: targeted training-free repair helps in the signal-degradation regime, but even advanced low-rank compensation fails in the computation-collapse regime. That gives a practical criterion for when patching is worth trying and when structural retraining is necessary.

For a pretraining-oriented reader, the most informative experiments are not the benchmark scores themselves, but the layer-wise signal tracing, representation-geometry analysis, and component-function tests, and how these diagnostics change sharply with bit width.
