The authors set the classification prior's max_classes to two and keep the rest of the TabICLv2 recipe unchanged, including optimizer settings, learning rate, weight decay, dropout, sequence-length distribution, and the regression prior configuration. The proposed explanation is that classification is implemented as an in-context comparison operation. Labels from the training rows are encoded and injected into the context, and predictions are obtained by comparing query-row representations against class-conditioned representations or prototypes derived from the context. Under a shared label encoder, the model is not tied to a fixed class-specific embedding table, so binary match/mismatch supervision can train an operator that accepts more than two candidate classes at inference. The paper also separates three notions that are often conflated: the class count sampled by the prior, the per-forward comparison cap, and the number of classes the system can express. The reported failure modes are large K, severe class imbalance, and ordinal labels.1a:T808,The method is built under a strict black-box assumption. The attacker does not access weights or internal logits, and instead relies on crafted inputs and observable outputs. The main finding is that individual signals are weak, but a combination of multiple weak signals can support useful dataset inference.

The pipeline has three parts. First, the paper formalizes two audit settings: external audits, where a data owner suspects unauthorized use of a dataset and only has the suspect dataset plus limited auxiliary data; and internal audits, which are closer to release-time risk assessment. Second, it designs TFM-specific signals around the in-context prediction mechanism. Because TFM outputs depend on the provided context dataset, the auditor can manipulate the support set, subsample the suspect dataset, perturb labels or columns, alter context-query matching, and then observe changes in confidence, loss-like quantities, ranking behavior, or output stability. A central point in the paper is that each signal alone tends to have limited separation power, partly because pre-training pipelines weaken direct evidence through augmentation and task reformulation. Third, the paper aggregates these weak observables into a dataset-level inference model: for each candidate dataset, it extracts a feature vector of black-box signals and trains a meta-classifier to predict whether the dataset was in the pre-training corpus.

A useful conceptual contribution is the distinction between TFM auditing and prior dataset inference work in LLMs or self-supervised vision. In TFMs, the context dataset actively participates in prediction, so the auditor can probe training-related behavior by constructing different context-query configurations. This is not a direct reuse of existing membership inference methods; it exploits the conditional structure of in-context learning. Another important result is interpretive rather than algorithmic: detectability is tied much more strongly to the nature of the pre-training corpus than to raw parameter count.1b:T7bc,The experiments are organized around understanding detectability rather than just maximizing attack accuracy.

First, individual signals are generally weak. The paper evaluates a broad set of candidate black-box observables and shows that, in isolation, they usually provide only limited evidence about whether a dataset was used in pre-training. This matters because it frames the attack as an accumulation of subtle biases rather than exploitation of a single obvious vulnerability.

Second, combining signals substantially improves performance. The paper reports that multi-signal inference can accurately identify training datasets for some strong contemporary TFMs, which is the main practical result.

Third, the factor analysis is especially informative. The dominant driver of detectability is pre-training data composition. Real datasets and real-derived synthetic datasets leave stronger dataset-level traces, whereas models trained on prior-synthetic data leak much less, except when the number of pre-training datasets is very small. By contrast, increasing model size alone has little effect on detectability. This is an important correction to the naive intuition that larger models are automatically more vulnerable.

Fourth, the paper studies additional factors on both the data and model sides, including suspect dataset properties, context construction choices, and differences across TFM training setups. The consistent message is that attack success depends on whether context manipulation can reliably expose training-related biases.
