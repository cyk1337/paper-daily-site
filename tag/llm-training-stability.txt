19:T424,The paper explains two tightly coupled questions in Muon: why momentum is empirically indispensable, and why it must be applied before orthogonalization rather than after it. Prior analyses typically isolated the polar update and removed momentum, or kept momentum only in convergence proofs that did not explain its practical benefit. That leaves a gap between theory and LLM pretraining practice, where standard Muon clearly outperforms both a no-momentum variant and an orthogonalize-first variant. The paper argues that the key role of momentum in Muon is not generic acceleration. It is spectral filtering on the matrix gradient stream. Momentum suppresses temporally incoherent perturbations while preserving persistent low-rank signal, thereby enlarging the singular-value gap and stabilizing the leading singular subspaces. Orthogonalization should then act on this denoised matrix. If polar normalization is applied first, it destroys the amplitude information that separates signal from noise, and later momentum cannot recover that lost distinction.1a:T78d,The technical setup combines a structured gradient model with a comparison of three update pipelines. Gradients are modeled as a coherent low-rank signal plus a bounded-variance, mean-zero perturbation that is orthogonal to the signal. This is a deliberate abstraction: it captures the idea that some directions persist across steps and should accumulate, while other components behave like high-frequency noise.

The paper studies three pipelines. Pre-polar is standard Muon: first form the momentum buffer M_t = beta M_{t-1} + (1-beta) G_t, then apply the polar factor O(M_t). Post-polar is the Orthogonal-SGDM style variant: first orthogonalize each step O(G_t), then apply momentum. Polar-only removes momentum entirely. The first main result is that momentum acts as a spectral low-pass filter on the raw gradient stream. Because the coherent signal persists over time, it survives temporal averaging; the zero-mean perturbation is attenuated. This enlarges the gap between the r-th and (r+1)-th singular values and improves alignment of the top-r singular subspaces of M_t with those of the underlying signal. The second main result is about ordering. The polar factor preserves singular directions but replaces singular values by ones. If it is applied directly to a noisy gradient, it erases the magnitude structure that distinguishes signal from perturbation. Momentum applied afterward can only smooth already distorted directions; it cannot recreate the lost spectral separation. Hence pre-polar dominates post-polar for structural reasons, not as an implementation accident.

A useful aspect of the analysis is that it does not stop at the vague statement “momentum reduces noise.” It specifies what kind of noise reduction matters for Muon: creating a matrix whose spectrum is sufficiently separated so that the subsequent orthogonalization acts on stable leading subspaces. That is the optimizer-specific mechanism.1b:T615,The experimental goal is to validate the spectral-filtering story and the ordering effect, rather than to introduce a new benchmark result. The paper first compares pre-polar, post-polar, and polar-only in pretraining. The qualitative result is clear from the introduction: standard pre-polar Muon consistently outperforms both alternatives throughout training, showing that momentum is essential and that the order cannot be swapped.

The more informative experiments are the spectral diagnostics. The theory predicts that the momentum buffer should exhibit a larger top-r versus tail singular-value separation than the raw gradient, and that the leading singular subspaces should align better with the coherent signal. It also predicts that post-polar loses this advantage because orthogonalizing G_t first flattens all singular values to one. The full paper likely includes plots of spectral gap versus momentum strength or effective window size, subspace alignment metrics, and update-direction comparisons under controlled signal-plus-noise settings. Those are the figures to inspect carefully, because they connect the theorems to observable training behavior.

The provided text does not include complete numerical tables, model sizes, token counts, or exact performance deltas, so those should be verified directly in the experimental section and appendix. In practice, the most important numbers are not just end-task loss differences, but whether the measured spectral quantities track the theory across beta values and across the three pipelines.1c:T524,Start with the introduction and the figure comparing the three pipelines. That establishes the empirical fact to be explained: pre-polar beats post-polar and polar-only. Then read Theorem 1 and its corollary first. The key question there is what momentum changes in the matrix spectrum: focus on spectral gap growth and stability of the top-r singular subspaces. After that, read Theorems 2 and 3 to understand why the order matters. The central conceptual point is simple but easy to miss: the polar factor keeps singular vectors but discards singular-value magnitudes, so applying it too early destroys the very information needed to separate signal from noise.

Three details are worth deeper inspection. First, how realistic is the bounded-variance mean-zero orthogonal perturbation model for actual pretraining gradients? Second, how strong is the signal persistence assumption, and can it be related to measurable temporal correlation in real training runs? Third, how does approximate orthogonalization via Newton–Schulz interact with the theory—does the mechanism survive implementation error?
