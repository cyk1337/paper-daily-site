18:T563,The benchmark is built around controlled evidence requirements. For counting, the authors define three overlap regimes across views: complete overlap, subset overlap, and complementary views. The first two can often be solved by simple per-view heuristics such as taking the maximum count. Complementary views cannot: the model must determine which detections correspond to the same physical object and which are unique, so cross-view deduplication becomes necessary.

For cross-view consistency, the benchmark avoids a naive “spot the difference” setup. Instead, it introduces localized edits either in regions visible from both viewpoints or in regions visible from only one viewpoint. Only the former creates a true contradiction. This tests whether the model reasons about visibility and shared evidence, rather than merely detecting image-level differences.

A third task, multi-view spatial reasoning, asks models to infer physical proximity relations by combining evidence across viewpoints. The dataset is built from real-world multi-view captures from DL3DV-10K and manually verified. The paper also adds diagnostic analyses: attribution to simple aggregation strategies, sensitivity to image order, no-image bias checks, explicit reasoning prompts, and scaling with additional views. These analyses make the benchmark useful for failure diagnosis, not just ranking.19:T57e,The evaluation covers 12 open-weight and proprietary VLMs under an open-ended protocol. The clearest result appears in counting. Accuracy is relatively moderate on complete overlap, around 45.5%–81.5%, and on subset overlap, around 26.7%–76.7%. But on complementary views, where true cross-view integration is required, performance drops sharply to 4.5%–50.0%. This gap strongly suggests that many models do not reliably establish object correspondence across views.

The consistency task is even more revealing. Accuracy stays around 50%, essentially chance level, and models respond similarly to edits in shared regions and view-specific regions. That means they are often detecting differences without understanding whether those differences are compatible with two views of the same scene.

The auxiliary analyses add useful detail. A substantial fraction of counting outputs can be explained by simple per-view aggregation rules, indicating shortcut behavior. Sensitivity to image order suggests unstable multi-image fusion. Performance under reduced or removed visual input reveals language prior bias. Explicit reasoning prompts help only marginally, which implies the bottleneck is not merely missing chain-of-thought at decoding time; it is more likely a representational failure to bind observations into a coherent scene model.
