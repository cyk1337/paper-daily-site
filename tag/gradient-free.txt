19:T6a0,The method edits a single weight matrix W at a time, either the LM head or the down-projection in an MLP block. The model performs one forward pass on forget data and one on retain data, collects the input activations to W, and stacks them as key matrices. Keys are extracted only at positions where the next token should be forgotten or preserved, so the optimization is token-level rather than sample-level.

The objective is a ridge-regularized least-squares problem over an additive update ΔW. One term pushes the edited matrix toward a predefined forgetting target on forget keys. Another term constrains outputs on retain keys to stay close to the original model. Because the objective is quadratic and convex in ΔW, the optimum has a closed-form solution. This is the main departure from methods such as gradient ascent, RKLD, NPO, or preference-style unlearning, which search for a compromise through SGD.

The paper uses two target designs. Token suppression directly lowers the model’s tendency to emit the memorized answer token or related logits. Representation corruption instead perturbs intermediate representations so downstream layers cannot easily reconstruct the deleted content. The authors also introduce a logit-lens-based layer selection strategy: they inspect which intermediate layers already make the target content linearly decodable through the LM head, then edit only those high-impact layers. This avoids broad model changes. A notable extra is exact influence auditing. Because the update is analytic, the contribution of an individual deletion request to the final parameter edit can be decomposed exactly, which is much harder with iterative gradient methods.1a:T66d,The paper evaluates forgetting-utility trade-offs on five benchmarks and uses gold retraining as a reference point. Three findings matter most. First, efficiency: GROM needs only forward-pass statistics for forget and retain sets plus a closed-form solve per edited layer, leading to second-level execution and up to two orders of magnitude speedup over gradient-based baselines. Second, effectiveness: on Pareto trade-offs, it is typically better than or competitive with GA, GradDiff, RMU, RKLD, NPO, and SimNPO, meaning stronger forgetting at matched utility or better utility at matched forgetting. Third, robustness: under the recent low-bit quantization recovery attack, a strong gradient-based baseline regains a substantial amount of supposedly erased content, while GROM remains close to the retrained model in forgetfulness.

The most informative ablations are not just average scores but the structure of the edit: which layers are edited, whether the LM head or MLP down-projection is modified, token suppression versus representation corruption, the ridge coefficient, the size of the retain set, and interference across multiple deletion requests. The broader experimental message is that local linear edits can handle a meaningful fraction of memory-style unlearning when layer selection is accurate and retain constraints are strong, but distributed multi-layer knowledge remains a harder case. The provided full-text excerpt does not include all numeric tables, so exact benchmark-by-benchmark scores cannot be reproduced here, but the speedup scale, Pareto behavior, and quantization robustness are the key empirical takeaways.1b:T7be,The method learns a vector δ over the vocabulary and adds it to the base model logits at every step, yielding πδ(yt|x,y<t)=softmax(l(x,y<t)+δ). Because δ is context-independent, it can only induce a global reweighting of token preferences; it cannot implement prefix-specific decisions. The theory starts from a KL-regularized RL objective with terminal reward r(x,y1:T). If arbitrary policies are allowed, the optimum is an exponential tilt of the base policy, equivalent to a prefix-dependent logit correction derived from soft value or advantage terms. The proposed fixed-bias policy is then treated as a restricted approximation to that optimum. The paper derives a reward-gap bound showing that the approximation error depends on how much the optimal correction varies across prefixes. In plain terms, the method should work when the desired correction is mostly a stable token-level preference, such as favoring EOS, concise endings, or formatting tokens, and should fail when the correction must depend strongly on local context.

On the estimation side, the paper derives a closed-form IPS-style estimator that uses black-box rollouts, sequence-level rewards, and token probabilities. No backpropagation through the model is needed. Operationally, the estimator compresses systematic token-level differences between high-reward and low-reward trajectories into one shared bias vector. The paper also compares full-vocabulary bias against more restricted parameterizations and argues that full-vocabulary control is more robust, because many useful behavior changes require coordinated reordering across groups of tokens rather than a few manually selected ones. Relative to prompt optimization, this consumes no context and acts directly on the output distribution. Relative to PEFT or fine-tuning, it is almost non-invasive. Relative to generic black-box RL, the policy class is much smaller, which improves stability and sample efficiency but also lowers the ceiling.1c:T556,The experiments are organized around three questions. First, can a fixed logit-bias vector produce measurable gains at all? The paper reports improvements over the base model on math and reasoning benchmarks while using far fewer trainable parameters and much less compute than conventional fine-tuning. Second, where does the method help most? The empirical pattern is that it works better when the target behavior has a strong global token-bias component, such as verbosity control, formatting, stopping behavior, or small distribution shifts with recurring answer patterns. It helps less on tasks that require fine-grained conditional decisions. Third, do the empirical results match the theory? The ablations indicate that full-vocabulary bias is better than restricted token subsets, and that KL regularization plus the IPS estimator improves training stability. The setup appears to rely mainly on RLVR-style rewards, where prompts have verifiable answers and rewards are computed from correctness or related rule-based checks.
