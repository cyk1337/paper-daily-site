18:T7a2,PRISM introduces model preference into the construction of the target direction. The target behavior is defined through paired anchors: for each prompt q, there is a target-positive response y_q^p and a target-negative response y_q^n. A standard influence-based method would typically average the gradient differences g(q, y_q^p) - g(q, y_q^n) across target pairs and then score each candidate SFT example z by how well its gradient aligns with that averaged direction.

The paper argues that this uniform aggregation is too crude. If the current model already assigns relatively high probability to some positive anchors, those target pairs correspond to nearby behavioral directions that can be strengthened efficiently with a small amount of data. Distant target pairs may still describe the desired behavior semantically, but they are weaker guides for local data selection under a fixed budget.

Concretely, PRISM computes a preference score π_q for each target pair, defined from the model’s normalized probability of the positive anchor relative to the negative anchor. It then uses π_q to weight the gradient difference for that pair, producing a preference-aware target direction. Candidate training examples are scored by the similarity between their influence signal and this weighted target direction. High-scoring examples are those expected to move the model in a direction that increases preference for the desired behavior.

The paper also provides a first-order local analysis: near the current parameter point, PRISM approximates the direction that most efficiently increases target preference per unit data budget. Uniform averaging, by mixing high-preference and low-preference targets, can point away from the behavior that the current model can most readily amplify. A practical advantage is that the same ranking machinery supports both retention of useful data for efficient fine-tuning and removal of harmful data for repair.19:T589,The experiments cover two settings. The first is capability-oriented data selection, where the target behavior corresponds to knowledge ability, mathematical reasoning, and safety guardrails. The second is safety-critical SFT repair, where the goal is to remove a limited amount of harmful training data to reduce undesirable behavior. The main empirical claim is consistent across both settings: under the same keep budget or removal budget, PRISM selects data that yields better target-behavior improvement or more effective safety repair.

The paper emphasizes two patterns. First, the gains hold across multiple model families and scales, suggesting that the effect is not tied to one specific backbone. Second, the ablations indicate that the main benefit comes from preference-aware weighting of target examples rather than from replacing the influence-scoring machinery itself. In other words, the key idea is not a more elaborate influence estimator, but a better target representation that depends on the current model state.
