5:T7c6,This is a large empirical analysis rather than a new model. The evaluation is organized as a progression of bias complexity: ImageNet for overall recognition, CelebA for a single spurious attribute, and UrbanCars for two simultaneous spurious attributes. The paper formalizes this through group structure induced by labels and spurious attributes, with the number of groups growing combinatorially as more attributes are involved. That framing matters because it explains why multi-attribute bias is qualitatively harder, not just a slightly noisier benchmark.

The analysis decomposes several design factors: model size, training data size, whether the training data is curated or unfiltered, patch size, and architecture family. A useful part of the paper is that it does not rely only on global correlations. It also performs controlled comparisons where one factor changes while others are kept as comparable as possible within a family. That reduces the risk of attributing gains from better data or newer architectures to scale alone.

Three findings stand out. First, model scaling remains effective for standard recognition but loses predictive power for robustness under complex bias. Second, data properties are more stable: larger and better curated datasets retain meaningful association with worst-group performance even in the harder settings. Third, input granularity matters: coarser visual patches hurt worst-group accuracy, suggesting that fine-grained visual tokens help the model separate causal object evidence from background shortcuts.

The mechanism proposed by the paper is sensible. Contrastive VLM training benefits from frequent image-text co-occurrences, which are often shortcuts. More capacity helps fit those regularities, but does not force the model to rely on causally relevant features. Data curation changes the shortcut distribution directly, and finer patching preserves local evidence that may be necessary to resist background or co-occurrence bias.6:T5d8,The experimental coverage is the backbone of the paper: 194 public VLMs from 16 families, parameter counts from 63M to 3.6B, and 24 training data sources ranging from 13M to 12.8B samples. Evaluation uses ImageNet, CelebA, and UrbanCars, corresponding to overall performance, single-attribute bias, and multi-attribute bias.

The most important numbers are straightforward. The Spearman correlation between model scale and performance drops from 0.68 on ImageNet to 0.48 on CelebA and to 0.05 on UrbanCars. That is the clearest quantitative evidence for what the authors call bias complexity sensitivity. In controlled comparisons where model size is the main varying factor, scaling improves ImageNet accuracy by about +2.6% on average, but reduces UrbanCars worst-group accuracy by -4.2%. So larger models can become better at exploiting shortcuts rather than better at avoiding them. Data-related factors are more stable: training data size still shows a correlation of 0.41 with robustness on the multi-attribute benchmark, and curated datasets outperform size-matched unfiltered alternatives by up to 25% in worst-group accuracy. Patch granularity also has a measurable effect: moving from patch size 16 to 32 lowers UrbanCars WGA by 6.8% in controlled comparisons.

The main experimental lesson is not which single model wins, but which design axes keep their effect as the benchmark becomes more bias-sensitive. First, it builds a controlled matrix of five LVR variants spanning different latent-supervision designs, including reconstruction-heavy training, input-noise regularization, and progressively scaffolded supervision. The backbone, data source, and training budget are held fixed so that differences can be attributed to the latent objective rather than unrelated recipe changes. Second, the authors introduce PRISM, a pair of inference-time diagnostics. The first is a corruption test: at each generation step, latent positions are zeroed, perturbed with Gaussian noise, or replaced with latents from another sample. If the latent channel is truly carrying reasoning-relevant information, these interventions should hurt substantially. Instead, all variants move by at most four points, and one variant even improves when its latents are zeroed. The second is a two-position linear probe. The probe asks whether the answer is decodable from the answer-decoding hidden state and from the autoregressive feedback variable that gets re-injected as the latent. The answer is decodable from the former but not the latter. The paper interprets this through an Information Bottleneck lens: the dominant cross-entropy term pushes the network to route answer-relevant computation wherever it most directly helps prediction, while cosine/MSE reconstruction does not guarantee that the supervised latent preserves answer-relevant information.
