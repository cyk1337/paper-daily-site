9:T442,The paper has two main components. First, it develops a framework separating functional equivalence, representational equivalence, representational alignment, and functional alignment. This distinction matters because two representations can support similar tasks without being easy to map into one another, and a learned adapter can appear to work while memorizing paired data rather than learning a general cross-modal translation. In typical generative VLMs, a visual adapter maps vision features into the language-model embedding space; in retrieval models, both modalities are projected into a shared space. The paper investigates whether this mapping becomes easier with scale. Its answer is negative for existing representations: vision and language representations are linearly unalignable, and the source of this gap is tied to the data rather than just model size or adapter capacity. Second, it connects this misalignment to a controlled generalization failure: a VLM may fail on a concept known to both unimodal backbones when that concept is absent from paired finetuning data.a:T448,The experiments appear to follow three lines. The first measures representational alignability between existing vision and language models, focusing on whether a linear map can reliably align their representations. The second induces a generalization failure by selecting cases where the vision and language backbones each contain the relevant concept, but the paired multimodal training data does not provide the necessary bridge. The third relates alignment quality to known VLM failures, including visual analogies, fine-grained image-text matching, simple geometric relations, and atypical object counting. The qualitative result is that more representationally aligned backbone pairs lead to fewer VLM failures, while misaligned models are more likely to fall back on language priors, such as assuming a chicken has the typical number of legs despite contradictory visual evidence. Starting from static 3D scenes and camera trajectories, the system renders RGB observation sequences. During data construction, it also has access to privileged information such as camera poses, scene geometry, and object identities. These are never exposed to the model at inference time, but they are used to compile supervision.

The supervision is organized into three connected capabilities within the same episode. First, self-motion tracking asks the model to infer how the observer moved between observations. The important point is that motion is not treated as an isolated target; it is the mechanism that should drive state updates. Second, persistent object mapping requires the model to maintain object locations and relations as the viewpoint changes, including when objects become occluded or leave the field of view. Third, operations over state ask downstream spatial questions on top of the updated internal state.

A second important design choice is motif-driven trajectory rendering. Instead of random camera paths, the authors define trajectory motifs that control how visibility, evidence, and viewpoint changes unfold over time. This lets them systematically generate episodes that require memory, re-localization, reference-frame handling, and state maintenance. The resulting questions are grouped into shared episodes so that motion estimation, state update, and reasoning are learned as connected parts of one experience rather than as unrelated instruction examples.c:T569,The paper builds Uruqi-600k and trains URUQI Syn-8B.
