First, policy decomposition. For N rewards, PRISM does not train one policy to maximize all of them jointly. The design choice is deliberate: desirable behavior is often reward-specific, while failure modes can often be pooled under a shared “do not do this” branch.

Second, efficient parameter sharing. PRISM does not instantiate N+1 separate full models. It uses one shared LM and conditions different sub-policies with different prefix tokens. This keeps training and serving practical while preserving a shared representation backbone.

Third, policy composition. At rollout and inference time, PRISM combines sub-policies by taking a weighted sum of their logits, producing a mixture policy. The merge weights become an explicit preference-control interface. Unlike reward weights, these coefficients act on already-optimized policy distributions rather than on raw reward scales, which is the authors’ main argument for improved robustness and interpretability.

Optimization is built on GRPO. Each reward mainly updates its corresponding positive branch, while the negative branch absorbs failure signals across rewards. Relative to linear scalarization, adaptive reward weighting, or constrained/Lagrangian formulations, the novelty is not in the policy-gradient estimator but in moving composition from reward space to policy space. On diagnosis, the authors do more than compare layer-wise accuracies. They analyze residual-stream shifts, contribution norms to logits, and token prediction trajectories across depth. This is used to identify tokens whose semantics stabilize in intermediate layers and tokens whose predictions are noticeably redirected in the final layers. The paper interprets this as late-stage perturbation and frames it as a planning–pragmatics tradeoff: the model internally forms a reasoning-oriented token preference, but the final layers may redirect it toward generic, safe, polite, or alignment-preferred continuations. That framing is more precise than simply saying intermediate layers can be better, because it localizes the issue to a near-final readout problem rather than a broad claim that shallow layers should replace deep ones.

The proposed method, Confident Decoding, keeps the full forward pass intact. It is not an early-exit method. At each generation step, the model still computes all layers, but the decoder additionally reads logits from a small near-final layer window. Entropy is used as a confidence proxy. Starting from the final layer and moving backward conservatively, the algorithm selects the first local entropy trough—intuitively, the point where the distribution has already become sharp, but before a possible late-layer perturbation has redirected it. The selected layer’s logits are then used for greedy decoding or downstream sampling.

A key design choice is that the method does not simply pick the globally lowest-entropy layer. That would often favor layers that are too early: sharp but semantically immature. Nor does it use a fixed intermediate layer, because the best readout depth varies across tokens, tasks, and contexts. The paper formalizes layer selection as an optimal stopping problem. Under bounded projection noise and dominant late-stage perturbation, the preferred policy is to stop at a high-confidence point before the perturbation, not to mechanically decode from the final layer. The theory is stylized rather than a full model of transformer dynamics, but it gives a coherent rationale for the “first local trough” rule.

Relative to prior work, the differences are clear. It is not contrastive decoding that subtracts or contrasts layers while keeping the final layer as the semantic anchor. It is not an efficiency-oriented early-exit method. And it is dynamic at the token and step level, rather than assigning one static exit layer to the whole model.
