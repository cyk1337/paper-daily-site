18:T48a,CineEvolve models multi-shot creation as iterative trajectories: initial narrative/shot planning and storyboard generation → defect critique → targeted revision. A single vision-language policy is prompted to act as both Director (create/revise) and Critic (diagnose/score), enabling the critic to evaluate against the director’s own narrative intent.

Training:
- Supervised warm-up on curated trajectories to learn planning, critique language, and revision actions.
- Alternating RL: the Critic is optimized on dynamic storyboard rollouts with composite critic rewards; the Director is optimized for both initial creation and targeted revision using the updated Critic’s self-evaluative scores plus narrative coherence and tool-executability rewards.

E-GRPO targets co-evolution advantage inflation via:
- Historical anchoring: calibrating updates against historical policies/distributions.
- Revision safeguarding: preventing destructive or reward-hacking revisions during refinement.

The system relies on external visual generation tools to render storyboard frames from tool calls; the policy focuses on structured plans and revision instructions.19:T510,Self-Credit is built on group-based RL, using a GRPO-like setup. For each query, the policy samples a group of trajectories, each receives a binary outcome reward, and a trajectory advantage is computed. Instead of applying this advantage uniformly to all tokens, Self-Credit introduces three components.

First, during rollouts, the policy generates summaries with citations to earlier turns that contain critical evidence. The summaries compact the long interaction history, while the citations become self-generated credit signals at the turn level.

Second, intra-group rescaling refines these credits. For the same prompt, the method compares cited turns across successful and failed rollouts. Turns that are repeatedly cited in successful trajectories and less common in failed ones receive stronger credit. Turns that also appear frequently in failed trajectories are downweighted or stripped of credit. This makes the signal less dependent on trusting the model’s citations in isolation.

Third, advantage reweighting applies the rescaled credit to the RL loss. Tokens inside highly credited turns receive stronger advantages, while unrelated turns receive less reinforcement. The method remains end-to-end and avoids training an additional critic or collecting dense human supervision.1a:T5b3,The approach is intentionally end-to-end and depends on three coupled steps:
1) Axis decomposition and per-axis scaling fits (Sec. 4): formalize the training corpus as a tensor over tasks T, environments E, and trajectories per (t,e) cell. Define cardinalities DT=|T|, DE=|E|, and a trajectory axis Dτ capturing sampling density/volume, plus a reward-density control knob. Fit independent power-law exponents αT, αE, ατ by varying one axis while controlling others.
2) Pricing synthesis with a common ruler (Sec. 5): use the real-data scaling laws to convert synthetic data into “real-equivalent” units. Introduce κ∈[0,1] as an axis-specific discount (effectiveness of synthetic vs real) and identify a critical synthetic-to-real ratio r* where performance degradation/collapse begins. Reported thresholds are strongly ordered: rE*≈9.4, rT*≈3.4, rτ*≈1.4, with a mechanistic interpretation tied to how strongly each axis is grounded by external truth.
3) Compute-optimal allocation (Sec. 6): solve a constrained optimization over model size N and axis budgets (DE,DT,Dτ) under total compute C, incorporating synthesis costs/discounts. Derive closed-form scaling prescriptions: DE*∝C^0.34, DT*∝C^0.27, Dτ*∝C^0.13, N*∝C^0.26. Further refine the environment axis into world diversity vs environment-structure diversity, yielding αW>αV≈αT>ατ, which maps cleanly to engineering choices (build new worlds vs expand API/structure).1b:T4a0,Coverage is designed to test robustness across models, algorithms, and domains rather than a single benchmark:
- Models/algorithms: Qwen3-4B/8B/14B; both GRPO and PPO.
- Domains: five domains including math+code, SWE, web, and UI/tool-use.
- Total: 21 experimental settings (as stated).
Key quantitative takeaways:
1) A direct failure case for scalar “token” scaling: two Qwen3-8B runs with identical total tokens and FLOPs but different (task, environment, trajectory) allocations differ by 14 percentage points in success rate.
2) Stable exponent ordering across settings: αE>αT>ατ; with environment split, αW>αV≈αT>ατ.
3) Synthetic mixture limits: collapse thresholds rE*≈9.4, rT*≈3.4, rτ*≈1.4, implying trajectory-heavy synthetic rollouts are the most fragile.
4) The compute-optimal allocation derived from fitted laws is validated at a held-out compute scale, supporting the claim that the fitted exponents are predictive for budget planning.

The first component is session-aware trajectory-level advantage estimation. In the standard broadcasting setup, one computes a GRPO-style normalized trajectory advantage from outcome rewards within a rollout group, then assigns that same advantage to all tokens across all sessions. Mosaic argues that this ignores session efficiency. It therefore refines the trajectory-level signal using the relative ranking of session counts within trajectories that share the same outcome. Intuitively, if two trajectories both succeed, the one using fewer sessions should receive a larger advantage. If both fail, however, extra sessions should not be penalized in the same way because failed samples still serve exploration. The paper implements this through a bounded modulation term based on session-count ranking inside homogeneous-reward groups. Two design constraints are central: the modulation must not overturn the primary ordering induced by outcome reward, and its magnitude must remain controlled so that session preference does not dominate task success. The point is not to replace outcome supervision, but to increase advantage resolution within reward-tied groups.

The second component is session-level advantage shaping. Each session is assigned an immediate shaping signal based on its marginal contribution to the final outcome. Concretely, the method estimates a session-level marginal outcome gain, normalizes it with group-relative statistics separately for each session type, and combines this shaping term with the trajectory-level advantage. The type-conditioned normalization is important because different session types have different gain distributions. A compaction session, a main-agent session, and a forked subagent session differ in length, function, and contribution scale. Pooling them into one normalization scheme would distort the signal.

Together, these two components form a hierarchical objective: the trajectory-level term provides a global, long-horizon, outcome-oriented signal, while the session-level shaping term captures local marginal effects. In the propose stage, an LLM-based judge compares successful and failed trajectories from the same GRPO group and selects a segment in a successful trajectory that may have caused the divergence in outcome. The judge is used only for localization, not for direct reward assignment. This is an important design choice: it leverages model-based pattern recognition without trusting model judgment as the final training signal.

In the verify stage, the method restores the environment states immediately before and after the proposed segment, then samples fresh continuations from the current policy from both boundary states. The difference in empirical terminal success rates estimates the segment’s advantage. Intuitively, if the post-segment state is much easier for the current policy to complete successfully than the pre-segment state, then the segment likely contributed real causal progress.

In the credit stage, only positively verified segment advantages are added to the original GRPO advantages, and only for policy tokens inside the proposed segment. So ProVer does not replace GRPO. It augments GRPO with sparse, localized, outcome-grounded bonus credit.

The upper-level variables are the LLM policy parameters θ, and the lower-level variables are the retriever parameters η. The goal is not simple alternating optimization. Instead, the lower level seeks a retriever that best supports the current policy’s answer generation, and the upper level then updates the policy using rollouts produced with that retriever.

Several implementation choices are central. First, the retriever is adapted only through a query-side LoRA module, rather than full retriever finetuning. This keeps memory and compute manageable. Second, retriever learning combines two signals: trajectory-level RL credit at retrieval turns, and a RAG-style likelihood term that scores whether retrieved evidence supports the target answer. The RL signal captures whether retrieval helped the task succeed, while the likelihood term stabilizes training by rewarding semantic support for the answer. The authors show that a common simplification—treating the lower-level optimal value as nearly constant with respect to θ—does not hold here.
