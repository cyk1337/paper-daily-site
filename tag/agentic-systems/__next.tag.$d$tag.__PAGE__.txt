9:T7ca,The setup is built around Qwen3-8B as a hybrid model. The same base model can be decoded in think mode, where it generates reasoning before deciding, or in no-think mode, where it emits a direct action token. This allows a relatively controlled comparison between a reasoning policy and a direct classifier without changing the model family. The task is binary intervention timing: given a prefix of a multi-party conversation, decide whether an assistant should speak now or remain silent. The dataset contains about 16,000 synthetic conversations expanded into roughly 173,000 token-level decision points, with only about 13% positive intervention labels, so the class imbalance is severe. The paper compares several training routes. One is direct decision-token SFT, which tends to produce a stronger classifier but suppresses reasoning. Another is a masked objective that preserves the model’s native reasoning, but only on cases the model already solves correctly; this means it receives little useful signal on failure cases and therefore struggles to improve beyond the base reasoning policy. A third route is think-mode RL, optimizing either accuracy or a behavioral faithfulness reward. The authors point out a key limitation of group-relative RL objectives: when all sampled rollouts agree, there is no learning signal, even if they all agree on the wrong answer. That leaves many confidently wrong prompts untouched. For auditing, the paper combines activation probes with behavioral interventions. Probes test when the decision becomes decodable in the hidden states, while interventions remove or replace parts of the generated reasoning to see whether the final action changes, which is a more causal test of whether reasoning matters. The paper also argues that several common metrics are misleading in this setting, including probability-based contribution scores, probe accuracy that ignores class imbalance, probing only at the end of reasoning, and uncontrolled flip tests.a:T613,The main empirical result is that the strongest action policy is the direct classifier that does not explicitly generate reasoning, while the reasoning policy is more auditable but less accurate, with the largest cost coming from missed interventions. That is the central capability–auditability tradeoff. In training experiments, decision-token SFT further strengthens the direct policy but pushes reasoning toward irrelevance. The masked objective cannot substantially beat the base reasoning policy because it only preserves reasoning on examples the model already gets right. Think-mode RL also fails to clearly surpass the base reasoning policy, not because RL is useless in general, but because the group-relative objective gives no gradient on prompts where all sampled rollouts agree, including confidently wrong ones. The audit results are also informative. For the reasoning policy, the eventual decision becomes decodable during the chain of thought, whereas for the direct classifier the decision is already decodable at the prompt stage. That supports the interpretation that the direct policy commits early, while the reasoning policy at least partially forms the decision during reasoning. The authors also stress that single-run comparisons can confuse seed variance with reward-objective effects, so multiple seeds or replicates are necessary when comparing RL variants.
