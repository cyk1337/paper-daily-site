The paper also compares these with functional interference measures, which operate closer to model behavior than raw gradient geometry.19:T798,The proposed method, Pareto LoRA, treats text loss and image loss as two coupled objectives and seeks a shared descent direction instead of combining them with a fixed scalar weight. Let g_txt and g_img be the gradients of the two modality-specific losses with respect to the trainable LoRA parameters. The method searches for the minimum-norm vector in the convex hull of these gradients. By the MGDA result, this vector either corresponds to a Pareto-stationary point or provides a common descent direction that improves both objectives. Because there are only two objectives, the optimal mixing coefficient has a closed-form solution, which keeps the additional optimization overhead modest.

An important detail is that the method is not applied blindly at every step. The paper first diagnoses the relationship between modality-specific gradients using two signals: cosine similarity for directional conflict and gradient magnitude ratio for dominance. This is a sensible design choice. If the gradients are already aligned and similarly scaled, intervention is unnecessary. The problematic cases are those where one modality overwhelms the update or where the two objectives pull in conflicting directions. This is precisely where fixed loss-level weighting is too crude, because it cannot adapt to layer-wise, stage-wise, or batch-wise changes in gradient geometry.

The LoRA-specific angle also matters. Since LoRA updates only low-rank adapters, adaptation capacity is limited. If the shared update direction is persistently driven by language gradients, the vision side is more likely to be underfit. Pareto LoRA can be interpreted as using that limited adaptation budget more carefully by steering each update toward a more balanced direction. Compared with generic gradient surgery methods, the paper is more targeted: unified multimodal generation, LoRA parameters, and text-image balance rather than arbitrary multi-task settings.1a:T67e,Several key findings are clear from the provided text. Vision performance drops much more relative to its unimodal counterpart than text performance does, which makes the imbalance concrete rather than anecdotal. Second, the authors directly inspect modality-specific gradients on shared parameters and find substantial imbalance in three of four evaluated tasks, with gradient magnitudes differing by orders of magnitude. Third, the direction and severity of imbalance vary across tasks, layers, and training stages, which explains why a fixed loss coefficient is often inadequate.
