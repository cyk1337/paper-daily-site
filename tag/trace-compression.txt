18:T654,The pipeline has three stages. First, the authors generate teacher traces using two large reasoning teachers, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B and gpt-oss-120B. For each question, they sample a response containing a <think> trace and a final answer, extract the answer, and use rejection sampling until they obtain a correct example or hit the retry limit. This yields datasets of question, correct trace, and correct answer.

Second, they compress the traces post hoc. Instead of training a dedicated compressor, they use two instruction-tuned models with a generic prompt asking for a shorter reasoning trace that preserves the essential logical steps, key insights, and final answer. The two compressors differ in aggressiveness and style, producing parallel compressed datasets.

Third, they fine-tune student models on three supervision variants: raw traces, compressed traces, and answer-only targets. Training uses standard next-token prediction on assistant tokens with sample packing. The student grid includes Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base, Qwen3.5-9B-Base, Llama-3.1-8B, and gpt-oss-20B, under both LoRA and one-epoch full/FSDP fine-tuning.

The most important design choice is the truncation ablation. For the Qwen-teacher runs, they create a compute-equivalent baseline by truncating the raw trace to match the token length of the most aggressive compressed version while keeping the final answer unchanged. This is the right control if one wants to test whether compression improves information density rather than merely reducing token budget. The results do not support a strong information-density advantage for compressed traces.19:T6a0,The experimental grid is fairly broad. The two teachers each generate about 283k correct traces. Compression reduces trace length to 8.6% to 21.0% of the original character count. Training tokens drop to 12% to 30% of the raw-trace budget, wall-clock training speeds up by 2.0x to 7.6x, and student inference outputs become 3x to 19x shorter. The gains are smaller under the gpt-oss teacher because its raw traces are already shorter.

The main study contains 48 runs across two teachers, four students, four data sources, and two training regimes. The ranking is consistent: raw traces achieve the best downstream accuracy at every tested scale and for both teachers; compressed traces are in the middle; answer-only is cheapest but performs worst and can be unstable under full fine-tuning. That consistency is more informative than any single number.

The seven Qwen-teacher truncation ablations are especially important. When the token budget is matched, compressed traces do not reliably outperform equally short truncated raw traces. This suggests that the main benefit of compression is compute reduction, not better supervision per token. The paper also reports failure modes such as termination failures in smaller students and instability in answer-only training, which hints that reasoning traces provide structural anchoring in addition to task information.

Evaluation covers in-distribution datasets across math, science, commonsense/logic, and medical QA, plus several OOD reasoning benchmarks.
