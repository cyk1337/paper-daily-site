9:T746,Hebatron is built on NVIDIA Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-Base-BF16, a sparse MoE model with roughly 30B total parameters and about 3B active parameters per forward pass. The base model choice serves two purposes. First, sparse activation keeps localization and inference efficient. Second, Nemotron’s documented pretraining distribution allows the authors to reintroduce selected original data as an anti-forgetting anchor during continued pretraining.

The training recipe uses a three-phase easy-to-hard curriculum. From the introduction, the intended progression is clear even if the exact mixture details are not fully visible in the excerpt: start with more regular, formal, and morphologically stable Hebrew sources such as literary or legal text, then move toward broader and noisier web-style data that demands more world knowledge and reasoning. The paper explicitly compares this ordering against the reversed curriculum, which is important because it turns curriculum from a narrative choice into an experimentally tested variable.

A second key component is continuous anti-forgetting anchoring. Instead of localizing on Hebrew alone and trying to recover English later, the model is co-trained throughout continued pretraining with a curated subset of the foundation model’s original signal, especially high-quality English reasoning data. The goal is to retain baseline reasoning and English proficiency while improving Hebrew. After continued pretraining, the model is supervised fine-tuned on 2 million bilingual Hebrew-English samples, which likely serves both instruction adaptation and cross-lingual alignment.

There is no new MoE routing method or tokenizer innovation here. The contribution is the training recipe: sparse MoE as the base, staged curriculum for localization, continuous anchoring against forgetting, and bilingual SFT on top.a:T5d0,The headline findings are straightforward. First, curriculum order matters: using the intended easy-to-hard curriculum yields about a 3-point aggregate benchmark gain over the reversed ordering. That is probably the most informative result from a pretraining perspective, because it isolates data sequencing as a meaningful factor in continued pretraining. Second, the final model reaches a Hebrew reasoning average of 73.8%, outperforming the open-source Hebrew baselines considered by the authors and remaining competitive with Gemma-3-27B-IT on tasks such as GSM8K-HE and Israeli Trivia. Third, because the model is a 30B-A3B sparse MoE, only around 3B parameters are active per forward pass, and the paper reports roughly 9x higher inference throughput at native context length.

The more interesting interpretation is in the trade-off. The model keeps a relatively large total parameter budget for knowledge capacity, but uses sparse activation to control cost. It also avoids a purely monolingual localization path by preserving some of the original English/reasoning signal during continued pretraining and then using bilingual SFT. If the full paper includes ablations on anchor ratio, English retention, forgetting, or stage-wise data mixtures, those would be more valuable than the final benchmark table alone.
