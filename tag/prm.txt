18:T447,The method defines routing confidence from three step-level statistics. For each token and MoE layer, the authors take the probability mass assigned to the selected top-k experts, normalize it within the current reasoning trajectory for each layer, and average across MoE layers to obtain a token-level routing signal. They then aggregate it over a reasoning step. Overall routing mass M measures the step-wide routing strength. Head routing mass H focuses on the beginning of the step, where the direction of a reasoning move is often set. Within-step routing variation D captures instability inside the step. Empirically, M and H tend to correlate positively with external step-quality scores, while D tends to correlate negatively. Two systems are built on this signal. RoCo-Rethink identifies the least confident reasoning step, keeps the prefix, and regenerates the remaining trajectory. RoCo-PRM uses a strong MoE teacher to assign routing-derived process scores and distills them into a standalone PRM, so the supervision can also be used by dense models or models without routing access.19:T61f,The framework has two stages. In stage one, the system starts from roughly 17.8K theorem statements collected from public formal corpora, mainly NuminaMath-LEAN and Lean-Workbook. For each theorem, a model is prompted to produce a proof attempt in a single pass, as numbered natural-language steps paired with corresponding Lean tactics. A key design choice is that the model never receives Lean execution feedback during generation. This preserves genuine model errors instead of repaired trajectories, which is exactly what a verifier needs to learn from.

In stage two, the formal proof is compiled in Lean. All steps before the first failing tactic are labeled correct; the first failing step and all subsequent steps are labeled incorrect. The authors recognize that Lean failure is not always a mathematical failure: some failures are purely syntactic or due to formalization mismatch. To reduce such false negatives, they add a second model call that reads the Lean error message, attempts one syntax repair, and classifies the failure as mathematical versus merely formal. Only proofs whose first failure is judged to be mathematical are kept. For fully compiling proofs, there is an additional consistency check to ensure the natural-language steps actually express the same mathematics as the formal proof.

This yields 10,634 labeled proofs and 152,005 step labels, plus a held-out 200-proof benchmark called LeanPRM-Bench. Verifiers are then trained purely on natural-language inputs, so Lean is only used once during data creation, not at inference time.1a:T535,The main empirical result is a controlled comparison against PRM800K-style human supervision. With the same model, the same training recipe, and the same number of updates, LeanPRM-Verifier-4B outperforms the PRM800K-trained verifier on four out of five benchmarks. The gains are large: +14.4 exact step-label accuracy on LeanPRM-Bench, +9.7 on Hard2Verify, and +9.4 on IMO-GradingBench.

The paper also evaluates proof selection in a best-of-N setting on IMO-level problems. The LeanPRM-trained verifier selects correct proofs noticeably more often than random, while the PRM800K verifier stays close to chance. This is an important sanity check: the model is not merely learning stylistic preferences, but something closer to proof validity.

Another useful result is scale consistency. The improvement from LeanPRM supervision appears across all tested model sizes, from 0.6B to 8B, on LeanPRM-Bench, Hard2Verify, and IMO-GradingBench. Given that the dataset is only 10.6K proofs, this suggests that supervision quality and domain alignment matter more than raw label count in this setting.

The most informative ablations are the ones around filtering and label construction: whether syntax-repair filtering is used, how much noise comes from formal-only failures, and how robust the gains are across model scales and benchmarks.1b:T86e,The central idea is to replace the usual frontier state with a persistent pool of historical prefixes. Each round still expands only a fixed budget of N new prefixes, so after t rounds the pool size is at most Nt. This keeps memory growth linear rather than storing the full exponential tree. The key change is that a prefix that was not selected in one round is not permanently discarded; it can be reconsidered later.

The paper instantiates this idea in two ways.

The first is Subpool Selection. A naive global top-k over the full history would still be dominated by a few over-scored but ultimately wrong prefixes. Uniform sampling from history would waste tokens on stale or low-quality states. Subpool Selection instead samples a random historical subpool and then applies PRM-based ranking within that shortlist. This preserves PRM guidance while giving under-scored prefixes a chance to re-enter expansion. In effect, it adds controlled stochasticity on top of greedy PRM ranking to break long-lived blocking by falsely high-scored nodes.

The second is Power Backtrack SMC. This extends SMC-style sequential sampling from the current frontier to a persistent historical pool. Prefixes are sampled from a mixture over historical states, with powered PRM scores controlling how sharp the proposal distribution is, and mixture-corrected importance weights compensating for the fact that candidates come from multiple historical proposal sources. Compared with standard frontier-only SMC, this version can jump back to earlier states and recover from bad pruning decisions.

A strong part of the paper is its treatment of target mismatch. The ideal quantity is the true marginal probability that a prefix can still be completed into a correct final answer under the deployed generator. Real PRMs are usually trained on binary supervision or stronger rollout policies, so their scores are only proxies. They may be uncalibrated and may not match the continuation behavior of the base LLM used at inference. The paper’s main mechanism claim is that the real brittleness comes from combining this imperfect proxy with irreversible frontier-only pruning.1c:T5e3,The experiments focus on mathematical reasoning and evaluate the accuracy–token trade-off rather than only final accuracy at a fixed sample count. The proposed methods are compared against strong baselines from two families: independent full-sample methods such as Best-of-N and self-consistency, and PRM-guided frontier-only methods such as beam search, tree search, and SMC variants. The qualitative result is consistent: at the same token budget, Subpool Selection and Power Backtrack SMC usually achieve higher accuracy; or, for the same target accuracy, they require fewer generated tokens.

The gains are most visible when PRM scores are noisy or only weakly correlated with eventual correctness, because historical revisiting can repair early mistakes. The ablations carry three main messages. First, persistence alone is not enough: keeping history but using overly greedy global selection gives limited benefit.
