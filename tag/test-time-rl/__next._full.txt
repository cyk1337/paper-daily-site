18:T8e7,APEx maintains two levels of experience. Instance-level memory stores summarized execution traces for concrete queries, preserving operational details that can later be retrieved. Category-level skills aggregate many experiences within a category-modality bucket and distill them into reusable procedural guidance. At inference time, a query q is routed to a bucket, relevant memories R_M(q) and the corresponding skill R_S(q) are retrieved, and both are packed into a structured context E(q). The Planner uses E(q) to generate a research plan. The Executor follows that plan through multi-turn tool interaction and produces a trajectory τ and a final answer. After execution, the trajectory is summarized into a new memory entry, and once enough fresh experience accumulates, the Distiller updates the category-level skill.

The key idea is not just hierarchical memory, but the Executor-Distiller-Planner loop. Planner converts retrieved experience into a task-specific plan, Executor interacts with tools, and Distiller abstracts new trajectories into procedural skills. These modules are trained with a three-stage alternating GRPO schedule rather than a single joint objective. The motivation is cross-module credit assignment: if everything is optimized end-to-end at once, it is difficult for final task reward to shape the quality of distilled skills in a stable way. Alternating optimization gives each module a cleaner relative-reward signal while the others are partially fixed. In that sense, skill distillation is treated as reward-guided policy learning, not prompt-based summarization.

Another notable component is skill-guided TTRL. During deployment, the Planner performs test-time reinforcement learning on unlabeled queries, while retrieved skills and memories act as procedural priors that regularize online adaptation. The model is therefore not exploring from scratch at test time; it adapts locally around previously distilled high-quality procedures. The resulting improved trajectories are then written back into memory and can later trigger skill updates. This is the main conceptual difference from standard memory-augmented retrieval: retrieval helps the current sample, but APEx is designed so that the current sample also improves future behavior.19:T64b,The headline result reported in the paper is an average score of 64.4% across deep research benchmarks. This is 14.7 points above GPT-5.4 and 3.0 points above the strongest memory-augmented baseline. For judging the method, the second comparison matters more, because it isolates the gain from experience organization and training rather than from a stronger base model.

The paper also highlights a context-efficiency result: a skill-only variant remains competitive while injecting fewer memory tokens. That supports the claim that category-level procedural skills are a more compact carrier of useful experience than flat trajectory retrieval. This matters for long-context agents, where the bottleneck is often not retrieval quality but context budget and retrieval noise.

From the full text, the experimental story appears to rely on three main ablation axes. First, replacing hierarchical experience with flat memory retrieval hurts performance, suggesting that instance memories and category skills play different roles. Second, removing reward-guided distillation or the alternating GRPO schedule degrades both skill quality and downstream task performance, supporting the claim that useful skills must be shaped by task reward. Third, removing skill-guided TTRL weakens online adaptation, indicating that test-time updates without procedural priors become high-variance exploration. Then jump to the method overview figure and trace the data flow: query routing, two-level retrieval, the Planner-Executor-Distiller loop, and TTRL. After that, read the alternating GRPO section carefully enough to understand which module is optimized in each stage, where the reward comes from, and why this is expected to be more stable than joint training. If you want to go deeper, inspect the appendix for retrieval details, bucket construction, reward design, and memory-update triggers. Those implementation choices are likely to determine whether the method transfers to other agent settings.1b:T78f,DDRL has three components, each targeting a different source of error.

First, balanced confidence-aware sampling. The authors analyze the relationship between answer frequency under repeated sampling and answer correctness. High-frequency answers are usually correct, low-frequency answers are usually wrong, and medium-frequency answers fall into an ambiguity region with unstable correctness. Standard TTRL uses all rollouts equally, which lets these ambiguous samples inject noisy rewards. DDRL instead filters samples by frequency, discarding much of the ambiguous middle while preserving a balanced set of positive and negative examples. The balance matters: RL needs contrastive signal, so this is not simply confidence filtering.

Second, debiased advantage estimation. Vanilla TTRL uses GRPO, where rewards are normalized relative to the sampled group. In an unsupervised pseudo-label setting, that normalization is not benign. When consensus is weak, a few mislabeled rewards can receive disproportionately large relative advantages, pushing the policy in the wrong direction. The paper provides a theoretical analysis of this bias. DDRL replaces group-relative normalization with fixed, label-dependent advantages, aiming to remove the extra distortion introduced by group statistics. The logic is straightforward: if rewards are already noisy, do not let normalization amplify the noise.

Third, consensus-based off-policy refinement. After the RL phase, the method constructs a rejection-sampling style dataset from high-consensus outputs and performs a lightweight off-policy refinement step. This converts the more reliable samples discovered during RL into lower-variance supervised updates. The sequencing is important. Pure rejection sampling from the start would be more stable but less adaptive; the RL phase first moves the policy toward the test distribution, and the off-policy stage then consolidates the gains.1c:T509,The headline numbers in the abstract are relative gains of 15.3% on Qwen2.5-MATH-1.5B and 12.7% on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct over the original TTRL setup, with improvements across multiple math benchmarks. The most important question is whether the paper cleanly attributes those gains to the proposed diagnosis.

The key experiments should include: frequency-versus-correctness curves showing that medium-frequency samples are indeed the ambiguity region; theoretical and empirical analysis of GRPO bias under different consensus levels; ablations for balanced sampling, debiased advantage estimation, and off-policy refinement; and transfer across model sizes and math benchmarks. If the gains remain after isolating the final refinement stage, that would strengthen the claim that the main contribution is better signal handling rather than simply adding a supervised cleanup step.

It would also be useful to inspect rollout count, sampling temperature, number of test-time update steps, and sensitivity to pseudo-label purity, because these define the practical cost and stability envelope of TTRL. A reasonable caution remains: this setup benefits from tasks with relatively discrete answers and meaningful majority voting.
