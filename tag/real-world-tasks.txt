19:T43c,The Apodex Discovery framework defines the core operational unit of discoverative AI as a heavy-duty solver, which relies on four supporting infrastructure components: 1. Problem formulation, which translates ambiguous open human goals into explicit objectives, constraints, assumptions, and success criteria; 2. Reality-based environments, which provide access to data, tools, compute, experimental interfaces, and human interaction channels required for problem solving; 3. Verification mechanisms that support both outcome and process verification, producing reproducible checks of the correctness and relevance of intermediate claims and final outputs, with support for blind trajectory auditing; 4. Repair loops that feed signals from computation, experiment feedback, external evaluation, and human judgment back to the solver to support failure localization, hypothesis revision, and approach iteration. The accompanying TRACES reality benchmark shifts the unit of analysis from individual answers or single-task success/failure to full end-to-end discovery iteration episodes.1a:T84e,The work has two main components: building EdgeBench and fitting environment-learning curves on top of it.

On the benchmark side, the authors deliberately avoid short tasks and instead curate 134 realistic, workflow-like tasks across six capability families, including scientific research and ML, systems and software engineering, combinatorial optimization, formal mathematics, professional knowledge work, and interactive games. Each task runs inside an executable workspace that supports both fast local exploration and slower, more authoritative judge feedback, resembling real workflows where local iteration and external evaluation coexist. Tasks allow at least 12 hours of continuous agent interaction, with some analyses extended to 72 hours. The paper also reports human expert effort, averaging 57.2 hours and reaching 320 hours, to argue that the tasks are not trivially saturable.

On the analysis side, the independent variable is cumulative environment interaction time, and the dependent variable is benchmark-level performance. The central empirical finding is that average performance is fit extremely well by a log-sigmoid curve, with R^2 = 0.998. The authors also propose a theoretical explanation: environment learning is modeled as frontier expansion on latent task graphs. As the agent explores, it gradually expands the reachable frontier of useful states or strategies, producing slow-then-fast-then-saturating progress. On a log-time axis, this yields a stable sigmoid-like form. The value of this theory is not formal rigor so much as a plausible mechanism for why the law is not a simple power law or pure logarithmic trend.

The paper also compares frontier agents across release generations and claims that learning speed has roughly doubled every three months since September 2025. Another important design choice is the comparison between continuous experience accumulation and independent restarts. This separates true learning from mere repeated sampling, and that distinction is more important than the benchmark score itself for anyone interested in online adaptation or test-time learning.1b:T6f2,There are four main result blocks. First, across roughly 38,000 hours of interaction and 134 tasks, overall performance follows a highly stable log-sigmoid curve as a function of environment interaction time, with R^2 = 0.998. The paper further claims that this functional form holds across task families, under longer horizons, and when forecasting later performance from early trajectories. Second, gains continue over long horizons, with analyses extending to 72 hours, so the effect is not just an early transient. Third, learning speed appears to improve rapidly across model generations, with the headline empirical trend being a doubling roughly every three months. Fourth, learning dynamics matter in their own right: continuous runs outperform independent restarts, longer context helps retain experience, and case studies show how local and judge feedback can convert many failed probes into a few durable improvements.

From a pretraining researcher’s perspective, the most informative comparisons are not the absolute scores but the controls: continuous experience versus restart, different context lengths, and the predictability of late performance from early trajectories. If those findings are robust, then “ability to accumulate experience” is becoming a distinct capability axis beyond static pretrained competence.

A note of caution is warranted. The exact curve shape can depend on task composition, normalization, judge design, and the agent scaffold. A very high R^2 means the fit is clean, not that the mechanism is uniquely identified. Also, benchmark-level averaging can smooth away substantial task-level variation. When reading the experiments, it is worth checking both the aggregate fit and the dispersion across families or individual tasks.1c:T461,For a skim, start with the benchmark design. The key question is whether EdgeBench really measures learning from environment feedback, rather than just longer execution. Look for multi-level feedback, support for iterative trial-and-error, and the separation between local workspace and judge environment.

Then go straight to the main scaling-law figure and the fitted functional form. Check what alternative functions were compared and why log-sigmoid is preferred over power-law, pure log, or standard saturation curves. If the theory section is concise, it is worth reading the latent task graph and frontier expansion argument, because that determines whether the paper offers more than an empirical fit.

In the experiments, prioritize four parts: benchmark-wide and family-level fits, horizon extension from 12 to 72 hours, continuous learning versus restart, and context-length effects on experience retention. A useful skim should take about 30 minutes.
