b:T5dc,The method is ATWU, Alternating Token-Weighted Unlearning. The paper first formalizes a notion of forget-specificity: a forget token is important to unlearn if applying the forget objective to that token is compatible with staying near retain optimality; if forgetting that token strongly conflicts with the retain objective, it is more likely to be structural rather than target-bearing. This turns token weights into latent variables in a joint optimization over model parameters and token importance. Under a stated separation condition, the authors argue that the objective can recover the oracle support of forget-specific tokens. In practice, ATWU uses a lightweight scorer to assign token weights from hidden states, parameterized as a linear direction in the LM representation space rather than a separate large auxiliary model. Training alternates between updating the scorer and updating the model. The intuition is that direct simultaneous optimization can be unstable because the model and the weights co-adapt too quickly; alternating updates make the procedure closer to an EM-like loop where token responsibility is estimated and then used to drive parameter updates. The method is compared against sample-level unlearning, probability-based token heuristics, and auxiliary-model-based weighting. Its main claim is that the retain objective is not just a regularizer but also an informative signal for identifying which forget tokens should actually receive strong forgetting pressure.c:T4b6,Experiments are conducted on TOFU and RWKU. A 25–35 minute skim should be enough to capture the main idea.e:T8c2,The starting point is standard OPD. The student samples rollouts from prompts, and the teacher provides token-level supervision on those student-generated trajectories. The base objective is written in a PPO-style clipped policy optimization form, where the token-level advantage is derived from the teacher-versus-old-student log-likelihood ratio. The authors argue that this objective is too coarse because it assumes all trajectories and all token positions provide equally reliable supervision.

FiRe-OPD has two stages. The first is trajectory-level filtering. Instead of using external verifiers, outcome correctness, or reward models, the method scores each student rollout by the teacher’s normalized log-probability on that trajectory. The intuition is straightforward: if the teacher assigns very low probability to a student path, then the student has moved into a region the teacher does not naturally model well. In that case, token-level supervision from the teacher may be noisy or even misleading. So within each batch, trajectories are ranked by this score and the bottom p% are discarded, with p=20 by default.

The second stage is token-level soft reweighting. For the retained trajectories, the method does not hard-drop tokens. Instead, each token receives a continuous weight formed by multiplying two signals: teacher confidence and student confusion. The first term measures whether the teacher’s local supervision is reliable; the second measures whether the student actually needs learning at that position. This gives larger gradients to tokens where the teacher is confident and the student is uncertain. Compared with hard token selection methods such as TIP-style filtering, FiRe-OPD keeps gradient contributions from all positions but scales them by informativeness, which should make optimization smoother and less threshold-sensitive.

One of the paper’s strongest claims is asymmetric: hard filtering works better at the trajectory level because bad rollouts are often globally unhelpful, while soft weighting works better at the token level because useful and useless positions often coexist within the same trajectory. That “filter first, then reweight” structure is the core recipe contribution.f:T5bf,From the full text provided, the authors evaluate FiRe-OPD in strong-to-weak, single-teacher, and multi-teacher distillation settings, comparing against standard OPD and several selective OPD baselines such as EOPD, TIP, ExOPD, and Uni-OPD. The abstract does not expose the full benchmark list or exact numbers, but the main empirical claims are clear.

First, combining trajectory filtering with token soft weighting outperforms doing selection at only one granularity. That supports the paper’s central argument that granularity isolation leaves performance on the table.

Second, the preferred operation differs by level: hard filtering is better for trajectories, while soft weighting is better than hard token selection for tokens. This is the most interesting empirical takeaway because it is not a generic “more selection is better” story; it is a claim about matching the operation to the granularity.

Third, the gains persist in both single-teacher and multi-teacher settings, suggesting the method is not tied to one specific teacher.
