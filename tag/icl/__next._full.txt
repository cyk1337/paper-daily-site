L0 Context Grounding tests whether a model can locate, read, and combine multimodal evidence. L1 New Information Application tests whether it can use newly supplied facts, values, or cases within capabilities it already has. L2 New Knowledge Learning tests whether it can absorb rules, discoveries, task semantics, or empirical conclusions that are defined only in the context. This is more diagnostic than standard document QA or chart QA because it is explicitly organized around context dependence and failure localization. Second, the benchmark mixes converted public tasks with newly constructed domain-specific tasks, including recent medical-paper conclusion inference and financial-report ROE analysis. The authors emphasize automated construction and filtering so that instances scale while still requiring genuine context use rather than memorized world knowledge. Third, they standardize inference and scoring across tasks and also study judge reliability. That makes the benchmark more than a collection of multimodal questions; it is a structured evaluation asset with a clear capability taxonomy and analysis protocol.19:T588,The method is a black-box search procedure for discovering spurious system prompts. A spurious prompt is defined by exclusion: it must not name or describe the target task, invoke domain-specific vocabulary, or use common reasoning cues such as asking the model to think carefully, verify answers, or eliminate options. At the same time, it must still instruct the assistant to answer the user’s query directly.

The search loop has three components: a generator produces initial candidate prompts, a validator filters them using manually specified lexical constraints, and a mutator iteratively rewrites top-performing candidates. Candidates are evaluated on disjoint training subsets, selected by top-K performance, and refined over multiple rounds. No gradients, logits, hidden states, or parameter updates are used; the procedure is fully black-box.

The important design choice is not search sophistication but the strict semantic exclusion. By aggressively removing task-related content from the prompt space, the paper tries to isolate non-semantic control factors such as tone, persona, ritual, protocol, or formatting. The paper then extends the analysis beyond accuracy gains to behavioral steering, showing that such prompts can bias the model toward first-option selection, wrong answers, or even/prime-number outputs in math settings. That makes the phenomenon more than a curiosity about odd prompts.1a:T4e8,The experiments cover reasoning and QA benchmarks, models from 0.8B to 27B, and three model families. The main result is that spurious prompts can match or outperform standard prompting baselines and PromptWizard, despite being intentionally stripped of task-specific semantics. The paper also argues that these prompts are semantically indistinguishable from unrelated text and are often shorter than task-aware optimized prompts, which weakens two simple explanations: that they are secretly task descriptions, or that they work merely because they are longer.

Transfer experiments are especially informative. The paper reports that effective spurious prompts often do not transfer cleanly across models or benchmarks, suggesting that they exploit highly specific interactions between a given model, a benchmark, and the prompt’s latent control structure. If they were simply activating a general reasoning mode, stronger transfer would be expected. The steering experiments are another key result because they show directional control, not just accidental performance variation.
