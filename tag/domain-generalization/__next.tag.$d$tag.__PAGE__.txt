The benchmark contains 793 episodes, 24 multi-step web tasks, 56 attack templates, 8 attack families, 5 depth levels, and 4 system-prompt configurations, with hybrid screenshot plus accessibility-tree perception. Metrics are aligned with AgentDojo-style BU, UuA, ASR, and safety measurements.

The methodological choice that matters most is the separation between attack technique and optimized phrasing. Instead of training a new attacker, the authors manually transcribe attack methods from prior red-teaming papers into human-readable templates. This means the benchmark measures a reproducible hand-crafted floor, not the full ceiling of RL-discovered attacks. They then run prompt ablations down to L0_bare and L1_helpful to test whether resistance comes from the system prompt or from the model weights. Finally, they perform a domain-transfer check by evaluating the same frontier weights in a coding-agent harness, SkillBench, under analogous skill-injection setups.

The paper also includes two useful boundary checks. One is a small-budget RL attacker baseline, AutoInject-style adaptive random suffix search, to estimate whether RL can recover a gap under a constrained query and cost budget. The other is an image-channel replication that tests popup-overlay and in-content malicious-text attacks on frontier models. These additions help distinguish between “hand-crafted attacks fail” and “the attack surface is closed.”

Compared with prior work, the contribution is not a new attack algorithm. It is a reproducibility-oriented evaluation design plus an audit of what recent headline ASRs actually depend on: retired target models, unreleased optimized strings, and domain-specific hardening.c:T907,The main browser-domain result is straightforward. Across the benchmark, the authors report 0/140 successful multi-step attacks on Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4, with a Clopper–Pearson 95% upper bound of about 2.60% on ASR. There were 2 apparent successes out of 158 raw episodes, but both occurred on a bank-balance task where the ground truth changed during the experiment, so they were excluded as invalid. Hand-crafted reproductions of RL-Hammer, WASP, TRAP, and MUZZLE-style attacks also score 0/40 on frontier models. Importantly, this remains true under prompt ablations down to L0_bare and L1_helpful, which supports the claim that browser-domain resistance is primarily in the weights rather than in a defensive system prompt wrapper.

That robustness does not transfer to coding agents. On SkillBench, the same model weights are highly vulnerable to hand-crafted skill injection: Sonnet 4.6 reaches up to 40/40 = 100% ASR, GPT-5.4 up to 79/100 = 79%, with cross-method means of 33.3% and 66.8%, respectively. This is the paper’s most important empirical point. The large Sonnet 4.5 to 4.6 improvement documented in Anthropic’s browser-agent system card should therefore be read as browser-specific hardening, not as a general agent-safety improvement.

The reproducibility audit is also informative. For RL-Hammer, WASP, TRAP, RedTeamCUA, MUZZLE, and pop-ups, the authors record whether the original target model is still accessible, whether the optimized attack strings were released, and what ASR their hand-crafted approximations achieve on the closest frontier successor. Four of the six audited papers fall into the combination of retired target plus unreleased strings, which makes independent reproduction difficult.

The boundary experiments reinforce the same story. A within-harness AutoInject-style RL baseline achieves 0/100 on Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4 under a 5-query-per-pair budget and roughly $10 of API spend, so at least under that budget the RL gap is not opened. In the image-channel replication, popup-overlay attacks drop to 1/30 = 3.3% attempted compromise on frontier models, while in-content malicious-text fixtures still reach 5/20 = 25%, though many are bounded by harness login walls before exfiltration. So even within the visual channel, attackability is not uniform.d:T6bc,If you only want the main conclusions, read the introduction and Section 3 first. Then read the benchmark construction section. The credibility of the whole paper depends on this boundary, because the benchmark is intentionally measuring reproducible hand-crafted attacks rather than the full power of optimized attackers.

Next, read the browser-versus-coding comparison in detail. This is the strongest part of the paper. It directly tests whether safety hardening transfers across tool domains on the same weights. If the paper includes action traces or failure examples, those are worth inspecting because they often reveal whether the coding failures come from instruction-following bias, tool-surface differences, or weaker domain-specific refusal behavior.

Finally, read the reproducibility audit table in Section 6. It is useful beyond this paper because it helps interpret prior headline ASRs in terms of model availability and release practices. For someone working on agent safety evaluation, that table is more methodologically important than any single ASR number.
