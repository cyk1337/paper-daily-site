18:T691,RuBench 1.0 contains 25 repository-level bug-fix tasks drawn from five active open-source repositories: aiohttp, aiogram, Laravel, NestJS, and Fastify, spanning Python, PHP, TypeScript, and JavaScript. Each task comes from a real fix commit rather than a handcrafted puzzle. The task statement is then newly authored in Russian, in the style of a customer request to a contractor: symptoms, desired behavior, and compatibility constraints are described behaviorally, without translating or paraphrasing an English upstream issue.

The benchmark design has several strong protocol choices. First, the judge is the maintainer’s regression test suite, kept private and withheld from the released benchmark artifacts. Second, a freshness gate admits only tasks whose fix commits postdate the training cutoffs of all evaluated models, and this gate is enforced mechanically at import time. Third, the authors release full trajectories and diffs for every run, making outcomes auditable. Fourth, the unit of evaluation is a deployed product configuration—CLI agent plus model plus reasoning-effort setting—rather than a bare model, because the harness itself materially affects behavior.

A notable methodological detail is the validity audit. In a hors-concours setting with Claude Code + Fable 5, the authors found that an official safeguard silently substituted Opus 4.8 on a subset of tasks. From this they derive a practical validity rule: an evaluation cell is valid only if the executing model in the trajectory matches the requested model. This is especially relevant for benchmarking closed product agents where the visible configuration may not fully determine execution.19:T5e5,The benchmark is small, but the protocol is careful. The paper evaluates four product configurations, with three independent runs per configuration, for a total of 300 scored rollouts. It reports pass@1, task-level 95% confidence intervals, paired bootstrap comparisons, and per-task cost, token, and time accounting.

The headline numbers are straightforward: the best configuration, Claude Code + Opus 4.8, solves 78.7% of RuBench 1.0 tasks on average across three runs, while the weakest, Claude Code + Haiku 4.5, solves 53.3%, a spread of 25.4 points. The authors are careful not to overclaim statistical separation at N=25; they explicitly note that only gaps involving the weakest configuration are cleanly resolvable at this sample size.

The most interesting empirical finding is the trajectory audit. In the hors-concours Claude Code + Fable 5 setting, about 20% of tasks were silently executed by Opus 4.8 instead. This shows that benchmark validity can fail if one trusts product labels without checking execution traces.

On the construction side, every included task had to satisfy an import contract: the maintainer oracle must fail on the parent commit and pass on the fix commit in a reproducible environment. The paper also mentions a deliberately imported pre-cutoff task that was rejected by the freshness gate, serving as a sanity check for the machinery. Because the benchmark has only 25 tasks, it is better viewed as a high-quality small-sample measurement than as a stable leaderboard.1a:T49b,The core technical contribution is the CUSP benchmark and its evaluation protocol. The dataset contains 4,760 temporally anchored scientific milestones spanning January 2024 to March 2026 across multiple disciplines. Sources include top-tier journals such as Nature, Science, and Cell, along with community-curated milestone repositories. Each event is tied to a precise time reference so that model access can be restricted to information available before the event. Scientific forecasting is operationalized along four dimensions: feasibility assessment, mechanistic reasoning, generative solution design, and temporal prediction. This is a stronger design than a single yes/no forecast because it separates 'recognizing a plausible direction' from 'recovering the actual path that later succeeded'. The paper also compares pre-cutoff, post-cutoff, and full-information conditions to isolate the gap between knowledge availability and genuine forecasting ability, and analyzes whether highly cited advances are harder to predict. This is not a new model or training method; it is a temporally controlled evaluation framework built around verifiable, resolved scientific events.1b:T502,The main empirical pattern is consistent. Evaluating frontier models on 4,760 events, the authors find that models are weak on feasibility assessment and temporal prediction, often near chance or otherwise unstable, and they show a systematic delayed bias when predicting when advances will occur. They are also overconfident and exhibit response bias in feasibility judgments, which makes their uncertainty estimates unreliable. Models do better at identifying plausible technical approaches from candidate sets, but in generative solution design they often fail to produce solutions aligned with the methods that actually drove the later advance. Importantly, these failures are not confined to post-cutoff events. Even on pre-cutoff events, models do not fully exploit available knowledge. Supplying additional pre-cutoff information improves both pre-cutoff and post-cutoff performance, which indicates a knowledge-access or knowledge-use gap. But a substantial forecasting gap remains relative to full-information settings, and that gap grows for highly cited advances. The introduction and abstract do not expose the full numeric tables, but the qualitative result is clear: current frontier models are better at plausibility ranking than at calibrated scientific forecasting.1c:T423,For a skim, start with the benchmark construction and task definitions, then move to the comparisons between pre-cutoff, post-cutoff, and full-information settings. The first tells you whether this is truly a forecasting benchmark rather than a repackaged science QA task. The second tells you whether the paper is measuring forecasting rather than retrieval. The most useful sections are the introduction, the benchmark section, the main results on feasibility and temporal prediction, and the analysis of the knowledge gap versus the forecasting gap. If you only want the decision-relevant points, focus on three questions: how strict the temporal anchoring is, whether the four task types genuinely separate different aspects of forecasting, and how much performance improves with extra pre-cutoff knowledge versus how much gap remains.
