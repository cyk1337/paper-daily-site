9:T6a2,The method has three main ingredients. First, the authors approximate the Hessian with the empirical Fisher, using the standard assumption that a converged pretrained model is near a local optimum. They reshape the full gradient vector into a matrix G and show that the Fisher admits an exact decomposition as a sum of Kronecker products. After expressing the relevant expectation in terms of E[G⊗G], they take its SVD and recover a representation of the form J(w)=Σ_i σ_i U_i⊗V_i. The approximation comes from truncating this sum to the top-r components.

Second, they avoid explicitly constructing the enormous n^2 by m^2 matrix. Instead, they use an implicitly restarted Arnoldi solver that only needs matrix-vector products. The key identity is (G⊗G)vec(V)=vec(GVG^T), which turns the expensive operation into smaller dense multiplications on reshaped gradients. This is what makes the method scalable.

Third, they do not rely on the low-rank Kronecker term alone. They compute the Fisher diagonal exactly and combine it with the truncated decomposition. This hybrid design is sensible: the low-rank term captures correlations, while the exact diagonal preserves per-parameter scale. In practice, that should be more stable than either a pure diagonal approximation or a pure low-rank one.

Compared with K-FAC-style approximations, block-diagonal Hessians, or layerwise curvature proxies used in quantization, the distinguishing feature is the explicit attempt to preserve cross-layer interactions. The paper is not mainly about within-layer second-order structure. It is about whether off-diagonal coupling is strong enough to predict super-additive damage under joint perturbations.a:T868,The experiments span four LLM families from 350M to 7B parameters. Even without the full tables in the provided text, the main empirical findings are clear.

First, the paper reports direct evidence of substantial non-diagonal Fisher/Hessian structure in large language models. This matters because it validates, at realistic scale, the claim that diagonal approximations miss important information. Across multiple model families, value projection layers emerge as the most sensitive components and also show the strongest cross-layer correlations. Other module types behave less uniformly and appear more architecture-dependent.

Second, the proposed curvature scores correlate strongly with compression damage. The authors evaluate quantization and sparsification and show that layerwise Fisher/Hessian values track performance degradation well, better than cruder independent-layer approximations. In other words, layers with larger curvature are more likely to cause larger perplexity or task-performance drops when perturbed.

Third, they run inter-layer corruption experiments rather than only single-layer ablations. When two strongly coupled layers are compressed or corrupted together, the resulting degradation exceeds the sum of their individual effects. The off-diagonal curvature terms predict this excess damage. This is one of the most important results in the paper, because it shows that cross-layer structure is not just mathematically elegant; it explains real failure modes.

Fourth, they study post-corruption recovery. After damaging selected layers, they fine-tune different candidate layers and find that updating the layers most strongly coupled to the corrupted ones yields the best recovery. That is a practical result: the best repair target is not always the damaged layer itself, but sometimes the layer most tightly coupled to it.

The numbers worth remembering are the scale range, 350M to 7B, and the reduction from quadratic to linear memory complexity. The evaluation is also broader than a single compression setting: quantization, sparsification, joint corruption, and post-corruption fine-tuning are all included.b:T566,This is a skim paper, not one that requires line-by-line derivation.
