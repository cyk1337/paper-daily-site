9:T534,The method, REST (REpresentation-Supervised Thoughts), augments the standard final-answer cross-entropy with four differentiable losses on the latent thought itself. The theoretical setup argues that CE on the final answer does not penalize bad intermediate representations as long as the correct answer remains sufficiently likely, so recursive systems can accumulate representational errors over rounds and across agents.

REST targets four properties. Causality requires the thought to preserve information needed to reproduce the correct output. Minimality pushes it to discard information irrelevant to that output. Separability prevents semantically distinct inputs, especially those with different output distributions, from collapsing to similar latent thoughts. Stability encourages the thought to reflect the distribution over plausible outputs rather than a single sampled answer trace. These losses are added to CE with tunable weights.

Implementation-wise, the paper studies both a multi-agent latent recursive system and a single-agent variant where a model feeds its own hidden states back as the next reasoning step. Importantly, REST does not change the architecture or inference procedure; it only changes the training objective. That makes the paper more about missing supervision than about a new system design.a:T432,The evaluation spans MATH500, GPQA-Diamond, MedQA, AIME2025/2026, LiveCodeBench-v6, and MBPP+. Under matched training budget, identical training data, and the same latent budget, REST improves over CE-only by +3.5 points on average in the multi-agent setting and +3.3 in the single-agent setting. The best configurations reach +7.5 and +6.5 points, respectively. For a skim-level read, these are the main numbers to retain.

The paper also reports that REST decodes 15.4% more tokens on average at inference. The authors attribute this to a higher rate of convergence to a final answer. That is an interesting trade-off: better latent supervision improves accuracy, but not necessarily by reducing visible decoding cost in the current setup.

The analysis sections matter more than usual here. The paper claims that CE-only systems exhibit thought collapse and retention of irrelevant information, while REST-trained systems better satisfy the targeted properties. Those diagnostics are central to judging whether the method is doing more than adding another auxiliary loss.b:T78e,The proposed method, SCALPEL, is a contrastive sparse autoencoder designed to learn forget-selective features. The paper first formalizes an energy-bias view of reconstruction-based extraction. If activations decompose into multiple concept components, the marginal gain from improving reconstruction of component j scales with its energy. As a result, high-dispersion background concepts are favored over low-dispersion forget concepts. This is not presented as a full impossibility theorem, but as a useful incentive-level explanation for why standard extractors blend precise targets into broader features.

SCALPEL modifies the SAE objective with contrastive learning centered on forget anchors. Each forget example is augmented into paraphrases and rewritten variants, and different views of the same anchor are treated as positives. Negatives are sampled from retain data rather than from other forget examples. That design matters: using retain negatives helps separate target-specific structure from shared linguistic and task background, while avoiding the mistake of pushing related forget examples away from each other. After training, the SAE yields a fixed set of sparse forget-associated features. At inference time, the same feature set is available for every input, but each feature is subtracted only when activated by the encoder, producing an explicit and input-dependent internal filter.

Two details are especially important. First, the paper uses a norm-aware feature selection rule, because intervention strength depends both on encoder activation and on the magnitude of the decoder direction. Second, the theory section gives three supporting arguments: reconstruction induces energy bias, contrastive training favors target-selective sparse features, and high-scoring features admit a bound on expected background perturbation. These are best read as justification for the design, not as deletion guarantees.c:T648,Experiments are run in the controlled TOFU author-level unlearning setting across three model families. The paper compares SCALPEL against mechanistic baselines such as PCA, NMF, and standard SAEs, as well as optimization-based unlearning methods. The evaluation is multi-axis: forgetting effectiveness, retain preservation, feature selectivity, and robustness of the removed trace.

The main empirical picture is consistent. First, at the representation-extraction level, SCALPEL learns features that are more concentrated on the forget target and less entangled with retain-side background structure than reconstruction-only methods. The paper supports this with activation analyses and feature-level diagnostics, including residual-stream studies on Qwen. Second, as an unlearning intervention, SCALPEL achieves competitive forgetting relative to optimization-based baselines while causing less collateral damage on retained knowledge. Third, the norm-aware feature ranking improves over activation-only ranking, confirming that decoder magnitude matters for practical intervention quality. Fourth, ablations show that the contrastive setup is sensitive to positive/negative construction: treating other forget examples as negatives hurts selectivity because it breaks shared target structure.

The evidence is strongest in the controlled benchmark setting. The authors are explicit that this is not certified deletion and not a full solution to GDPR-style erasure. The evaluator has access to the full state ξ=(X,H), while the learner may not observe the latent context H. For a query z=(ξ,a), the evaluator defines a structural index σ_M(ξ,a), which records the active context class, the directly affected variable, the value class, and the first structural response delay. A mechanism memory μ is a finite set of tuples θ=(a,c,y,v,δ), and the answer map T_μ(z) is evaluated against the ground-truth mechanism map. The exact mechanism risk R_Q measures full answer equivalence under a fixed probe distribution Q; coordinate risks decompose errors over context, target, value, and delay, while missed and spurious mechanism risks give operational diagnostics. - Theory (MUtE*): define erasure as a map f: R^D→R^D producing Q=f(X) such that concept information is removed (perfect privacy / indistinguishability across class-conditionals in the erased space) while utility is maximized (minimal distortion / maximal retained information under the chosen criterion). The base architecture remains familiar: a recurrent belief encoder summarizes history, stochastic latent dynamics model future evolution, and planning is done by imagined rollouts in latent space. The key change is in the training signal. Instead of only optimizing observation, reward, and continuation prediction, the model is explicitly trained to separate imagined futures when different interventions lead to different control-relevant outcomes.

The central object is an action-conditioned latent summary, Φ(h_t, α), obtained by rolling out a candidate action sequence α from belief state h_t. Positive and negative pairs are defined by intervention outcome rather than pixel similarity. If two interventions are behaviorally different, their latent futures should be pushed apart even when their near-term observations are visually similar. If two trajectories are outcome-equivalent, their latent summaries should align despite nuisance visual variation. The paper highlights hard counterfactual negatives as the critical ingredient: these are exactly the cases where standard predictive objectives tend to collapse distinct futures.

The first component is preserving the VLM prior. The model uses shallow-layer protection to reduce destructive drift in early representations, and caption-data mixing to keep generic semantic anchors alive during robot continued pre-training. This is a deliberate choice: robot trajectories provide action supervision but poor coverage of open-world semantics, so relying on them alone would adapt faster but forget more. The second component is multi-head continuous action co-supervision. During continued pre-training, the same backbone is trained against multiple continuous action heads rather than a single head. The point is not to deploy all heads later, but to prevent the backbone from collapsing onto one head’s output geometry and to encourage head-agnostic action representations. Instead of fully separate robot-specific action spaces or an overly rigid global unification, the method aligns semantically comparable action dimensions where possible and uses a wrap-aware loss for periodic joints so angular variables are trained with the correct topology.
