9:T664,The core idea is to replace absolute scoring of a single review with anonymized pairwise battles plus structured dimension-wise judgments. The platform has four main design choices. First, annotators are not crowd workers; they are domain experts with AI paper-writing experience. The motivation is straightforward: judging review structure, field organization, and research directions requires field-level judgment. Second, the prompts are not synthetic topics. They are derived from real, highly cited surveys so that the tasks correspond to recognizable and academically meaningful review problems. Third, the platform performs expertise matching, assigning topics to reviewers within their area, which makes the setup closer to actual peer review. Fourth, the evaluation is decomposed into five literature-review-specific dimensions: D1 literature coverage, D2 claim support, D3 paper structure, D4 research suggestions quality, and D5 overall utility.

What distinguishes this from generic arena evaluation is that the battle format is not just a scaling trick for preference collection. It is used to operationalize subjective scientific judgment in a more disciplined way. The paper also adds a second layer: using LitReviewBench as calibration data for a low-cost evaluator. The authors show that off-the-shelf LLM-as-a-judge is substantially misaligned with expert preferences and can produce incorrect system rankings. Instead of treating the judge as a drop-in replacement for experts, they explicitly supervise or calibrate it with expert preference data. That is a more careful and more useful framing for evaluator design.a:T4fe,There are four headline results. First, the benchmark contains about 3k expert judgments, each with outcomes on all five dimensions rather than only an overall preference. Second, in decisive matches against human-written drafts, even the strongest current systems achieve only a 23.0% win rate on D5 overall utility, which indicates a large remaining gap to human performance. Third, the largest deficits appear on D3 paper structure and D4 research suggestions, with models trailing humans by roughly 200 rating points. This is an important diagnosis: the bottleneck is not just retrieval or citation accuracy, but coherent field organization and non-generic research insight. Fourth, agentic systems outperform pure language models across dimensions, but they do so at a substantial inference-time cost, averaging about 15× the token budget for the same topic.

The evaluator-calibration result is also practically important. Uncalibrated LLM judges correlate poorly with expert rankings; on D5 the reported alignment is only around ρ≈0.467. After calibration using LitReviewBench, the correlation rises to about ρ≈0.792.
