18:T516,The method operates in a strict query-only setting. Given only a query q, it uses no benchmark responses, preference labels, reference answers, human-written rubrics, or model training. The framework has three LLM roles: a rubric proposer, response generators, and a rubric-blind pairwise judge. A deterministic controller maintains the active rubric set, a tree structure, and evolution memory. At each step, the proposer emits either an Add patch for one atomic rubric or a Split patch for a bundled rubric. The central design choice is to avoid asking an LLM to compare two abstract rubric sets. Instead, each candidate rubric is tested in response space. The local pair starts from a strong answer satisfying the background rubrics and the candidate rubric, then minimally edits it to violate the candidate. This tests whether violating the rubric makes an otherwise similar answer worse. The alternative pair is generated from scratch: it first creates the strongest answer that satisfies the background rubrics while violating the candidate, then minimally repairs it to satisfy the candidate. This catches over-specific, optional, or style-only rubrics. The judge compares both pairs in both presentation orders. A candidate is accepted only if the answers satisfying it win in both comparisons.19:T543,The method decomposes evaluation into rubric generation and rubric-conditioned judging. A rubric is considered useful if it helps a judge assign a higher score to a preferred response than to a dispreferred one. Rubric quality is therefore directly trainable through discriminative utility, measured by the score gap induced by the rubric. The paper formulates rubrics as latent variables and derives a training objective from a variational-inference view. Training alternates between two phases. In the policy phase, a frozen rubric model generates rubrics for questions; the policy samples multiple responses; a frozen judge scores the responses under the rubric; GRPO updates the policy using these rubric-derived rewards. In the rubric phase, preference pairs are built through temporal contrast: current-policy responses are treated as preferred, earlier-checkpoint responses as dispreferred. The rubric generator samples candidate rubrics, and receives reward when the judge conditioned on that rubric separates the pair in the right direction with a larger gap. This differs from GPT-4.1-generated rubrics or labeled rubric training because it does not require an external rubric teacher. It also differs from scalar reward modeling because it learns an explicit, portable evaluation artifact rather than only a compressed preference score.1a:T49f,The experiments cover twelve benchmarks spanning math, code, general reasoning, knowledge, instruction following, and open-ended generation. On the OLMo3-Adapt suite, EvoLM reaches a 69.3% average, compared with 66.7% for GPT-4.1-prompted rubrics. A scalar reward model that performs strongly on held-out preference benchmarks, with 86.4% on RewardBench 2 and 80.8% on JudgeBench, produces the weakest downstream policy at 59.7%, trailing EvoLM by 9.6 points. This is a useful result: offline reward-model accuracy is not sufficient to predict RL usefulness. The paper also reports broader generalization. The co-evolution procedure extends to OLMo-3-7B. Learned rubrics transfer to unseen policies and judges without retraining. On out-of-distribution deep research tasks, EvoLM has higher pairwise agreement with expert human rubrics than GPT-4.1: 58.4% versus 52.5% on HealthBench, and 59.3% versus 51.0% on ResearchQA. - Proposer: sample d (min length constraint; truncate), then generate a question x and a reference answer y_ref. For tasks with factual grounding (health QA), x is constrained so the answer is derivable from d; for creative writing / instruction following, the response must build on d.
- Solver: sample multiple candidate answers {y_j} to x.
- Verifier: conditioned on (d, x, y_ref, {y_j}), generate a query-specific rubric (criteria + descriptions + optional gold labels + weights), then grade each candidate.
Stability / anti-hacking mechanisms:
1) Privileged access: the verifier sees d (and more context) while the solver only sees x, creating a generation–verification gap via information asymmetry.
2) Preference learning instead of scalar RL: select only the top- and bottom-scored answers and train with DPO, which is less sensitive to score calibration noise.
3) Diversity via corpus grounding: sampling diverse d helps prevent query mode collapse.
