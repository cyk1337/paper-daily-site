9:T736,CALM formulates controller-aware post-training as multi-task reinforcement learning over controller-induced MDPs. Each controller is implemented as an executable Python program in a code-as-controller style. The controller specifies the sequence of model calls, active roles, branching logic, aggregation, and verification steps. Training then samples from a distribution over controllers rather than optimizing for a single fixed protocol.

The central modeling assumption is compositionality. Different controllers may have different global structures, but they share recurring local modules such as CoT generation, verification, planning, critique, and related reasoning behaviors. If the model learns these modules well, it may generalize to unseen controller compositions instead of memorizing complete workflows. To study this, the paper explores four module-level training strategies under a turn-level GRPO objective: flat mixing of all turns, uniform weighting across module types, adaptive gradient-norm balancing, and single-role-per-step training. These variants are motivated by a real optimization issue: controllers differ in turn count and role composition, so some modules naturally dominate the gradient signal unless training explicitly compensates for that imbalance.

What makes the paper interesting is not merely that it uses multi-task RL, but that it treats controller structure as a source of reusable local behaviors and then asks how optimization should respect that structure. Once the goal is cross-controller transfer, module-level credit assignment and gradient balancing become first-order concerns. The evaluation setup is aligned with this hypothesis: the paper distinguishes transfer to held-out compositions built from familiar modules from broader controller shifts that alter role inventories or interfaces.a:T605,From the full introduction, the main empirical conclusion is clear: multi-controller training consistently outperforms single-controller baselines on transfer. Models trained on one native controller may do well on that workflow but degrade on unseen controller compositions, whereas CALM is more robust both on held-out compositions and on broader controller shifts. The experiments are conducted on mathematical reasoning tasks, which helps isolate controller generalization from large changes in task distribution.

The excerpt provided does not include the full result tables, so exact numbers are not available here. Still, the experimental design is already informative. The paper evaluates two distinct generalization regimes. First, it tests recombination: can the model handle new workflows assembled from familiar local reasoning primitives? Second, it tests stronger structural shift: does the shared policy remain effective when the set of roles or the interaction interface changes? It also compares the four module-level training strategies to understand whether simple mixing is enough or whether explicit balancing across modules improves transfer.

The most important thing to look for in the full experiments is not a single benchmark score, but the relationship between controller diversity during training and workflow transfer at deployment. In particular, the ablations should reveal whether weighting and gradient-balancing strategies prevent controllers with more turns or denser rewards from dominating optimization.b:T595,For a skim, start with the problem framing in the introduction. Then move to the method section and focus on three items: how controllers are represented as executable programs, how modules are defined, and how the four module-level training strategies differ in practice. Those details determine whether CALM is really learning reusable components or just jointly fitting several workflows.

In the experiments, prioritize the two transfer settings: held-out controller compositions and broader controller shifts. The first tests compositional generalization; the second tests robustness under stronger structural change. Then read the ablations comparing flat mixing, uniform weighting, adaptive gradient balancing, and single-role-per-step updates. If the paper includes controller code examples, they are worth a quick look because they help judge whether the module decomposition is natural and whether the framework could extend to tool use or agent settings.
