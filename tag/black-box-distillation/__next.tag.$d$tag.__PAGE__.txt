9:T7c6,The method has two parts. First, the paper formalizes behavioral indistinguishability as an empirical, bounded claim rather than an absolute property. A student S is (epsilon, q, t, A)-behaviorally indistinguishable from teacher T if no adversary in class A, with query budget q and compute budget t, can distinguish their outputs with advantage greater than epsilon over a specified prompt distribution. This framing matters because it forces the evaluation to state what prompts are considered, what attacker is allowed, and what resources are available.

Second, the authors instantiate this definition with a multi-view evaluation pipeline instead of a single metric. They use: (1) response-only discriminators to detect stylistic or surface artifacts; (2) prompt-response discriminators to test whether the answer is teacher-like in context; (3) semantic similarity metrics; (4) policy-level agreement measures for safety and refusal behavior; and (5) a pairwise teacher-identification judge that sees two answers to the same prompt and decides which one is more likely from the teacher. They also apply A/B-swap consistency filtering to reduce judge position bias.

The empirical setup uses two model families, Qwen and Llama. For each family, they compare the teacher, the original base student, and a LoRA-distilled student trained on black-box teacher responses. The choice of LoRA is important: it reflects a realistic low-cost distillation regime and also means the paper is measuring how much behavior can be transferred through limited-capacity adaptation, not through full-parameter imitation.

A further practical component is data acquisition under query budget. The paper compares disagreement-guided sampling against stratified random sampling. The result is notable: disagreement-guided acquisition does not consistently outperform the simpler coverage-oriented baseline, suggesting that diversity and category coverage remain strong defaults for behavioral distillation.a:T71c,The experiments are built around a controlled 5,000-prompt behavioral probe suite covering general QA, reasoning, coding, summarization, style/format control, ambiguous prompts, safety-boundary prompts, instruction conflict, domain-technical questions, and robustness perturbations. The authors explicitly position this as a probe distribution rather than a natural deployment distribution.

Three findings stand out. First, LoRA distillation improves semantic similarity and makes learned discriminators less effective, so black-box response distillation does transfer a substantial portion of observable behavior. Second, this does not imply behavioral indistinguishability. The clearest number comes from the pairwise teacher-identification setup: for the Qwen family, distinguishing advantage drops from 0.158 for the base student to 0.081 after LoRA distillation. That is a meaningful reduction, but still clearly above zero. Third, residual distinguishability is concentrated in specific categories rather than spread uniformly. Style/format prompts, robustness perturbations, and domain-technical prompts remain the easiest places to tell teacher and student apart. This suggests that distillation learns answer content more readily than stable response policy and boundary behavior.

The query-budget experiments also matter. Under limited API calls, disagreement-guided acquisition does not consistently beat stratified random sampling. That is a useful practical result: if the goal is behavioral transfer, broad prompt coverage may be more reliable than more elaborate active-selection heuristics.

Overall, the evidence is cumulative rather than tied to one metric.
