Near-parallel means that examples remain aligned by source identifier and task intent, while target-language content may be rewritten when literal translation would invalidate the task. The pipeline treats each record as a structured instruction-following instance. A sample-level policy first routes the example to either field-wise machine translation or task-level localization. For translatable examples, a field-level policy decides whether each field should be translated or preserved, which is important for labels, URLs, code, paths, required output strings, and other fixed artifacts. For examples that depend on linguistic form, such as grammatical errors, rhyme, alliteration, word length, acronym formation, or phonological constraints, the system reconstructs a task-equivalent target-language instance. The decision, rewriting, and validation modules use an 8-bit quantized google/gemma-4-31B-it with specialized prompts.19:T6ae,The method has two coupled pieces: ConsensusBench and ConsensusPR. The central hypothesis is that correct final answers are usually reached through a small set of stable intermediate outcomes, such as solved subproblems, validated constraints, case splits, or applied lemmas. Different correct trajectories may vary in wording, but they often converge on these checkpoints. The paper calls them consensus nodes. Construction proceeds by sampling N rollouts per prompt, keeping the correct ones, extracting intermediate conclusions from those trajectories, and clustering semantically equivalent conclusions into shared nodes. This is an important design choice: the target is not to imitate one canonical solution path, but to recover objective checkpoints that multiple successful paths pass through. Based on these nodes, the authors define ConsensusPR, a rule-based process reward that densifies the original terminal reward along the reasoning path. They also introduce three evaluation metrics: Final Answer Accuracy for endpoint correctness, Node Coverage Rate for how many consensus nodes a trajectory reaches, and Tokens per Node for reasoning efficiency. The metric design is tightly aligned with the failure modes they identify: NCR captures partial progress, while TPN distinguishes concise reasoning from verbose but uninformative chains. A practical advantage is that ConsensusPR plugs into existing GRPO and DAPO/RLVR pipelines without training a separate process reward model. Unlike PRMs, which try to score the quality of each step, ConsensusPR only checks whether the trajectory reaches verifiable intermediate states. That makes the signal more discrete, more objective, and easier to scale.1a:T452,The experimental story has four main parts. First, the paper presents a case study of four reward-mismatch types under outcome-only supervision; this is not just motivation, but the basis for the benchmark and metric design. Second, ConsensusBench is used as an evaluation asset to show that models with similar final-answer accuracy can differ substantially in node coverage and reasoning efficiency. Third, ConsensusPR is integrated into GRPO and DAPO-style RL training, where it improves not only final performance but also process quality, measured by higher node coverage and lower tokens per node, while maintaining training stability. Fourth, the paper emphasizes the efficiency advantage over PRM pipelines: no human process annotation and no separate reward-model training. EvoHarnessBench defines the harness as the set of tools an agent can call, the procedural skills it can reuse, and the specialist agents it can coordinate with. Each harness stream is a sequence of cumulative stages H1 ⊆ H2 ⊆ ... ⊆ HT, so later stages only add capabilities and never remove old ones. Streams are built along three axes: tools, skills, and agents.

Two design choices matter. First, each task is assigned to the earliest stage at which it becomes solvable, and it must require at least one newly introduced capability. This helps separate adaptation to new capabilities from retention of old ones. Second, capabilities are released from common core capabilities toward the long tail, which is meant to mimic real harness growth in deployed systems.

The benchmark supports two evaluation modes. In deployment evaluation, no experience is carried across stages; each harness version is evaluated independently. This isolates the direct effect of harness expansion: does a larger capability pool help, or does it mostly distract? In self-evolving adaptation evaluation, persistent artifacts such as memory, prompts, or learned skills can be updated within each stage and then carried forward, while the underlying model weights remain fixed. This tests whether accumulated experience helps or hinders adaptation under a growing harness.

The benchmark is reasonably large: 17 controlled harness streams, each with 3 to 6 cumulative stages, 802 unique tasks, 1,510 axis-specific evaluation examples, 520 executable tools, 42 latent reference skills, and 62 specialist agents. The paper evaluates representative single-agent and multi-agent systems under both modes.1d:T540,The empirical message is mostly diagnostic. First, harness expansion is not free. Agents can lose competence on tasks they previously solved even though the underlying model is unchanged and the required capabilities are still present. That is the benchmark’s evidence for harness-induced forgetting. Second, self-evolving adaptation is inconsistent. Gains vary substantially across stages, axes, and environments, which suggests that simply accumulating memory or prompts is not a reliable answer to harness evolution. Third, retention and adaptation often pull in opposite directions: methods that preserve earlier competence better can be worse at exploiting newly introduced capabilities, while methods that adapt aggressively can sacrifice old-task performance.
