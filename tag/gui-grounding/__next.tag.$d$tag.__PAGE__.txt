9:T74c,The core contribution is GUI-Primitives, a 994-item benchmark organized as 497 contrastive instruction pairs over seven GUI spatial primitives: left/right, above/below, containment, alignment, proximity, list ordinal, and occlusion. In each pair, the screenshot and anchor element stay fixed while the relation expression changes, so the correct target flips between two designated candidates. This minimal-pair construction is the key idea. It suppresses screenshot salience, centrality bias, and fixed-answer shortcuts: a model that always clicks the more salient element can only get one item in the pair correct.

The dataset also includes lexical variation beyond the relation term in 253 of the 497 pairs, changing verbs, head nouns, or anchor quoting, which reduces template memorization. A 196-item subset is validated by five annotators, with Fleiss kappa = 0.94 for well-formedness and kappa = 0.79 for target selection, giving the benchmark a reasonably strong annotation foundation.

Methodologically, the paper goes beyond strict point-in-box accuracy and performs candidate-level analysis. Errors are split into two types: selecting the contrastive wrong candidate versus predicting a point outside both candidate regions. This distinction matters because it changes the diagnosis. A low score does not necessarily mean the model misunderstood the relation word; it may simply have failed to localize either candidate reliably.

The paper also evaluates three training-free interventions: Set-of-Mark prompting, primitive-aware chain-of-thought, and activation steering. SoM explicitly marks the two candidates and therefore acts as an oracle upper bound on candidate discrimination rather than a deployable solution. CoT tests whether explicit reasoning helps. Activation steering tests whether internal representations can be nudged without retraining.a:T84b,The empirical picture is consistent and fairly stark.

First, absolute performance is poor. Across 19 VLMs, the best model, Claude Opus 4.7, reaches only 31% strict point-in-box accuracy on the human-clean subset; the abstract also states that no model exceeds 32% overall. So even on tightly controlled GUI spatial primitives, current models remain far from human-level grounding.

Second, all tested models fall below the two-candidate reference level of 0.50 on four primitives: containment, occlusion, alignment, and proximity. Candidate-level analysis explains why: the dominant error is not choosing the opposite candidate, but predicting outside both candidate boxes. Depending on the model, off-candidate predictions account for 60% to 92% of errors, and on the real desktop screenshot subset this rises to 96.2%. The main bottleneck therefore looks more like candidate localization failure than relation-word confusion.

Third, the benchmark tracks downstream grounding. For the ten models also evaluated on ScreenSpot-Pro, GUI-Primitives accuracy correlates with downstream grounding accuracy at Spearman rho = +0.74 with p = 0.015. The paper also reports item-level logistic regression over n = 15,810 model-item pairs with pseudo-R^2 = 0.40, which supports the claim that the benchmark is diagnostically meaningful rather than synthetic in a disconnected way.

Fourth, only Set-of-Mark helps substantially. Marking the two candidates improves selection accuracy by 35 to 57 points, raising GPT-5 from 30% to 87% and OS-Atlas from 10% to 52%. Primitive-aware CoT and activation steering do not produce significant gains. This is a useful diagnosis: once the candidate set is made explicit, models can often discriminate between candidates much better than their raw screenshot-grounding scores suggest.

Fifth, shortcut controls behave mostly as expected. Blank canvas, shuffled instructions, and heavy blur reduce performance, indicating that the benchmark is not solved by pure template matching.
