9:T75e,The work has two technical components.

First, FBHM. The dataset is built along two orthogonal axes. The functionality axis contains 25 curated multimodal hate mechanisms grouped into five broader families: visual format and imagery type, textual obfuscation and lexical evasion, structural composition and visual metaphor, pragmatic inference and sentiment mismatch, and non-hateful/counterspeech contrasts. The target axis contains 10 protected communities. For each base meme, the authors keep the underlying image-text structure and rhetorical mechanism fixed and swap only the target community, producing controlled variants. This enables near-causal comparisons: same mechanism across targets, or same target across mechanisms. The final dataset has 5,000 memes from 500 base items, with 500 examples reserved as a steering set and 4,500 as test data. Annotation is expert-driven, and the reported Cohen’s kappa of 0.84 on independently reviewed base items suggests solid label consistency.

Second, LSV, or learnable steering vectors. Instead of updating model weights, the method learns a set of layer-wise continuous intervention vectors for a frozen VLM. Each transformer layer receives a steering vector matching the hidden dimension, together with a learned scalar coefficient controlling intervention strength at that layer. The optimization objective is framed as a causal-style intervention on internal representations using the small steering set. The key design choice is to move the model in activation space rather than parameter space. Compared with PEFT/LoRA, this reduces the risk of overwriting the original task behavior; compared with in-context learning, it provides a persistent, layer-aware modification rather than a prompt-local effect. The learned per-layer scaling is important because the method does not assume all layers should be perturbed equally.a:T4de,The main empirical result is a severe generalization collapse. VLMs that perform strongly on standard hateful-meme datasets such as FHM and MAMI drop to near-random performance on FBHM, indicating that they rely heavily on benchmark-specific heuristics rather than robust multimodal reasoning. Under the low-resource adaptation setting of 500 steering samples, standard few-shot ICL and PEFT baselines fail to recover much of this gap. LSV, in contrast, raises FBHM performance to roughly 74–75 Macro-F1 while preserving performance on the original FHM and MAMI benchmarks.

The more interesting part of the evaluation is the slicing. Because FBHM is controlled by construction, the paper can report functionality-wise and target-wise disparities rather than only a single aggregate score. This matters: the failure is not just “the model is bad at hateful memes,” but “the model is selectively weak on certain multimodal mechanisms,” such as obfuscation, sentiment mismatch, or metaphorical composition. The preservation result is also important. LSV is presented not as a stronger task learner, but as a targeted representational correction that improves out-of-distribution functional robustness without obvious in-domain regression.b:T4aa,Read the paper in three passes.

First, What-If World builds a contrastive benchmark around physically meaningful interventions. Two domain experts define and merge intervention variables into six primitives spanning autonomous driving and robotic manipulation. Environment-side primitives include surface friction, material/medium, and obstacle configuration. Agent-side primitives include spatial alignment, force/degree, and temporal sequencing. Each test case is a paired triplet: the same initial frame x0 plus two prompts that differ in exactly one semantic variable. Construction proceeds in three steps: filter suitable clips from nuScenes or DROID; extract the frame right before the action starts and pair it with shared scene descriptors; then author a prompt pair that changes only the intervention variable. The goal is to approximate ceteris paribus through prompt design so that output differences can be attributed to the intervention. Second, the APEO evaluation framework decomposes success into four criteria: Adherence, whether the intervention was executed; Physics, whether the dynamics are valid; Environment, whether the non-intervened scene stays stable; and Outcome, whether the paired outputs diverge as the intervention predicts. The first three are scored in both single-video and paired modes, while Outcome is inherently paired-only. This decomposition is useful because it separates different failure modes: the intervention may be ignored, executed without sufficient divergence, or confounded by scene drift.
