5:T6f3,The paper first diagnoses the mechanism, then proposes a training-free fix. The diagnosis centers on the claim that standard dual-encoder similarity s(I,T)=<f(I),g(T)> behaves like soft pooling over atomic concept evidence rather than logical execution. Using one- and two-concept queries on COCO, images are partitioned into four quadrants: Both, A-only, B-only, and Neither. The authors then inspect mean scores and rankings under different logical forms. Anti-monotone queries such as ¬A∧¬B systematically prefer the Both quadrant, which should be the least preferred one. This shows that negation does not actually alter the ranking mechanism. The paper further analyzes the text embedding into dominant concept-related directions and weaker operator-related residuals, arguing that operator information exists but is attenuated or poorly aligned with image evidence under dot-product scoring.

Based on that diagnosis, the paper proposes factored inference and LCSE. The idea is to separate evidence extraction from constraint execution. A frozen VLM is used to score each atomic concept independently, producing concept-level evidence. Boolean operators are then executed outside the model using the parsed query structure. To avoid discarding useful non-logical information captured by the original similarity score, LCSE performs logic-constrained score editing rather than replacing the score entirely. It adaptively edits the holistic similarity only when the query requires non-mean aggregation. This preserves standard retrieval behavior where no logical constraint is present. The trade-off is clear: no retraining and better semantic control, at the cost of requiring query parsing and currently focusing on Boolean compositions over one or two visual concepts.6:T549,The experiments have three main parts. First, the paper shows on COCO val2017 that CLIP-like models can detect whether a concept is present, yet fail to execute negation in ranking. A representative result reported in the text is that concept detection reaches about AUC 0.88, while the rankings induced by “a X” and “no X” have Spearman correlation around 0.94. So the model changes the text embedding, but not enough to change the ordering. Second, the paper introduces FACTOR-Bench, built from operator-contrastive caption pairs with validated concept presence and absence labels, to test whether a model follows Boolean semantics rather than merely tracking mentioned concepts. Third, LCSE is evaluated on FACTOR-Bench, NegBench, and retrieval-retention settings against standard CLIP, SigLIP, and fine-tuned variants. The reported pattern is that LCSE improves constraint-sensitive retrieval while preserving ordinary retrieval better than training-based fixes. The paper also argues that fine-tuning on negation-heavy data often harms standard retrieval because it tries to repair an interface problem at the representation level.

Exact benchmark gains should be taken from Section 5 and the appendix rather than inferred.7:T752,The core contribution is a double-gate evaluation protocol. For each compositional instance of depth k, the authors define the required atomic facts, multiple paraphrased probes for each atom, and aligned sub-questions that match how each atom appears inside the chain. An example passes the atomic gate only if every atomic fact is answered correctly and consistently across paraphrases. It then passes the sub-question gate only if every aligned sub-question is answered correctly in isolation. Residual composition failure is measured only on examples that pass both gates but still fail on the main compositional question.

This changes the estimand. Instead of attributing the entire single-hop to multi-hop drop to composition, the protocol conditions on stable atomic access and explicit local verbalization. The paper then decomposes post-training gains into three channels: Δatom for atomic stability, Δcomp for residual composition at matched atoms, and Δdepth for the depth at which residual failure first exceeds 50%. This decomposition is the main conceptual tool of the paper.

The benchmark is D4v2, a temporal compositional dataset with 390 main questions and 2,490 atomic sub-questions spanning four task families and depths 2 through 11. The evaluation covers six open-weight models in the 7B–13B range across several post-training recipes: base, RLHF, SFT reasoning-trace distillation, and native outcome-verified RL. A particularly strong part of the design is the controlled same-base LoRA study comparing SFT-answer, SFT-trace, and GRPO-style training, which helps isolate objective-level effects from base-model confounds. The authors also note an important caveat: residual composition failure is still an upper bound on pure composition failure, because formatting issues, distractor attention, and decoding-path degeneracy can also contribute.8:T636,There are four results worth retaining. First, base models are mostly filtered out by the atomic gate rather than by composition. The paper reports that base models admit fewer than five cases at any depth, which means many apparent multi-hop failures are really failures of stable atomic access.

Second, once atomic stability is matched within 2 percentage points, residual composition failure can still vary from 0% to 47% at depth 2 depending on the post-training recipe. This is the empirical signature of composition collapse: nearly identical atomic knowledge, sharply different composition behavior. Under matched data and compute, outcome-verified RL substantially outperforms SFT trace distillation on this metric.

Third, training on a specific composition depth closes much of the residual failure at that depth but transfers only weakly to nearby held-out depths. That suggests the learned behavior is not a broadly extrapolating compositional procedure.

Fourth, enabling chain-of-thought reduces residual failure by roughly 3–5×. This is an important clue about mechanism: a large part of the bottleneck lies in generation-time computation, not only in static knowledge representation. The model may already possess the required facts, yet still fail to assemble them without enough inference-time workspace.

Because the paper combines cross-model comparisons with same-base LoRA interventions, the results support a stronger claim than simple leaderboard differences: training objectives themselves can alter the composition channel after atomic knowledge is controlled.9:T4d1,This is labeled skim, but it deserves a careful skim if you care about reasoning evaluation or post-training analysis.
