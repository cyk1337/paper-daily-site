18:T675,PGB Curriculum has two main components. The first is gradient-based learning-unit discovery. Motivated by an NTK-style view, the paper treats sample-level gradients as approximate descriptors of local optimization effects, projects them into a lower-dimensional representation, and clusters samples based on those representations. The resulting curriculum units are not grouped by task type or difficulty, but by whether they induce similar parameter-update directions under the current policy. The rationale is simple: group-level scheduling is meaningful only if samples inside a group have reasonably consistent optimization effects.

The second component is a predictive multi-armed bandit. Instead of using recent average advantage or success rate as a direct estimate of utility, the method trains a lightweight temporal predictor that jointly models external learning signals and internal policy states to forecast each group’s future utility. Those predicted utilities are then converted into a Boltzmann sampling distribution, increasing the probability of high-utility groups. The key distinction is that the scheduler predicts future usefulness rather than smoothing past usefulness, and it conditions on policy state rather than only on visible reward-like signals.

The method is designed to avoid two common failure modes. Some samples are already mastered and provide little marginal gain if oversampled. Others are currently too hard, producing nearly indistinguishable rewards and weak gradients. PGB tries to keep sampling mass on the region that is most useful for the current policy, and to move that region as the policy improves.19:T48d,The empirical results are straightforward. Relative to uniform-sampling GRPO, PGB improves in-domain performance by 8.8% to 14.3% and out-of-domain performance by 14.7% to 16.3%. Relative to existing curriculum baselines, it gains another 4.9% to 9.2%. The paper also reports better training efficiency: PGB reaches the final performance level of the GRPO baseline earlier in training, so the benefit is not only higher final quality but also faster progress.

The OOD gains are notable. They suggest the method is not merely oversampling easy in-domain prompts, but may actually be improving the quality of the learning signal. The provided excerpt does not include the full list of model sizes, datasets, or all ablation tables, but two ablations are especially important to inspect in the full paper: replacing gradient-based grouping with difficulty/category-based grouping, and replacing the temporal predictor with a retrospective average-based bandit. If both hurt performance, that would support the claim that the gains come from the combination of optimization-aware grouping and predictive scheduling, rather than from adding a generic curriculum module.1a:T468,The setup modifies DeepRD into a controlled next-step proof prediction task. Premises are written in natural language over entities and attributes, and the model must select or generate the next inference step that can lead to the goal. Lookahead is defined as the number of future proof steps the model must search through to identify the correct immediate step. Training supervises only the single next-step output, so the measured capability is internal computation during one forward pass rather than explicit CoT expansion. The curriculum jointly increases lookahead and the attribute vocabulary. This joint curriculum works better than increasing lookahead alone, likely because it prevents the task from remaining too templatic and makes the difficulty ramp smoother. The paper compares curriculum training with direct training at target depth, varies curriculum step size, tests several model sizes, and contrasts pretrained and randomly initialized models. A central technical point is that the curriculum step must be small enough; if the next stage is too far beyond the model’s current competence, learning stalls.1b:T400,The headline controlled result is large: at matched compute, Qwen3-0.6B reaches 98% accuracy on problems up to lookahead 256 with curriculum training, compared with 45% without curriculum. Achieved lookahead grows with compute according to a saturating exponential fit, indicating diminishing returns and a possible maximum intrinsic lookahead for each model. Scale matters: Pythia-410M, 1.4B, and 2.8B have estimated maximum intrinsic lookaheads of 51, 82, and 235 respectively. Initialization matters as well: under a 1.5M PFLOP budget, Pythia-1.4B reaches lookahead 63 from pretrained weights, but only 23 from random initialization. Curriculum granularity is also decisive: Qwen3-0.6B stalls at lookahead 128 with step size 64, while step size 8 reaches 256. Transfer experiments on 11 natural-language reasoning benchmarks show consistent gains over non-curriculum training at matched compute, with the largest improvements on multi-step deductive tasks; Qwen3-1.7B gains 14.3 points on ProofWriter OWA at lookahead 48.1c:Tace,ATC 的基本设定是，在输入和最终答案之间插入若干连续的 abstract thought token，但训练时并不给这些 token 任何人工语义标签，也不要求它们对应自然语言步骤。模型只对最终任务目标负责。关键做法不是“加 token”本身，而是围绕这些 token 设计一套逐级加难的课程。

课程的核心思想是，把原本难以直接学会的复杂任务拆成一串分布 D1, D2, ...，每一阶段都比上一阶段稍难，并且需要多一个 latent thought step 来承接更复杂的中间计算。模型先在较简单分布上学会利用较短的 thought chain；当达到阈值后，再进入更难阶段，增加问题复杂度和可用的 latent reasoning 深度。这样做的目的，是让后期阶段的某个 thought token 可以把前期阶段已经学到的表示当作“现成子程序”继续使用，而不是从头在最终监督下硬学长程组合计算。

理论部分聚焦 parity 任务，并回应 Lotfi 等人的 conjecture。作者分析一层 attention 模型在 ATC 下的梯度动力学，证明在一定条件下，一次足够大的梯度更新就会让注意力分数集中到最近的 thought token 上，从而出现所谓 attention simplicity bias：模型倾向于依赖最容易提供预测信号的中间 token。这个结果的意义不是 parity 本身，而是说明 curriculum 可以把原本 one-shot 难学的高阶函数，转化成一串局部可学的中间表示学习问题。

但作者也很诚实地指出，理论假设里“早期阶段学到的表示会被后续阶段保留”在实践中并不会自动成立。为此他们提出 curriculum backtracking：每推进到新阶段后，都重新在所有已完成阶段上评估；如果某个旧阶段准确率跌破阈值，就退回去继续训练，直到旧能力恢复。这个机制有点像显式的 retention control，目的是防止 latent thought 的语义漂移和灾难性遗忘。

另一个关键工程点是训练长 latent chain 的成本。若对所有 thought step 做完整反传，激活存储和计算量会随链长线性甚至更糟地增长。作者因此引入 truncated backpropagation through time，只在局部窗口内回传梯度，让更长的抽象思维链可训练。这里的取舍很明确：牺牲一部分全局梯度精确性，换取可扩展的链长和更稳定的训练。

与已有方法相比，ATC 不同于显式 scratchpad 监督，因为它不规定中间格式；也不同于单纯 curriculum learning，因为它不是只在输入难度上做排序，而是把课程和 latent thought depth 绑定起来；也不同于直接加 latent token 的做法，因为它额外处理了表示保留和训练成本这两个常见失败点。1d:Tae8,ATC inserts continuous abstract thought tokens between the input and the final answer, but does not assign them any human-readable meaning and does not supervise them with target traces. The model is trained only on the end task. The key idea is not merely adding latent tokens; it is coupling them to a staged curriculum.

The curriculum defines a sequence of increasingly difficult distributions. At each stage, the task becomes slightly harder and the model is allowed or required to use one more latent thought step. The intention is that later stages can build on representations learned in earlier stages, effectively reusing prior thought tokens as intermediate subroutines. This turns a hard one-shot learning problem into a sequence of locally learnable representation-learning problems.

The theoretical analysis focuses on parity and addresses the conjecture from Lotfi et al. The authors study gradient dynamics in a one-layer attention model trained with ATC and show that, under suitable assumptions, a single sufficiently large gradient step can make attention concentrate on the most recent thought token. This formalizes the attention simplicity bias in this setting: the model prefers intermediate tokens that provide the easiest predictive signal. The point is not parity itself, but the mechanism by which curriculum can make otherwise hard high-degree functions learnable through staged latent computation.

The paper is careful about the gap between theory and practice. A key theoretical assumption is that representations learned in earlier curriculum phases remain intact and usable later. Empirically, this does not happen automatically. To address that, the authors introduce curriculum backtracking: after advancing to a new stage, the model is reevaluated on all previously completed stages, and if performance on an earlier stage drops below threshold, training returns to that stage until the capability is restored. This acts as an explicit retention mechanism against semantic drift and catastrophic forgetting in latent thoughts.

A second engineering contribution is truncated backpropagation through time for long latent chains. Full backprop across all thought steps makes activation storage and compute grow with chain length. Truncated BPTT limits gradient propagation to local windows, trading some gradient fidelity for tractable training of deeper latent reasoning chains.

Relative to prior work, ATC differs from explicit scratchpad supervision because it does not prescribe an intermediate format; from plain curriculum learning because it ties curriculum progression to latent reasoning depth; and from naive latent-token insertion because it explicitly handles representation retention and training cost, which are major practical failure modes.1e:T85c,The experiments span theory-driven validation and three task families: parity, graph reasoning, and multi-digit arithmetic. The goal is not a single benchmark score, but to test whether ATC actually induces useful abstract thought tokens and whether those thoughts remain stable under curriculum progression.

On parity, used as a canonical hard-to-learn setting, direct training on the full target distribution struggles, whereas ATC substantially improves learning. The attention patterns also align better with the theory: later stages rely heavily on the most recent thought token, consistent with the predicted attention simplicity bias. The most informative evidence here is not just final accuracy, but the evolution of attention and learning trajectories across curriculum stages.

On graph tasks and multi-digit arithmetic, ATC outperforms fixed-difficulty training and other baselines in sample efficiency and final task performance. The ablations are especially important. Removing curriculum backtracking causes earlier-stage performance to degrade as the curriculum advances, showing that latent representations are indeed overwritten by later training. Removing truncated BPTT preserves fuller gradients in principle, but training cost rises quickly and chain length becomes hard to scale. With backtracking, the model maintains competence on earlier stages while extending the latent chain; with truncated BPTT, deeper latent reasoning becomes trainable under practical resource limits.

Three empirical lessons stand out. First, latent thoughts are not automatically useful; they need a staged training signal. Second, retention is central: without an explicit mechanism, longer curricula can erase the very intermediate representations they are supposed to build on. Third, the bottleneck in long latent reasoning is not only expressivity but also memory and optimization stability, so engineering choices are part of the method rather than implementation detail. For a deeper read, two questions are worth pursuing: whether the learned latent thoughts transfer beyond a single curriculum, and whether the ATC recipe can scale from synthetic reasoning tasks to realistic language pretraining.20:T8ce,The method has three core pieces. First, a unified parameter space. Instead of maintaining separate generator and solver models, the authors use one base model, switched by role-specific system prompts into a Scaffolder or a Learner. The Scaffolder proposes new tasks conditioned on historical outcomes, and the Learner attempts them in an external environment. The motivation is straightforward: if both roles share weights, improvements acquired by the Learner through RL can directly improve the Scaffolder’s ability to place tasks at the right difficulty.

Second, difficulty alignment through the so-called Stretch Zone. The paper does not optimize for “harder is better.” It tries to jointly satisfy task validity, learnability, and behavioral stability. In the formulation, a domain verifier provides a validity signal, a difficulty reward favors tasks near the Learner’s boundary, and a replay term reinforces likelihood on verified successful trajectories. This trade-off matters. If the generator only pushes difficulty upward, it can drift into unsolvable regions. If training only replays successful trajectories, it collapses back into the comfort zone.

Third, an alternating dual-loop update scheme. One loop updates the Scaffolder for difficulty alignment; the other updates the Learner for task solving. The implementation is not a single differentiable end-to-end loss. The Scaffolder generates adaptive constraints from historical feedback, such as bargaining bottom lines or resource constraints in optimization tasks. After verifier filtering, these tasks are passed to the Learner, which is optimized with a GRPO-style group-relative reward objective. At the end of each epoch, verified successful trajectories are replayed with an SFT-style objective to stabilize behavior and reduce drift.

Relative to prior work, the main differences are clear. It does not rely on a frozen environment generator. It is not a teacher-student pipeline. It is not standard curriculum learning with a manually scheduled difficulty ladder. And it is not classic two-agent self-play with fully separate models.
