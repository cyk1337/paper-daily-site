a:T812,The method combines a theoretical analysis of entropy dynamics with a simple rejection-sampling controller. The theory starts from a simplified policy-gradient objective without clipping or importance weighting and studies how predictive entropy changes after an update. The key result is that entropy change is negatively related to advantage, and the effect is amplified when the model is already highly confident. Intuitively, positive-advantage samples reinforce selected tokens, sharpen the distribution, and reduce entropy; negative-advantage samples do the opposite and tend to flatten the distribution. In high-confidence regions, this sharpening or flattening effect becomes stronger, which explains why entropy collapse can become self-reinforcing once the policy has already narrowed.

Entrocraft uses this result operationally. Instead of adding entropy regularization to the loss, it performs rejection sampling on rollout samples before the policy update. If the observed entropy is below the target schedule, it preferentially keeps samples whose advantages push entropy upward and filters out samples that would reduce entropy further. If entropy is above target, it does the reverse. In effect, it biases the advantage distribution so that the resulting gradient steers the policy toward the desired entropy level. Because the intervention happens directly at the sample-distribution level, the controller reacts quickly, often within a few update steps, rather than relying on a weakly coupled regularization term. The paper emphasizes two practical properties: it is drop-in for existing RL pipelines, and it is advantage-estimator-agnostic.

A notable aspect is that the paper is about crafting an entropy curve, not merely preserving entropy. It treats entropy scheduling similarly to learning-rate scheduling and compares different schedule families. The main empirical conclusion is that linear entropy annealing works best: keep exploration sufficiently high early on, then gradually tighten the policy later for stability and generalization.b:T664,The experimental story is centered on long-horizon RL rather than short-run reward gains. The paper reports that Entrocraft extends the useful training horizon by up to 4× before performance plateaus, which directly supports the anti-saturation claim. It also reports a strong generalization result: a 4B model with Entrocraft surpasses an 8B baseline. On diversity-sensitive evaluation, pass@K improves by about 50% over the baseline, consistent with the claim that better entropy control improves exploration coverage rather than merely sharpening one preferred trajectory.

From the paper structure and the introduction, the key experiments likely include four layers. First, entropy-curve tracking: regularization- and clipping-based baselines may raise entropy, but their curves drift or oscillate over long training, while Entrocraft follows the target schedule more closely. Second, schedule ablations: constant entropy, increasing schedules, decreasing schedules, and linear annealing are compared, with linear annealing performing best. Third, compatibility across RL algorithms: the method is presented as a drop-in and advantage-estimator-agnostic, so at least GRPO/GSPO-style settings are likely covered. Fourth, empirical validation of the theory: entropy collapse is more severe in high-confidence regions, and biasing the advantage distribution changes subsequent entropy dynamics in the predicted direction.
