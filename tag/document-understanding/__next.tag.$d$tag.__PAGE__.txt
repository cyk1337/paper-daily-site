5:T86b,The paper has two technical components: a controlled perturbation benchmark and an evaluation protocol designed to separate visual reading from language-driven rewriting. The authors sample raw text from mC4 in English, Chinese, and Korean, apply character-level perturbations directly in text space, and then render the perturbed text into single-page document images. This is a strong design choice because the ground truth becomes the perturbed text itself, making it possible to tell whether a model transcribes what is present or silently corrects it.

They use three perturbation families. Scramble preserves the first and last character while shuffling the middle, probing reliance on lexical priors. Random substitution replaces characters with random same-script alternatives, creating severe corruption. Visual substitution replaces characters with visually similar ones, probing the competition between visual evidence and language prior. English and Korean are perturbed at the word level; Chinese uses non-overlapping 4-character windows. The perturbation rate is controlled, with clean documents retained as a baseline.

The evaluation spans three system classes: general-purpose VLMs, OCR-specialized VLMs, and traditional OCR pipelines. This taxonomy is central to the paper’s argument because it roughly orders systems by the strength of their language prior. General VLMs have broad multimodal and text pretraining and are most likely to hallucinate corrections. OCR-specialized VLMs are constrained by document extraction fine-tuning. Traditional OCR systems operate much closer to character recognition and have little generative prior. The authors also avoid adding anti-correction instructions to the prompt, so the model’s default behavior is exposed rather than suppressed.

Beyond aggregate error rates, the paper probes Qwen3-VL-4B layer by layer. The mechanistic claim is that rewriting tends to occur when the internal representation of a perturbed word remains close to the representation of the original clean word. They also report a word-length effect: short words are much more likely to be rewritten than longer ones.6:T606,The benchmark contains 1,455 single-page documents across English, Chinese, and Korean, and the evaluation covers 15 systems. The main result is clean and consistent: general-purpose VLMs degrade the most under perturbation, with up to 4.5 WER points on English; OCR-specialized VLMs degrade by about 0.2 to 2 points; traditional OCR pipelines degrade by less than 0.6 points. This strongly supports the paper’s central claim that stronger language priors increase the risk of sacrificing faithfulness on imperfect text.

A particularly important finding is the non-local effect. Corrupting only around 5% of words can increase errors on the untouched remainder by 5–10x relative to the clean baseline for general VLMs. That means the issue is not just local character confusion. The model’s page-level generation process is being pulled toward a different textual interpretation, which is much more concerning for unified document models.

The multilingual results are also informative. The same qualitative pattern holds across English, Chinese, and Korean, but the most damaging perturbation type differs by script. This suggests that rewriting is shaped both by language priors and by script-specific visual and tokenization structure. The probing analysis on Qwen3-VL-4B further shows that rewriting is more likely when the perturbed token remains representationally close to the original word.
