a:T42f,Reasoning Jury is a two-phase evaluation system for step-segmented reasoning traces marked with [STEP-x] tokens. Phase 1 is independent judgment: a panel of jurors from diverse model families each read the problem and reasoning trace independently, and output a structured defect list including description, affected step IDs, severity, and supporting evidence. This ensures no anchoring effect and decorrelated judgment biases across jurors. Phase 2 is consensus building, with two available modes: Consolidation mode passes the original trace and all Phase 1 verdicts to a judge model to produce a unified consensus. Deliberation mode uses a moderator to run multi-turn debates between jurors, who discuss defect points until consensus is reached or discussion stalls. The moderator never accesses the original problem or reasoning trace — it only maintains consensus state at the claim level, selects speakers, and decides termination timing, then synthesizes the final consensus. All outputs are grounded to specific step markers for auditability and actionability.b:T610,The method, MARGO, stands for Mixed-Mode Advantage Regularization for Grounded Optimization. The conceptual starting point is a residual decomposition: the non-thinking response reflects the model’s direct-answer tendency, while the thinking response adds a reasoning residual on top. That residual can be helpful, recovering the right fact, or harmful, introducing unsupported associations, entity confusion, or speculative drift.

MARGO modifies the rollout grouping used in group-based RL such as GRPO. Standard setups typically compare only thinking trajectories for the same question. That makes the direct-answer baseline invisible: if all thinking samples are wrong, or all are right, the training signal cannot tell whether explicit reasoning helped or hurt relative to the model’s own non-thinking behavior. MARGO instead forms mixed rollout groups containing both thinking and non-thinking trajectories. The non-thinking output becomes a same-model reference inside advantage estimation.

This changes the optimization target in a meaningful way. Thinking trajectories that corrupt an already correct direct answer receive lower relative advantage, while trajectories that genuinely repair an incorrect direct answer are reinforced. The novelty is not a complicated RL algorithm, but the choice of reference signal. Rather than relying on an external teacher or a separate verifier, the method uses mode contrast within the same model to expose harmful reasoning residuals. That is well aligned with the failure mode the paper identifies.c:T455,The strongest empirical point is the instance-level transition analysis. On TriviaQA with Qwen3-8B, thinking corrects 11.74% of examples that are wrong under non-thinking, but it also overturns 7.50% of originally correct non-thinking answers into incorrect ones. That is the key evidence that thinking-induced hallucination is real and not a corner case.

The training evaluation covers two models and six factuality-oriented QA benchmarks. The paper reports that MARGO consistently improves factual reliability over fixed-mode inference, mode selection, supervised fine-tuning, and RL baselines trained on the same curated data. It also evaluates on mathematical benchmarks and claims that the factuality-oriented mixed-mode RL does not noticeably damage general reasoning ability. The excerpt provided does not include the full result tables, so the exact benchmark-by-benchmark gains cannot be reproduced here. Still, the most informative part of the evaluation is likely not the average score alone, but how each method changes the rates of correct-to-incorrect flips and incorrect-to-correct recoveries.d:T899,The central idea is competing rewards over different segments of the same trajectory. Instead of assigning a single scalar reward to the full rollout, the method splits generation into a reasoning segment and a final answer segment, and scores them separately. The reasoning segment is rewarded for exploring increasingly harmful or unsafe interpretations. The answer segment is rewarded for producing a safe final response conditioned on that preceding unsafe exploration. The intended behavior is not globally “safe-looking” reasoning, but controlled exploration followed by safe recovery.

Several design choices matter. First, the paper argues that optimization mismatch between the competing objectives leads to unstable training. If the exploration reward and the safe-answer reward are not balanced in how they are sampled or optimized, the model can collapse to one side: either unsafe exploration without recovery, or conservative refusal without meaningful exploration. Second, the authors show that dense outcome-level rewards are still too coarse for this setting. They do not provide enough credit assignment to tell the model which part of the trajectory should be exploratory and which part should be controlled. This motivates process-based rewards at the segment level on sampled rollouts. Third, the method keeps exploration and recovery within one continuous chain of thought, rather than outsourcing safety to a separate classifier or a detached control module. That is an important distinction: the goal is to train safety as a reasoning dynamic, not as a front-end filter.

Relative to prior safety-RL methods such as TARS, the difference is not merely a modified reward function. The underlying training assumption changes. Earlier work largely treats reasoning as a tool for deciding whether to refuse. This paper treats reasoning as a space where the model must be allowed to inspect unsafe possibilities under control; otherwise it cannot resolve ambiguous prompts well. The robustness analysis around pre-filling attacks reinforces this point: if a model has never learned to recover from unsafe intermediate reasoning, steering its chain of thought becomes much easier.e:T62d,The main model is SEAR-1.5B. The headline result is about the safety–refusal trade-off rather than a single benchmark score. The paper reports that SEAR achieves the best trade-off among open models in its size class, outperforming the closest baseline, TARS. It also claims that despite having only 1.5B parameters, SEAR performs comparably to the much larger TARS-7B on this trade-off, which suggests that the gain is not a minor tuning effect but a meaningful change in how the model uses reasoning.

The most important experiments fall into four groups. First, the paper analyzes existing safety-trained reasoning models and shows that their CoT often behaves like a rubber stamp rather than a mechanism for ambiguity resolution. That diagnostic section is important because it motivates the method. Second, there are reward-design ablations comparing single outcome rewards, dense but non-process rewards, and true segment-level process rewards. The conclusion is that only the last option reliably learns the desired combination of unsafe exploration and safe answering. Third, the paper studies optimization stability and shows that mismatched optimization across competing rewards leads to unstable behavior. Fourth, it evaluates robustness to adversarial attacks, especially attacks that target the reasoning process such as pre-filling. SEAR is reported to be more robust under such attacks. LeanCheck uses Lean’s accept/reject signal for candidate proofs, not to solve theorem proving end to end but to test whether selected rationale or proof spans encode the formal verifier outcome. Under counterfactual conflict, rationale-only and proof-only pooling achieve perfect directional separation, showing strong representation-level separation. The KataGo setup uses engine-derived Go evaluations; the supplied text reports that commentary spans encode a 10-way engine signal. The most informative failure case is code coupling: the model reaches 98.6% representation coupling, yet the generated prose fluently describes unrelated algorithms. A controlled experiment with pretrained GPT-2 reproduces the same fluent-but-unfaithful pattern, suggesting that the failure is not simply a capacity issue. The overall finding is that consistency training reliably makes verifier information decodable and sometimes causally influential, but does not by itself make generated rationales faithful.10:T469,The proposed Experiential Latent Memory (ELM) pipeline creates a small latent memory after each solved problem. Instead of updating the backbone, it trains a tiny soft-prompt-like module (claimed ~0.001% of parameters) with a few gradient steps, using self-generated signals from the model’s own reasoning trajectories (success/failure exploration under test-time compute).
The paper’s design logic is:
1) Textual trace replay is bottlenecked: raw CoT traces (even with reflection-style refinement) are surface artifacts and do not reliably transfer; hidden states may contain more abstract structure than what is verbalized.
2) Internal-reward learning can provide a learning signal without labels, but prior approaches are mostly offline and can degrade as more test data accumulates.
3) Modularity isolates updates: per-instance memories are stored separately and composed at retrieval time, aiming to mitigate interference and forgetting compared to full-parameter RL/finetuning.
Operationally it looks like retrieval (select relevant memories) + injection (compose soft prompts) + tiny online optimization (memory-only).11:T6d0,The intervention is deliberately minimal. The authors define source-conditioned role relabeling: after a model produces an incorrect intermediate claim c*, they append an additional message containing the exact same bytes of c*, but place it under an external role such as user, tool, or system memory, while keeping the audit instruction fixed across conditions. This isolates the causal effect of the role wrapper from content changes.

The paper then adds several mechanism probes. A self-distrust control asks the model to verify its own thoughts directly. This performs much worse than relabeling, which argues against a simple “extra verification reminder” explanation. A handle-granularity decomposition separates two effects: giving the claim an explicit standalone wrapper versus assigning that wrapper a specific role tag. The results suggest that a bare wrapper already helps, and the role label adds further lift. This supports an addressability account: a claim embedded inside the model’s own continuous scratchpad is harder to target, quote, and reject than the same claim presented as a discrete external object. The authors also analyze logprob signatures to distinguish engage-then-verify behavior from naive recomputation.

A useful part of the paper is the safety boundary analysis. The authors test the reverse direction: if relabeling helps correction, can role manipulation also inject errors? By default, the answer is mostly no. But once a trust-framing instruction is added, such as telling the model to treat memory as ground truth and not verify it, the attack channel opens up. So the effect is real, but it is mediated by instruction hierarchy and trust framing rather than being an unconditional role prior.12:T554,The study covers 13 model-domain cells across 7 model families and 3 domains, with 30 paired tasks per cell. Relabeling the same wrong claim from the model’s own <thought> into an external role improves correction rates by 23 to 93 percentage points. Ten of the 13 cells show significant gains; the remaining three are ceiling cases where the baseline correction rate is already high, at or above 67%. One highlighted example is Llama-3.3-70B, where the lift reaches +77 points.

The adversarial mirror includes five attack settings with n=30 each. Under default conditions, all 20 attack-rate cells stay at or below 3.3%, so the correction channel does not automatically become an error-injection channel. The mechanism ablations are also informative. A bare syntactic wrapper contributes roughly 17 to 23 points, and the role tag adds about 30 more. In the self-distrust control, explicitly asking the model to verify its own thoughts yields only 0 to 23% correction, versus up to 70% under relabeling. The defender releases outputs drawn from an admissible perturbation set around clean traces. The attacker trains a student model on those outputs. The defender wants to maximize the student’s evaluation loss on clean data while keeping the released output useful to legitimate users. Compared with classical data poisoning, two aspects change. First, the student architecture is often unknown, so the defense should not rely on gradients from a fixed proxy model. Second, the model provider is the party injecting the perturbation, while the distiller is the attacker. The motivation comes from interpretability observations that thought anchors and high-entropy tokens can disproportionately influence later reasoning paths.
