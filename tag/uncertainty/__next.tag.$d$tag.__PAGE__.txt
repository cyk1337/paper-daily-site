9:T748,The method has two layers. The first is CaRE-Divergence, a token-level objective that interpolates between Forward KL and Reverse KL based on relative confidence. Confidence is measured by normalized entropy from a single forward pass: sharper distributions imply higher confidence. When the teacher is more confident than the student, the gate moves toward Forward KL, encouraging support coverage. When the teacher is less confident, the gate moves toward Reverse KL, making the update more mode-seeking and less sensitive to the teacher’s unreliable tail. The paper considers both hard and sigmoid-based soft gates, but the key idea is that the gate depends on the teacher-student confidence gap rather than a fixed global coefficient.

The second component is Revival, a sequence-level rejection rule. The authors argue that single-pass entropy only captures aleatoric uncertainty and cannot separate intrinsic ambiguity from hallucination-like epistemic uncertainty. To address this, they estimate BALD scores for both teacher and student using Monte Carlo dropout, then reject a distillation update when the teacher is epistemically less reliable than the student. The threshold is defined by running quantiles rather than a fixed scalar, which makes the rule robust to training-time drift in BALD magnitudes.

What distinguishes the paper from prior work is the combination of token-level geometry adaptation and sequence-level supervision rejection. Earlier methods mostly use a static divergence with a global precision-recall bias, or perform filtering at the dataset level. A path outcome is represented by a first decisive-event index T_ir, and the terminal label Y_ir indicates whether no decisive event occurred within K steps. 1) Self negative conditioning: for each prompt x, the model first generates a per-question negative condition n (e.g., “act as a careless reasoner”). Conditioning on n yields a negative-teacher distribution π_neg from the same base model.
2) Divergence-from-negative with token gating: the student is trained to reduce alignment with π_neg on its sampled trajectory, i.e., push probability mass away from tokens favored by the negatively conditioned teacher.
The core technical issue is selectivity: tokens with high probability under π_neg are not always “reasoning errors”. A naive unlikelihood or negative KL objective would penalize many ordinary structural/language tokens and degrade pretrained linguistic competence. NSD introduces a dynamic token-level gating mechanism using a benign reference model: the negative objective is activated only when the negative condition increases the likelihood of the sampled token relative to the reference. This treats the probability lift induced by the negative condition as a proxy for flawed-reasoning bias.
They further stabilize optimization with bounded unlikelihood (cap the penalty to avoid large gradients on high-confidence common tokens) and a KL regularizer to prevent the student from drifting too far from the reference distribution. It has four parts. First, define the comparison target explicitly: which two configurations are being compared, and what quantity is the score intended to estimate—raw success on the observed benchmark mix, equal-repository weighting, expert-labeled success, deployment-style success, or cost-aware utility. Second, check common support under that target. If the two systems do not both cover the same support cells after stratification or reweighting, the target comparison is not identified from the released data. Third, estimate the supported gap and attach a stated uncertainty rule plus a practical margin. The paper stresses that uncertainty is not a cosmetic add-on: one must specify the sampling unit, clustering level, and whether simultaneous inference is needed. Fourth, return a decision label rather than forcing a total order. Labels include stable superiority, target-sensitive, verifier disagreement, insufficient precision, and missing support.

The paper also includes controlled checks. Simulations test whether these labels recover known finite-sample states. A known-target estimation exercise shows why target reweighting must be judged with uncertainty included; otherwise one overstates how often rankings “flip.” An AgentRewardBench permutation analysis provides a task-conditioned exchangeability reference for interpreting close gaps.
