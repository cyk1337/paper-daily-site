9:T8c2,LinCa follows a Decompose-Predict-Reconstruct pipeline. The motivation comes from empirical analysis: hidden features in diffusion models show strong heterogeneity across models, timestep segments, and feature dimensions. Some components evolve smoothly and are suitable for higher-order extrapolation; others are unstable and exhibit abrupt changes, making direct prediction unreliable. The difficulty is that these components are interleaved in the original feature space, so simple manual partitioning does not work.

To address this, LinCa introduces a lightweight invertible network that maps cached features into a new representation space. In that space, features are decomposed into sub-components with different continuity and predictability properties. Stable components are predicted from historical caches using polynomial extrapolation, and the prediction order is matched to the component rather than shared globally. Unstable components are not extrapolated; they are simply reused from the nearest cache. The processed sub-components are then mapped back to the original feature space through the inverse transform. The role of invertibility is important: it prevents information loss during decomposition and allows the training objective to be defined directly on reconstructed features.

A second key design is timestep segmentation. Instead of training one global predictor for the entire denoising trajectory, the method splits timesteps into several segments and trains isomorphic but independently parameterized predictors for each segment. This matters because early, middle, and late denoising stages often have very different hidden-state dynamics, and a single predictor tends to fail in some regions.

Training overhead is intentionally small. LinCa does not need to load or finetune the original diffusion model. It only uses a small set of pre-generated intermediate features as supervision. The paper reports 100-200 feature samples, less than one hour of training on a single 12GB GPU, and parameter overhead below 0.2% of the base model. From a systems perspective, this keeps much of the deployment convenience of training-free caching while replacing brittle heuristics with a lightweight learned predictor.a:T782,The evaluation covers FLUX.1-dev, Qwen-Image, Qwen-Image-Edit, and HunyuanVideo, and also tests compatibility with distilled and quantized models. The main result is that LinCa preserves generation quality much better than prior caching methods at high acceleration ratios while keeping nearly the same runtime profile. Reported speedups include 5.51× on FLUX.1-dev, 6.95× on Qwen-Image, 7.08× on Qwen-Image-Edit, and 5.50× on HunyuanVideo, with the paper describing the quality loss as near-lossless.

The most important evidence is not the headline speedup table but the validation of the paper’s core premise: heterogeneous feature dynamics. The useful experiments are the ones showing (1) different predictability across timestep segments, (2) different continuity patterns across feature dimensions within the same layer, and (3) lower prediction error from decomposed, differentiated prediction compared with a uniform predictor. If those analyses are convincing, then the gains are not just from adding a larger auxiliary model but from matching the predictor to the actual dynamics.

For ablations, the key questions are: whether invertible decomposition is necessary, whether component-specific prediction orders outperform a shared order, whether segmented predictors beat a global predictor, and whether 100-200 training samples are already enough to saturate performance. If the paper reports FID, CLIP-based metrics, video quality scores, or editing consistency, the most informative comparison is quality at matched speedup against methods such as FORA, ToCa, and TaylorSeer, rather than absolute quality alone.

A clear limitation is that this remains an inference-time patch; it does not change the sampling algorithm itself. At resolution transition points, it assigns each latent token a joint importance score and uses that score to decide which tokens are upsampled earlier.

The first component is spatial importance. Instead of relying only on local latent structure or one-step statistics, AViTS explicitly uses latent-text attention. The intuition is straightforward: if a visual token consistently responds strongly to the text condition at low resolution, it is more likely to correspond to prompt-relevant objects, attributes, or edit targets, and should therefore receive high-resolution compute earlier. The paper suggests that this signal is aggregated rather than taken from a single layer or step, which makes it more stable.

The second component is temporal importance. The paper measures token-level step variance, namely how much a token representation changes across diffusion steps. Tokens with large variation are still evolving and likely need further refinement; tokens with small variation are closer to convergence and can be deferred. This complements attention-based importance because some regions may not be the strongest text-aligned areas but still require continued updates for editing fidelity or fine-detail recovery.

The third component is fusion and selective upsampling. AViTS combines the spatial and temporal signals into a unified spatiotemporal importance score, then performs importance-aware selective upsampling: high-importance tokens are promoted to high resolution earlier, while low-importance tokens are delayed. This differs from prior partial upsampling methods that mostly depend on local structure heuristics, edge cues, uncertainty proxies, or single-step latent statistics. AViTS jointly models conditional semantics and cross-step dynamics.

Importantly, the framework is presented as an add-on policy for dynamic-resolution DiTs rather than a backbone redesign.
