18:T4eb,BrowserForge has four main components. First, open-web URL sourcing and cleaning: candidate URLs are drawn from Common Crawl and filtered into a large pool of real, reachable, publicly accessible websites. This replaces fixed website whitelists with the open web itself as the source of diversity. Second, parallel browser sandbox orchestration: a cluster manager schedules hundreds of browser sandboxes through a shared work queue, turning web interaction collection into a high-throughput process. Third, a Proposer–Solver synthesis loop: a Proposer agent inspects a rendered page and proposes an executable task, and a Solver agent attempts the task and records the trajectory. Structured page information such as the accessibility tree is used only during synthesis as an auxiliary signal; it is not part of the deployed agent’s inference input. Fourth, verification and cleaning: rule-based and model-based filters remove invalid or low-quality trajectories, and the surviving reasoning traces are rewritten into a unified chain-of-thought format. The design choice is deliberate: use structure when generating and validating data, but keep the final policy interface purely visual to avoid HTML/a11y brittleness and token overhead at inference time.19:T4db,The paper formalizes a synthetic-data pipeline as (Q, T, p), where Q is the seed-question set, T is the teacher model, and p is the synthesis protocol, including prompt templates, decoding settings, filtering, selection, or rewriting. Source Expansion modifies Q or T. Fixed-Source Synthesis fixes (Q, T) and changes only the per-question budget r or the protocol p. The main FSS experiment uses rejection sampling: for each seed question, the teacher generates multiple candidate responses, and filtered responses become SFT examples. The authors adapt the rectified scaling law to the FSS setting. The key modeling assumption is coverage: repeated sampling from a fixed source gradually covers the available useful samples, so the performance curve should saturate and marginal gains should decline with r. This differs from ordinary data-scaling intuition because effective new information does not grow linearly under a fixed Q and fixed T. The paper then compares SE and FSS at matched total-sample budgets, and evaluates common fixed-source protocols such as alternative sampling temperatures, embedding-based selection, persona prompting, unfiltered sampling, trace-level repair, and generating additional questions from existing seeds.1a:T511,The experiments cover Mathematics and Physics. Synthetic-data construction uses about 2B teacher-inference tokens, including 1.6B tokens from DeepSeek-V3.1 rollouts. The study then runs more than 500 student SFT jobs, totaling over 2,000 GPU-hours. The key findings are consistent. First, increasing the per-question response budget r under fixed (Q, T) improves student accuracy, but the gains diminish and a clear teacher-student gap remains even at the largest budget. The proposed fixed-source scaling form, fitted only on low-budget points, predicts performance at the held-out highest budget for every evaluated teacher-student pair. Second, under matched total-sample budgets, SE and FSS are comparable at small budgets, but expanding the real seed-question set becomes more reliable as the budget increases. Third, generating additional questions from existing seeds does not improve over plain rejection sampling, and common protocol changes such as diversity selection, persona prompting, no filtering, or trace repair do not consistently beat plain RS under the fixed-source control.
