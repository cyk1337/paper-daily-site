5:T74d,The method, AKBE, explicitly probes the model’s knowledge boundary during training. For each question, it runs dual-path rollouts under the current policy: one path with tool access and one path without tools. The two outcomes are then compared to infer whether the question is inside the model’s current parametric knowledge boundary and, if tools are needed, what the minimum effective tool-use pattern looks like.

The paper defines four cases. If the with-tool path is correct and the no-tool path is wrong, the sample is Tool-dependent: the model genuinely needs tools. In this case, AKBE reinforces the correct trajectory with the minimum number of tool calls, not just any successful tool-using trajectory.

If both with-tool and no-tool paths are correct, the sample is Efficiency: tools were unnecessary. AKBE then reinforces the no-tool correct trajectory to suppress redundant calls.

If the with-tool path is wrong but the no-tool path is correct, the sample is Hallucination: tool use hurt performance, likely by injecting misleading evidence or derailing reasoning. AKBE again reinforces the no-tool correct trajectory.

If both paths are wrong, the sample is Both-wrong, and no extra boundary-guided supervision is applied beyond the base RL objective.

Two design choices matter. First, the supervision is on-policy, because the dual-path rollouts come from the current model. This lets the boundary estimate evolve with training rather than relying on static labels. Second, AKBE does not modify the reward function. Instead, it adds an auxiliary loss on top of standard agentic RL, which is how it avoids the usual reward-shaping failure mode. Relative to prior efficiency-oriented methods, the novelty is not merely reducing tool calls, but estimating, per instance, whether tools are needed at all and what the minimal necessary usage should be.6:T564,The paper reports experiments on seven QA benchmarks with two backbone models, comparing standard agentic RL against AKBE and other efficiency-oriented baselines. The headline numbers are: +1.85 average accuracy improvement, 18% fewer tool calls, and 25% higher tool productivity. The authors emphasize that this is not an accuracy-efficiency tradeoff; in most settings, both improve together.

The more informative parts of the evaluation are the analyses. One is compatibility: AKBE can be plugged into multiple agentic RL algorithms rather than being tied to a single trainer. Another is training dynamics: the distribution of samples across Tool-dependent, Efficiency, and Hallucination categories changes over training, suggesting that the knowledge boundary is not static and is itself reshaped by RL. A third is category-level attribution: Efficiency supervision mainly removes redundant calls, Hallucination supervision reduces harmful tool reliance, and Tool-dependent supervision teaches more economical tool-use patterns when tools are genuinely needed.

From the available details, the paper is focused on training behavior rather than scale. It does not appear to hinge on very large models or massive data. The gains are meaningful from an engineering perspective, but they are not large enough to imply a new scaling law or a major shift in agentic RL fundamentals.7:T4b3,Start with the introduction and the first motivating figure. The key claim is that tool calls steadily increase during RL training, and naive reward shaping creates the wrong optimization pressure. Then read the method section carefully, especially the dual-path rollout setup and the four-way categorization. That is the core of the paper.

In the experiments, prioritize three things: the main results table showing simultaneous gains in accuracy and efficiency; the training-dynamics plots showing how the boundary categories evolve; and the ablations or category analyses that isolate the role of each supervision type. For a quick pass, 20 to 30 minutes is enough if you focus on the method diagram, the main quantitative table, and one dynamic analysis figure.

Two points deserve closer scrutiny. First, dual-path rollouts add sampling cost. Check whether the paper quantifies the extra training overhead and whether the reduced tool usage compensates for it in practice. Second, the boundary estimate depends on current-policy rollouts and may be noisy. If the no-tool path is unstable, misclassification could inject noisy supervision.
