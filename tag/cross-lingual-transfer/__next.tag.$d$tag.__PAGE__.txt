a:T8c0,The technical core is the experimental design. Training data comes from Multilingual Reasoning Gym, which procedurally generates reasoning problems in 14 languages and supports adjustable difficulty. Instead of simply translating English datasets, the authors use an online curriculum: for each task, difficulty increases when more than 70% of the last 32 rollouts are correct and decreases when fewer than 10% are correct. This matters because the same model can have very different capability baselines across languages, so fixed difficulty would confound language effects with reward availability. They also pre-filter tasks using Qwen3-14B-Base at easy difficulty, removing tasks with pass@8 = 0, and exclude heavily English-dependent tasks from training while keeping them for evaluation. The resulting setup uses 62 training tasks and 30 held-out tasks for OOD evaluation.

Training uses a DAPO-style GRPO objective without KL penalty. Each run uses 500 steps, learning rate 1e-6, 64 prompts per step, and 8 rollouts per prompt. Rewards consist of a binary correctness reward plus two auxiliary rewards, each weighted at half of correctness: a format reward and a reasoning-language reward. The reasoning-language reward is instantiated either as English reasoning or native-language reasoning. A key implementation detail is that auxiliary rewards are only granted when the final answer is correct, which prevents the model from gaming language or format rewards at the expense of solving the problem. The paper notes that preliminary runs in low-resource languages could otherwise collapse or overfit to formatting.

The model suite spans Qwen, Gemma, and SmolLM families, including both base and post-trained variants from 1B to 8B parameters. The authors run monolingual GRPO for 11 MGSM languages and one multilingual run with uniform language sampling. Italian, Korean, and Portuguese are fully held out as unseen languages. For model-language pairs with sufficient initial support for the target language, they additionally train native-reward variants. The point of this design is to disentangle training language, rewarded reasoning language, and model pretraining background rather than to optimize a single benchmark score.b:T6a8,The most robust findings are fourfold. First, native-language reasoning rewards usually trail English-reasoning rewards by only a small margin on average; there is no universal large penalty for asking the model to reason in the prompt language. Second, cross-lingual transfer is strong: GRPO in one language often improves performance in many others, suggesting that RL is transferring problem-solving behavior rather than only surface-form language patterns. Third, the transfer is not monotonic. The paper identifies a regression failure mode where training in a particular language improves some evaluations but causes substantial drops on unseen languages or tasks. These regressions are highly model- and language-specific. Fourth, multilingual training is not always better than the best monolingual run, but it often provides a more stable compromise and is especially relevant for held-out-language generalization.

Evaluation covers seen tasks, 30 unseen tasks, MGSM, and PolyMath. The abstract does not expose the full numeric tables, but the full paper makes clear that the conclusions come from a broad comparison over 9 models, 11 training languages, monolingual versus multilingual runs, and English versus native reasoning rewards. For a pretraining researcher, the more useful part is the analysis style: the authors do not stop at average scores, but inspect transfer matrices across languages and compare how different model families react to the same training language. That supports a practical conclusion: the main bottleneck in multilingual RL is not merely data availability, but the interaction among initial language competence, reward attainability, and policy drift during RL.c:T544,A factorial experimental design over three language roles:
- Setup: fine-tune to inject knowledge in language L_FT, unlearn a subset in language L_unl, and query the supposedly forgotten knowledge in language L_Q. Enumerate permutations of (L_FT, L_unl, L_Q) across five languages varying in script/family and pretraining coverage.
- Transfer measurement: quantify forgetting effectiveness when querying in languages different from the unlearning language; analyze patterns by linguistic relatedness and dominance.
- Cross-lingual prompting: ask in L_Q but allow decoding in a different language; measure recovery gains and relate them to unlearning-transfer strength.
- Mechanistic analysis: layer-wise comparison of hidden representations pre/post unlearning across languages; finding that cross-lingual alignment is largely preserved in early/mid layers and altered more in later layers.
- Unlearning directions (steering vectors): extract explicit directions from representational shifts; steering along them restores knowledge across languages without re-training on the forgotten data, supporting a language-agnostic suppression signal.
The work treats unlearning methods (continued fine-tuning style) as the object of study; the novelty is in multilingual transfer characterization and reversibility evidence, not a new unlearning algorithm.d:T437,The evaluation covers four multilingual models: Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Ministral-3-8B-Instruct. It tests six languages: English, Chinese, Bengali, Spanish, Italian, and German; and five figurative categories: idiom, metaphor, simile, irony, and sarcasm. Final evaluations use 500 held-out literal prompts per target language, and the prompt does not ask for figurative language, so increases in Target Category Rate come from the intervention. Monolingual steering improves targeted figurative behavior in 74 of 96 settings against unsteered and random-vector controls. Zero-shot cross-lingual steering succeeds in 369 of 416 settings, and in some cases a source-language vector matches or exceeds the vector derived from the target language itself. Metaphor and simile are the most stable categories, while idiom, irony, and sarcasm show more variability because they depend more on culture and discourse context. Aggregate multilingual vectors often rival native-language vectors, and ablating the shared subspace sharply reduces figurative generation.e:T683,The method extends the FineWeb2-HQ embedding-based filtering recipe in three important ways. First, the positive anchor pool is expanded from MKC+ to MKC-e, adding more instruction-style, synthetic, and encyclopedic multilingual sources such as Tagengo, the Wikipedia subset of MURI-IT, EuroBlocks-SFT-Synthetic, and WikiQA. This broadens the notion of “high quality” beyond a single genre. Second, the representation and classifier remain intentionally simple: 768-dimensional document embeddings from XLM-RoBERTa, followed by a lightweight MLP with one 256-d hidden layer, ReLU, 20% dropout, and a sigmoid output. Keeping the classifier small helps isolate the effect of data construction and transfer rather than model capacity. Third, the paper studies negative sampling carefully. Besides standard random negatives from raw FineWeb2, it introduces a Q3 hard-negative strategy that samples documents from the 50th–75th percentile of an existing quality-score distribution. The motivation is important: random negatives are often too easy and encourage the classifier to rely on shallow cues such as boilerplate or formatting noise, whereas Q3 negatives are fluent but low-utility, forcing the model to learn a sharper boundary around information density and usefulness. Training uses 100k positive documents per language, with up to 3x positive upsampling for low-resource languages, and balanced positive/negative classes. First, large multilingual pooling often beats monolingual classifiers on both rank stability and aggregate accuracy, which means mixing languages does not wash out the signal and may actually regularize the decision boundary. Second, transfer across language families appears to work: classifiers trained on one family can help curate typologically distant languages, which is the key result for low-resource settings. Third, Q3 hard negatives improve the boundary in high-resource languages such as French and Spanish, outperforming simple random negatives. This is a useful result because it shows the real challenge is not separating clean text from obvious junk, but distinguishing useful text from merely fluent text. Fourth, the paper validates the approach in an actual pretraining setup: a 1B model trained on 103B tokens, where multilingual pooling improves over several baselines and beats strong baselines for low-resource languages.
