18:T485,The framework, Multi-Turn Certified Robustness (MTCR), models conversation as a State-Adversarial MDP. Dialogue history is mapped to a continuous state; at each turn an adversary chooses a user message from an epsilon-bounded perturbation set; the LLM responds according to its policy; and the state is updated. Safety is defined by a predicate over the state space. The central quantity is k-turn certified robustness: the worst-case probability of staying safe for k turns under any adaptive adversary. A naive certificate gives a lower bound of p^k, where p is the worst-case single-turn certified safety probability. MTCR tightens this through embedding-space mode decomposition, separately certifying intra-mode safety and inter-mode transitions. It also introduces (alpha, beta)-safety persistence, a structural condition under which the degradation can improve from p^k to beta^k when beta is larger than the worst-case single-turn bound, with a linear characterization 1 - k(1 - beta) for interpretable horizon estimates. The paper also discusses tightness for non-overlapping decompositions and impossibility results without structural assumptions.19:T48d,CAST (Constrained Ablation for Safe Steering). Setup: a steering vector v is injected into the residual stream at a chosen layer during inference (activation addition), typically built as a mean activation difference from contrastive prompt pairs.
CAST parameterizes a sanitized vector by projecting v away from a learned safety-degrading subspace S: v* = v - P_S(v). Since the true dimensionality is unknown, the main experiments use a rank-1 approximation with a learned unit direction r̂: v* = v - r̂ r̂^T v.
Learning r̂ is posed as a Lagrangian constrained optimization with primal-dual updates, balancing three requirements:
- Safety restoration: on a harmful prompt distribution D_harm (multiple jailbreak styles), the steered model with v* should refuse at least as often as the unsteered baseline.
- Effect preservation: on D_effect, outputs under v* should remain close to outputs under the original v.
- False-refusal control: on D_benign, v* should not significantly increase over-refusals relative to baseline.
The paper argues the safety-degrading component is largely separable from the effect-relevant component, enabling “surgical” ablation.1a:T772,The paper has two main technical moves. First, it cleanly separates the quantities being measured. It defines harmful intent H, wrapper presence W, and realized harmful output Ym under a fixed judge, then evaluates ranking, calibration, and threshold transfer separately. This matters because many prior discussions collapse these notions into a single “harmfulness score”.

Second, it introduces Active Attention Probing (AAP) to remove a confound in attention-based detectors. Passive detectors often read from prompt-dependent locations. A jailbreak wrapper changes not only the content but also the coordinate from which the signal is extracted, making score changes hard to interpret. AAP inserts a short learned probe embedding into the system region after chat templating, creating a fixed, content-independent reference point. The model’s attention response at that probe is collected across selected heads and layers, concatenated into a feature vector, and mapped to a scalar score with XGBoost.

The key idea is not that the probe token is intrinsically better, but that it stabilizes the readout coordinate so plain and wrapped prompts can be compared at a common location. The primary probe uses K=3 rows, is trained only on the BeaverTails training split, and then frozen for all audits. The authors explicitly avoid selecting probes, heads, or readouts on jailbreak benchmarks such as JailbreakHub, WildJailbreak, or JailbreakBench. They also test multiple alternative channels—rare token, common token, norm-matched token, system-span, passive attention, and refusal-logit channels—to show that the main conclusion is not an artifact of one specific readout family. An outcome-supervised positive control further shows that the model does contain predictive signal for success labels; the failure comes from label mismatch, not from a total absence of useful internal information.1b:T4a2,The central empirical result is the divergence between harmfulness and realized success. On Llama targets, adding a wrapper increases the harmful generation rate from 0.05 to 0.27, so the wrapper makes attacks more successful. At the same time, harmful-intent AUROC drops from 0.936 to 0.803. More strikingly, within wrapped harmful prompts, the score ranks successful attacks below failed ones, yielding an outcome AUROC of 0.220. This is not merely weak prediction; it is active anti-ranking.

The paper also reports that this pattern persists across measurement channels, target models, attack families, and independent judges. That makes the result harder to dismiss as a probe-specific artifact. The deployment analysis is also important: under a fixed benign false-positive budget, a threshold tuned on harmfulness spends blocking capacity on attacks that would have failed anyway, while letting more successful wrapped attacks pass. The authors further separate ranking failure, probability miscalibration, and threshold-transfer failure, showing that a score can remain superficially useful on its original validation task while failing under wrapper-induced distribution shift.1c:T486,Start with the problem formulation. Read the Introduction and Measurement Setting carefully, especially the distinction among H(x), W(x), and Ym(x), and the three notions of validity: construct, outcome, and decision validity. That framing is the real contribution.

Then read the Audit Design section to understand why passive attention readouts are confounded by wrapper-induced coordinate shifts, and how AAP fixes the readout location in the system region. You do not need every implementation detail on a first pass, but it is worth understanding that XGBoost is only the final readout layer; the methodological novelty is the controlled measurement setup.

In the experiments, focus on the plain-vs-wrapped comparisons and the harmfulness-vs-outcome tables and figures. Do not stop at AUROC. The calibration and threshold-transfer analyses are where the deployment implications become concrete. A 30-40 minute skim is enough to capture the main argument. If your work involves safety probes or internal representation auditing, the appendix is worth extra time for channel ablations, head-selection details, and the outcome-supervised positive control.1d:T4bf,The analysis uses a finite decision-theoretic setup. For a fixed public task context W=w, the context includes the task, claimed identity, role, and stated use, but these claims are not treated as evidence of actual downstream use. The deployment has a finite release menu A, including refusal. Each release has a utility under a legitimate-use world B and a misuse utility under a malicious-use world M; because the malicious user can choose how to exploit the answer after observing it, the malicious utility takes the most useful allowed misuse. A safeguard is modeled as a Markov kernel over terminal releases conditioned on a pre-release transcript H. The central assumption is that the attacker can reproduce the same observable evidence as a legitimate user. Total variation distance is used to quantify imperfect copying or credential separation. Under a legitimate utility target q, the paper derives a capability floor Γ(q): the unavoidable amount of attacker assistance induced by preserving useful answers. This differs from jailbreak-specific work because it proves a structural lower bound for any safeguard based only on copyable evidence, including randomized decisions and multi-turn verification.1e:T433,The work first formalizes the IPJ problem, constructing a taxonomy of 9 linguistic "attractor" structures that append to harmful requests to keep sentences grammatically incomplete and induce continuation. Model outputs are decomposed into pre-termination, sentence-termination, and post-termination segments, with attack success rate and refusal distance as core metrics. The authors then demonstrate that fine-tuning models to refuse early on incomplete harmful prompts suffers from three consistent failure modes: poor transfer across harmful content domains, poor transfer to unseen attractor types, and inability to generalize across base models with different default refusal styles. Finally, activation correlation analysis is used to identify two functional neuron groups: termination neurons strongly associated with sentence-final punctuation, and continuation neurons that show large activation differences between termination-forbidden and termination-forced prompting contexts, with causal effects on generation continuation verified via activation intervention.1f:T5c7,DT-Guard combines (i) a structured decision decomposition, (ii) an intent-enriched dataset, and (iii) rollout-guided hard-case optimization.
1) Progressive decision structure: instead of mapping text directly to a safety label, the model is trained to follow Intent → Category → Safety. Intent is meant to disambiguate benign vs malicious use under the same sensitive topic; Category captures risk taxonomy; Safety is the final verdict. Reasoning trajectories are used as supervision during training but are not emitted at inference.
2) Intent-driven data construction: heterogeneous safety resources are normalized into a unified prompt–response–label format and augmented with intent labels, risk categories, safety labels, and structured CoT-style trajectories. The pipeline mentions distillation (GLM-5.1), multi-round voting, and expert verification to reduce annotation noise and surface boundary cases.
3) RG-PHO (Rollout-Guided Progressive Hard-Case Optimization): the model performs multiple rollouts per sample; rollout consistency is used to categorize residual errors.
