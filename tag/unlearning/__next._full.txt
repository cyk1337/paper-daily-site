19:T712,The proposed audit, J-Access, measures how accessible target knowledge remains along the model’s output pathway. Instead of relying on a standard logit lens, which directly decodes intermediate residual states with the unembedding matrix, the method uses a Jacobian lens. The motivation is specific: after unlearning, the geometry of the residual stream may shift, so the shared-basis assumption behind the logit lens becomes less reliable. The Jacobian lens locally linearizes the downstream computation from an intermediate state to vocabulary space, making it a better proxy for whether hidden knowledge is still close to being verbalized.

Operationally, the audit uses probe queries that implicate a forgotten entity without explicitly naming it. For a band of mid-to-late layers, the residual representation is projected into vocabulary space through the Jacobian lens, and the method checks whether any token from a target concept set appears among the top-ranked decoded tokens. The concept set is defined at two granularities: an identity-level set containing full-name and name-piece tokens, and a broader knowledge-level set containing answer content words filtered by document frequency and inferability constraints. The resulting access rate is normalized against both the original pre-unlearning model and a retain-only gold model trained without the forget set.

The paper is structured around two evaluation regimes rather than a new unlearning algorithm. In the predictive regime, pre-attack J-Access is measured and then compared against recovery under cross-entity relearning attacks. In the optimization regime, J-Access suppression is added directly to the unlearning objective to test whether the signal is robust or merely gameable. That design is the paper’s strongest contribution.1a:T5df,The study evaluates two unlearning benchmarks, TOFU and OpenUnlearning, covering 8 unlearning methods and 398 public unlearned models. Three findings drive the paper.

First, many models that pass standard forgetting and utility checks still show J-Access substantially above the retain-only gold reference. So behavioral forgetting does not imply internal erasure. The result appears across methods and datasets, suggesting this is not an artifact of one benchmark.

Second, pre-attack J-Access predicts model-level recovery speed under cross-entity relearning attacks. Models with higher residual access tend to recover forgotten knowledge faster under a small amount of continued training. However, the signal is much weaker for predicting which specific items will recover. That indicates J-Access behaves more like a global accessibility or connectivity measure than an instance-level fate predictor.

Third, directly minimizing J-Access during unlearning lowers the audit score but increases post-attack recovery. The model learns to evade the readout rather than delete the underlying knowledge. This is the most consequential result in the paper. It shows that a useful audit can become a misleading optimization target.

The paper also includes analyses across identity-level versus knowledge-level token sets, different layer bands, and comparisons with standard behavioral metrics. The emphasis is on trends, correlations, and attack outcomes rather than a single headline scalar improvement.1b:T421,This is a skim paper, but the setup is worth reading carefully. Start with the introduction, especially the distinction between two goals: forecasting future recovery and using an internal signal as an optimization target. That framing is more important than the exact formula.

Then read the J-Access method section. Focus on why the authors reject the plain logit lens in favor of the Jacobian lens, and how the concept token sets are defined and normalized. Those choices determine what the metric is actually measuring.

In the experiments, prioritize the relearning-attack sections and the ablation where J-Access is directly optimized. The former tells you whether the signal has predictive value; the latter tells you whether it survives contact with training. If time is limited, those parts are enough to capture the paper’s main message in about 25 to 35 minutes.

The deeper question to keep in mind is whether J-Access is measuring memorized content itself or merely the ease with which latent content can be reconnected to the output pathway.1c:T43e,The evaluation uses two scenarios. For biohazardous knowledge, the authors filter WMDP biology to 189 targeted multiple-choice questions, generate three paraphrases per question, use all paraphrases for unlearning, split questions for relearning versus held-out MCQ evaluation, and use domain-matched biology text from FineFineWeb as retain data. For harmful behavioral tendency removal, they use the animal_abuse category from BeaverTails and construct a synthetic retain set by replacing harmful concepts with benign counterparts while keeping surrounding context. The models include Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen 3.5 9B, Gemma 4 E4B, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite, covering dense and MoE families. Methods are compared under a fixed disruption budget: training stops once KL(pθ0∥pθ) on WikiText exceeds 0.01, roughly a 0.4% NLL increase. RepSelect reports an 8–50× larger post-relearning answer-probability reduction than the best baseline, NPO, on biohazardous knowledge, and a 4–15× larger reduction than NPO or RMU on animal-abuse tendencies, while matching general capability preservation.1d:T5dc,The method is ATWU, Alternating Token-Weighted Unlearning. The paper first formalizes a notion of forget-specificity: a forget token is important to unlearn if applying the forget objective to that token is compatible with staying near retain optimality; if forgetting that token strongly conflicts with the retain objective, it is more likely to be structural rather than target-bearing. This turns token weights into latent variables in a joint optimization over model parameters and token importance. Under a stated separation condition, the authors argue that the objective can recover the oracle support of forget-specific tokens. In practice, ATWU uses a lightweight scorer to assign token weights from hidden states, parameterized as a linear direction in the LM representation space rather than a separate large auxiliary model. Training alternates between updating the scorer and updating the model. The intuition is that direct simultaneous optimization can be unstable because the model and the weights co-adapt too quickly; alternating updates make the procedure closer to an EM-like loop where token responsibility is estimated and then used to drive parameter updates. The method is compared against sample-level unlearning, probability-based token heuristics, and auxiliary-model-based weighting. Its main claim is that the retain objective is not just a regularizer but also an informative signal for identifying which forget tokens should actually receive strong forgetting pressure.1e:T4b6,Experiments are conducted on TOFU and RWKU. The provided text does not include the full numeric tables, but the qualitative findings are clear. First, ATWU improves the forget–retain trade-off over sample-level baselines, heuristic token weighting, and auxiliary-model-based methods, indicating that learned token importance reduces unnecessary collateral damage. Second, the paper evaluates not only end-task unlearning metrics but also alignment between learned token scores and ground-truth forget-specific spans. The learned weights concentrate more on names, dates, attribute-bearing phrases, and rare identifiers, rather than on function words or punctuation. Third, the empirical evidence is aligned with the theory: the paper argues that retain conflict should define forget-specificity, and then shows that the learned scores track the content-bearing spans while preserving retain behavior better.
