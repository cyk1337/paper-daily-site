9:T40f,The analysis covers 1,338 public agent trajectories on PostTrainBench, spanning seven benchmarks, four base models from 1.7B to 4B parameters, and 20 agent configurations across five scaffolds. Each trajectory has a 10-hour budget on one NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU. The main finding is strategy lock-in. The high-level strategy is usually fixed at the beginning, before code and experiments provide feedback, and the rest of the run is spent on local adjustments. The pattern depends more on the agent scaffold than on the task: 80.7% of Claude Code trajectories converge on full-parameter SFT, while 89.6% of Codex CLI trajectories converge on PEFT. The experience scaffold improves execution, with reported gains of +12.6 points on GSM8K and +40.8 on HumanEval, but does not make strategies adaptive. Human review can redirect the initial strategy before training begins, yet agents still fall back to local adjustment after training starts. Spending 2–8x more inference tokens helps easier tasks but gives almost no gain on the hardest one.a:T8ab,The paper has two main technical threads. The first is diagnosing and aligning the model’s perceived knowledge boundary. The authors estimate internal knowledge availability with avg@1024, the average correctness over 1,024 independent samples for the same question. Higher avg@1024 is treated as stronger evidence that the model can solve the item without external help. They then bucket examples by this proxy and analyze tool-use behavior across knowledge regimes. The key finding is that tool calls do not reliably decrease as internal knowledge availability increases. In other words, the model is not performing capability-aware routing. It behaves as if it has already reached its knowledge boundary even when it has not.

To mitigate this, the authors build preference pairs that favor trajectories producing correct answers with minimal tool use over trajectories that are correct but tool-heavy, or incorrect and tool-heavy. They then apply a knowledge-aware DPO procedure. The novelty is less about the DPO form itself and more about the preference construction objective: it explicitly aligns perceived epistemic boundaries with actual capability.

The second thread analyzes RLVR incentives. Tool reasoning is formulated as a POMDP with a hybrid action space consisting of token-generation actions and tool actions. Under outcome-only rewards, any unnecessary tool call that still leads to a correct final answer receives no penalty and can be positively reinforced through credit assignment. As training proceeds, the policy drifts toward a conservative outsource-first behavior. The authors validate this under a DAPO/GRPO-style RLVR setup and observe increasing tool-call turns over training. They then propose a reward design that balances answer correctness and tool efficiency. The key idea is to estimate a capability ceiling through larger inter-group rollout sampling and incorporate tool efficiency into the relative reward signal. This is more nuanced than a fixed tool penalty because it depends on whether the model could likely solve the item internally, reducing the risk of penalizing genuinely necessary tool use. The paper also includes theoretical arguments supporting both mechanisms.b:T4e9,The empirical story is fairly coherent. Across frontier, open-source, and RLVR-trained models, the authors find pervasive tool overuse: on average, 0.93 unnecessary tool calls per query for questions that do not require external assistance. More importantly, enabling tool use causes a 3.29% to 14.48% accuracy drop on questions solvable from internal knowledge alone. That makes the issue a capability-routing problem, not just an efficiency concern.

In the knowledge-boundary analysis, one of the clearest examples is Qwen3-8B: even in a high-knowledge regime with avg@1024 > 0.8, it still makes 2.2 tool calls per query on average. For mitigation, the knowledge-aware DPO setup reduces tool overuse by 82.8% on a 32B-scale model while improving response accuracy by about 3%.

For RLVR, the paper reports that tool-call turns increase progressively with training and eventually rise by 65%, supporting the claim that outcome-only rewards worsen overuse. The revised reward reduces tool-call turns by 66.7% on a 7B model and 60.7% on a 32B model on math reasoning benchmarks, while maintaining answer accuracy.
