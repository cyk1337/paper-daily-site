9:T65c,The method is called signed symmetric quantization. Standard symmetric quantization uses a signed alphabet with z = 0 and a positive absmax scale. The authors observe that because the alphabet is inherently skewed by one extra negative value, allowing the scale to take either sign effectively lets that extra endpoint protect either the negative tail or the positive tail. Flipping the sign of the scale does not introduce a zero point, so the deployed format remains symmetric and avoids the offset-handling overhead of asymmetric quantization.

The main contribution is not merely allowing negative scales, but providing a closed-form sign selection rule that is data-free in deployment and metadata-free when scales are already stored as signed real values. Intuitively, the rule aligns the extra representable endpoint with the dominant outlier direction, reducing clipping-driven error. The paper also provides theory: a worst-case l2 error bound, conditional bound-optimality of the proposed sign rule, and an analytical equivalence between a scale-sign flip and a unit zero-point shift on the same signed alphabet. That equivalence is important because it clarifies what the method gains: a restricted form of asymmetric alignment while preserving the symmetric deployment path.

From a systems perspective, the proposal is compelling because many real formats already store scales as signed floating-point values. In that setting, the change is nearly free. The authors also implement corresponding 2-bit and 4-bit formats in a llama.cpp/GGUF-style setup to show that the idea survives contact with real inference code.a:T657,The evaluation covers model families including Llama3, Qwen3, and Qwen3.5, with metrics spanning WikiText2 perplexity, few-shot accuracy recovery, and actual deployment throughput and memory. The most striking result is on Llama3 8B at 2 bits: WikiText2 perplexity drops from 103.1 under the conventional positive-scale symmetric quantizer to 17.8 with the proposed signed symmetric variant, and few-shot accuracy recovery improves by 7.9 percentage points. That is a large effect for such a small change, and it confirms that endpoint assignment can dominate error in the ultra-low-bit regime.

The comparison to asymmetric quantization is equally important. On an AMD EPYC Turin CPU, the paper reports that a 4-bit symmetric format can use about 9% less memory and achieve around 2.21x higher prefill and 2.03x higher decode throughput than its asymmetric counterpart. The abstract also reports that the proposed signed symmetric method retains a 1.24x prefill advantage, a 1.08x decode advantage, and 14% memory savings over asymmetric quantization while substantially improving over the conventional symmetric baseline. So the goal is not to beat asymmetric quantization on every accuracy metric, but to recover much of its alignment benefit without giving up the symmetric deployment profile.

A useful pattern in the full results is that gains are largest at the lowest bit widths. As precision increases, the benefit shrinks because one-sided clipping is no longer the dominant error source. The inclusion of few-shot accuracy recovery, not just perplexity, also makes the evaluation more relevant to real LLM usage.b:T411,This is a good paper for a fast but careful read.
