18:T4e8,The paper uses Partial Information Decomposition to separate multimodal information into four channels. Redundant information R is accessible from either modality. Unique information U_1 and U_2 is accessible only from modality 1 or modality 2. Synergistic information S is accessible only when both modalities are present together. The proposed scaling law writes data loss as a sum of four independent power-law curves. Each curve receives only the relevant data stream: R can be reduced by either modality, U_1 by modality 1 data, U_2 by modality 2 data, and S only by the paired count n_p. This differs from prior L(N, D_1, D_2) forms because it gives paired data its own causal role rather than folding it into modality counts. The experimental protocol is built to isolate this variable. Unpaired examples are represented by a learnable null vector plus a modality-presence bit. At test time, both modalities are always present. Cross-entropy is the main loss, with accuracy checked separately. The main sweeps fix architecture and parameter count, folding finite-model effects into E_N, while appendices repeat Digits and VQA under different architectures and parameter sizes to check that the qualitative conclusions are not tied to a single model.19:T5ca,The paper evaluates three settings. Digits combines MNIST images and AudioMNIST spectrograms in a four-digit classification task where the four positions correspond to U_1, U_2, R, and S, giving explicit control over the information channels. VQA trains classification heads on top of frozen pretrained embeddings; this setting is dominated by the text modality and is useful for observing how unpaired data helps non-synergistic information. NLVR2 finetunes a pretrained multimodal transformer on a task where mutual information is largely synergistic and unimodal models remain near chance. Across settings, the authors sweep total data D, compute budget C, paired count n_p, and multiple random seeds. They count D as D_1 + D_2, the sum of unique examples per modality. As p approaches 1, the number of unique rows decreases; training steps are fixed, so fully paired data sees more epochs but the same number of gradient updates. This counting convention favors unpaired data, making improvements from higher p more attributable to pairing itself. The main findings are consistent: pairing ratio has a large effect on loss; laws without p cannot capture the observed curves; only paired data reduces synergistic loss; unpaired data reduces redundant or unimodal loss only up to the relevant floors; and synergy shows a threshold-like acquisition pattern.
