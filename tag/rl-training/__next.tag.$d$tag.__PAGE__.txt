The paper argues that a single process signal is insufficient. Forward-looking rewards encourage locally helpful search, but they do not distinguish necessary steps from redundant ones. Pure hindsight credit can identify pivotal decisions after the fact, but it does not tell the agent whether a step actually improved the information state when it happened. BiCAA combines both.

The first branch is forward solvability gain. For a question with ground-truth answer, each retrieval step is treated as incremental evidence acquisition. At step t, the method compares the model’s conditional log-likelihood of the correct answer given the trajectory prefix up to t versus up to t-1. This marginal change measures whether the newly retrieved evidence makes the answer more solvable. In other words, it is an immediate estimate of how much that search action improved the model’s belief in the correct answer.

The second branch is hindsight success criticality. This branch looks back over the full trajectory and estimates whether a step was actually pivotal for reaching a correct final answer, rather than merely producing a small local gain. The full formula is not visible in the provided excerpt, but the intended role is clear: steps that are causally important to successful trajectories should receive more credit, while accidental or redundant steps should be downweighted.

BiCAA then uses a modulation rule to combine the two signals. A step that both increases local solvability and proves critical in hindsight gets amplified credit. A step with only weak local gain or low retrospective importance gets reduced credit. Finally, the calibrated process reward is fused with the trajectory-level outcome reward through unified advantage estimation for RL updates. The novelty is therefore in reward decomposition and credit shaping, not in a new RL optimizer.d:T460,From the available full-text excerpt, the experimental story is centered on two outcomes: final answer quality and search behavior quality. The second component is Numca, a domain-specific heuristic for mathematical reasoning. The paper reframes math problem solving as sparse-reward goal reaching and observes that numerical spans in intermediate reasoning often act as natural milestones. Numca groups disjoint rollouts using these numeric anchors and uses them to estimate future return from intermediate states. Conceptually, it borrows the spirit of hindsight-style relabeling, but adapted to text reasoning through easily parsable numbers. It is simple and cheap, but clearly tied to domains where numbers are meaningful intermediate structure.

The third component is Hista, the general solution. Hista represents each generation step with the model's hidden state and estimates the value of a state by probability-weighted averaging of returns from semantically similar states found in disjoint rollouts. It is a non-parametric estimator built on the intuition that similar hidden states should imply similar future rewards. Importantly, it requires neither extra rollouts nor additional critic training. The paper also provides a theoretical result showing that, under its assumptions, Hista yields lower estimation error than the group-average baseline. Compared with PPO critics, Hista avoids the difficulty of fitting token-level values from sparse terminal rewards. A student ViT reconstructs masked regions in the feature spaces of two teachers: SigLIP2 for semantic representations and DINOv3 for texture and local structure. The setup uses a 35% masking ratio at 224x224 resolution, with a quality-aware data mixture of 80% high-quality natural images, 10% instruction-following data, and 10% scientific imagery. The intent is clear: build a strong visual backbone before forcing it into cross-modal alignment. Training uses the Muon optimizer, and QK-Norm is introduced to stabilize attention by mitigating logit explosion. Stage 2 switches to contrastive image-text pretraining with the SigLIP loss. The main upgrades are variable-resolution NaFlex inputs, a 64K global batch for large-scale contrastive learning, and an 8B bilingual Chinese-English image-text corpus. This is a representation-first, alignment-second recipe rather than pure contrastive training from scratch.

Second, MMTP, or Multimodal Multi-Token Prediction. In text-only MTP, prefix tokens can be passed naturally into the MTP head via token embeddings. In multimodal settings, however, it is unclear how image tokens should be exposed to the MTP head. The paper compares three options: directly passing visual embeddings, masking out visual tokens entirely at the MTP head, or preserving visual positional information while replacing visual content with a shared learnable <|image|> token. GLM-5V-Turbo adopts the third option. The reason is the trade-off between optimization and infrastructure efficiency. Direct visual embeddings make the head heavier and potentially less stable; removing visual tokens entirely loses multimodal conditioning; the shared special token preserves the structural signal that image content exists at certain positions without injecting full visual features into the MTP head. This is one of the most interesting parts of the paper for foundation-model training.

Third, multimodal training is deeply integrated across pretraining and supervised fine-tuning, rather than adding vision only at the instruction-tuning stage. The public text does not expose every data-mixture and curriculum detail, but the intended direction is clear: vision should participate in the model’s reasoning backbone, not merely provide a few side tokens.
