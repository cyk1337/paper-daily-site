19:T6bf,The method has three main components. The first is synthetic data construction. Quest-8K is built as more than query-answer pairs: each instance couples a complex research query with a task-specific rubric tree. The rubric encodes subgoals, evidence requirements, and output structure, so it serves both as supervision and as the basis for downstream reward design. Compared with synthesizing only final answers, this is better aligned with long-horizon tasks because both intermediate behavior and final reports can be assessed in a structured way.

The second component is context management. Long web-research trajectories quickly run into context bloat: search results, snippets, discovered facts, unresolved subproblems, and partial drafts all accumulate. Quest maintains compact summaries of the evolving information state instead of naively replaying the full history into the context window. This is not only a token-saving trick. It pushes the model toward abstraction and state tracking, which is closer to how a practical research agent should operate than simple retrieval concatenation.

The third component is the training pipeline itself. The paper emphasizes a full MT → SFT → RL recipe. Mid-training adapts the base model to the distribution and behavior patterns of deep-research tasks. SFT aligns the model to the target interfaces for tool use, task formatting, and report generation. RL then uses rubric-based signals to improve the harder objectives, especially report synthesis and citation grounding, where exact-match supervision is weak or unavailable. The novelty is less about a single module and more about unifying objective and open-ended tasks under one rubric-driven optimization framework.1a:T626,The reported results span model sizes from 2B to 35B and evaluate across eight benchmarks covering the three capability groups. The headline numbers are Quest-35B reaching 64.6% on BrowseComp, 30.7% on Mind2Web 2, and 48.2% on DeepResearch Bench, with the paper claiming it surpasses strong proprietary systems including OpenAI DeepResearch. If these results hold under the paper’s setup, the important point is that the unified recipe does not appear to trade away one capability class to gain another.

The more informative part is the stage-wise ablation story. Figure 2 and the surrounding discussion make a clear argument: prior open systems often support only fact seeking or only report synthesis, and many omit either mid-training or rubric-based RL. Quest is designed to show that SFT alone gives incomplete coverage; adding mid-training improves adaptation to search-style tasks and long-horizon state handling; adding RL further improves open-ended report quality and citation-constrained behavior. The excerpt provided here does not include the full ablation tables, but the paper explicitly positions controlled studies as a core contribution.

Another notable result is scaling behavior. The study covers 2B, 4B, 9B, and 35B models, aiming to show that deep-research-agent capability scales with model size but is not exclusive to large models. The authors also highlight strong small-model results, including Quest-2B-SFT and Quest-4B-SFT, suggesting that the gains come not only from model capacity but also from better training signals and task structure.1b:T443,This is a skim paper, so read for recipe design rather than implementation minutiae. Start with the introduction and Figure 2 to understand the gap the authors are trying to fill: unified support for fact seeking, citation grounding, and report synthesis. Then read the synthetic data section carefully, especially how Quest-8K and the rubric trees are built, because that determines whether the later RL signal is meaningful. After that, inspect the context-management section and ask what state is preserved, how aggressively it is compressed, and whether evidence chains are lost. Finish with the training and ablation sections, focusing on three questions: what exactly is used in mid-training, how rubric trees are converted into executable rewards, and which stage contributes most to each capability.

If time is limited to 20–30 minutes, the best path is Figure 1/2, then the data synthesis section, then the training recipe, then the main result tables. If you want one part to study more deeply, choose the rubric-based RL design.
