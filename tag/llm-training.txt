Starting from unlabeled prompts, the base model generates candidate solutions; the same model, or a procedure built from it, then produces verification judgments, feedback, or revisions; these internal signals are converted into preference data for training.

The first strategy is single-round verification. The model samples multiple candidate answers and uses an internal verifier to score or classify them, producing chosen/rejected pairs. This is the simplest generate-then-rank setup. It is cheap and easy to implement, but its ceiling is tightly coupled to verifier noise.

The second strategy is multi-turn revision with feedback. Instead of only assigning a scalar or binary judgment, the model critiques an answer, provides natural-language feedback, and then revises the solution. This richer signal matters because it turns sparse preference supervision into directional error information. For reasoning tasks, that is often more useful than a bare preference label, since many failures come from incorrect intermediate constraint propagation rather than surface-level formatting issues.

The third strategy is iterative training. The authors repeat the offline self-evolution cycle: generate internal supervision with the current model, train on it, then regenerate data with the updated model and continue. This mirrors practical self-training and self-rewarding loops. The paper shows that iteration helps, but with diminishing returns, and that errors can be repeatedly reinforced if verifier quality does not improve in parallel.

The fourth strategy is curriculum learning. Knights-and-Knaves provides a natural difficulty axis, so the authors train from easier to harder instances. The role of curriculum here is not just standard optimization smoothing. It improves the effective quality of internal supervision, because self-verification and self-revision are more reliable on easier examples. The model can first stabilize reasoning patterns in a higher-confidence regime before moving to harder cases.

The central comparison is against oracle preference optimization. This is an important design choice. The question is not whether self-evolution beats the base model, but how much learnable information is lost when preference labels come from the model itself rather than from ground-truth-derived oracle signals.

The task choice is also deliberate. Knights-and-Knaves offers deterministic unique solutions, controllable difficulty, and low contamination risk, making it a good testbed for studying generalization rather than benchmark memorization. The first is pre-quantization normalization. The authors argue that if gradients or momentum are quantized directly at each step, the absolute scale of the quantization error changes with the state magnitude. Over many steps, this can accumulate into a preferred direction. Since Muon preserves direction and discards singular-value magnitudes, such bias is especially damaging. MuonQ normalizes the state before quantization so that each step contributes errors of comparable magnitude. The goal is not simply to reduce one-step MSE, but to make accumulated error more isotropic over time.

The second is structural decomposition. Direct low-bit quantization of the full momentum matrix can perturb singular vectors, and those perturbations survive the orthogonalization step. MuonQ instead decomposes the momentum structurally and quantizes the singular factors separately, so that quantization error is pushed more into singular-value magnitudes than into singular-vector directions. This matches Muon’s update rule: singular-value errors are largely washed out by the polar operation, while directional errors are preserved. The paper also proposes a truncated top-k variant to trade off memory and fidelity. The important point is not decomposition for its own sake, but deliberate error reallocation into the part of the representation that Muon is less sensitive to.

The third is μ-law companding quantization. The authors argue that Muon needs higher resolution near zero because, after polar decomposition, different singular directions are treated more evenly than in Adam-like optimizers. Uniform quantization wastes bins on large magnitudes and under-resolves the dense region near zero, where subtle directional information can still matter. Companding compresses the dynamic range before quantization and allocates more effective resolution to that dense region.

Together, these components form a coherent design: normalization controls the geometry of accumulated error, structural decomposition controls where the error lands, and companding improves resolution where Muon is most sensitive.
