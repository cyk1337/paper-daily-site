18:T417,UMoE has three stages. First, it prunes low-saliency experts using target-domain calibration data. The default score is REAP saliency, which combines the magnitude of an expert’s MLP output with its normalized router gate, averaged only over tokens where that expert is selected by top-K routing; unselected experts receive zero score. The method keeps the top half of experts per layer. This is different from routing-frequency pruning, because frequent routing does not necessarily imply strong contribution to the target-domain computation. Second, it regrows the expert pool through perturbation-based expansion from the retained experts, restoring the original number of experts. Third, it applies standard domain SFT. The final model keeps the same number of experts E, active experts K, parameter count, and inference cost. Compared with direct SFT, it explicitly reallocates expert slots; compared with permanent pruning, it preserves capacity; compared with generic upcycling, its expansion source is selected by target-domain saliency.19:T578,The audit is clean and well targeted. The authors study three high-redundancy MoE LMs: OLMoE-1B-7B-0924, Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite. These models span major MoE design choices: different load-balancing objectives, different top-k activation ratios, scratch training versus dense upcycling, and the presence or absence of shared experts. At five representative layers per model, they evaluate four standard observational metrics: utilization rate, activation norm, mean routing weight when active, and activation standard deviation. These are corpus-level expert summaries. They then compare them against token-level interventional effects using router-aware ablation. For an expert active at token t, they zero that expert’s output and measure the resulting next-token loss change, ΔL_i^(t), as the functional-importance target. They also report residual-stream displacement, the gap norm, as a representation-level effect size. To rule out lack of statistical power, they add a stronger control: instead of using population-averaged routing summaries, they use the token-conditioned routing weight g_i(x_t) itself. This gives routing-derived signals their best possible chance. The key design choice is that the comparison is made on the same token, layer, and active-expert event, so the paper is genuinely testing whether observational rankings predict interventional consequences.1a:T466,The main result is a strong null. Across 60 metric-layer combinations—four observational metrics, five layers, three models—none reaches Bonferroni-corrected significance. Effect sizes remain small throughout, with Cohen’s d below 0.17, and even the sign is not stable across layers. In other words, standard observational metrics have essentially no predictive value for token-level expert ablation effects in these models. The routing-weight control shows this is not a power issue: under the same machinery and sample size, token-conditioned routing weight recovers one Bonferroni-significant effect. It appears only in OLMoE, at the final MoE layer, with d = +0.231 and p = 0.0013. In that setting, expert ablation moves the residual stream by roughly 40 times more than in early layers. Qwen and DeepSeek-V2-Lite do not show a comparable late-layer concentration. The progressive-ablation analysis is also important: it suggests many pruning methods work because early layers are redundant enough that many selection criteria are interchangeable, not because the metrics truly identify causally unimportant experts.1b:T425,Read the introduction carefully first, because it draws a useful boundary between two findings: a general observational null and a narrow late-layer effect in OLMoE under direct token conditioning. In the experiments, focus on three pieces. First, the main significance and effect-size tables showing the failure of all four observational metrics across models and layers. For MoE researchers, this is worth reading closely, including the appendix, because the conclusion depends on careful intervention design and statistical treatment.1c:T840,The method has three stages.

First is expert selection. For each layer, all E experts are scored and the top-K are retained. Here k is the original router top-k, while K is the number of experts selected for conversion. The paper evaluates seven scoring methods and finds that scoring dominates the final outcome. The main technical novelty is a diversity-aware criterion: instead of selecting experts only by frequency or activation strength, it jointly rewards importance and non-redundancy. Concretely, the authors build an importance-weighted Gram matrix and use a log-determinant objective, a D-optimal selection criterion, to favor experts that are both strong and diverse. Their best variant is called DO-ACP.

Second is grouping, merging, and densification. If K > k, the selected experts are assigned to k groups and merged within each group by score-weighted averaging; if K = k, they are copied directly without merging. The resulting k experts are then concatenated along the intermediate dimension to form a standard dense FFN. Because an MoE output is originally a router-weighted sum while a dense FFN uses a single down projection after concatenation, the paper also studies down-projection scaling to preserve output magnitude after conversion.

Third is knowledge distillation from the MoE teacher to recover quality lost in the structural conversion. A useful part of the experimental design is that the authors first search over recipes under a short distillation budget, then verify whether the best recipe remains best under longer distillation. That makes the conclusions more credible than a single hand-picked configuration.

Two takeaways stand out. First, expert selection matters much more than grouping, which means the upper bound is mostly determined by which experts are kept, not by how they are merged. Second, the best configuration is often pure pruning: select diverse experts and concatenate them directly, without averaging. That suggests many experts are specialized rather than trivially mergeable; averaging can erase useful specialization if selection is already good.1d:T825,The experimental study is broad. The main sweep is on Qwen3-30B-A3B, covering 7 expert-scoring methods, 5 grouping strategies, 2 down-projection scaling choices, and multiple K values, for a total of 350 configurations. The best findings are then validated on DeepSeek-V2-Lite and GPT-OSS-20B.

Four results matter most.

First, scoring is the dominant factor. The gap between the best and worst scoring methods is 5.7 percentage points in average downstream accuracy, while grouping contributes only about 1 point. That is a strong signal that selection quality is the real bottleneck.

Second, the best recipe is diversity-aware scoring plus pure pruning. In other words, DO-ACP selects a diverse set of experts and direct concatenation works better than weight averaging. Frequency-based scoring tends to pick redundant experts, so it benefits more from merging. This is an informative result: merging is not inherently helpful; it mainly compensates for poor selection.

Third, MoE-to-dense beats dense-to-dense pruning in a controlled comparison. With roughly matched teacher size (~30B) and student size (~3B), the best MoE-to-dense configuration outperforms dense-to-dense pruning by +6.3 points after about 4B distillation tokens, while being 1.6x faster in wall-clock training time. This is one of the strongest claims in the paper because it argues not just that conversion is possible, but that the MoE teacher is a better starting point.

Fourth, the recipe selected under a short distillation budget (~0.3B tokens) remains strong under extended distillation, and the trend transfers across three model families. The provided excerpt does not include every benchmark name and score, but the evaluation is clearly broader than a single perplexity comparison.

A boundary to keep in mind: the paper compares compression pipelines, not the absolute best dense student obtainable from scratch, and it does not fully explain why MoE teachers are better distillation sources at the representation level. The evidence is strong for engineering choice, less complete for mechanism.1e:T52a,A good skim should be conclusion-driven.

Start with the overview figure and the conversion pipeline in the method section. Make sure the distinction between K and k is clear, and understand why the active parameter budget of an MoE maps naturally to the intermediate width of a dense FFN. Without that mapping, the rest can look like a collection of heuristics.

Then focus on expert scoring. The diversity-aware Gram log-det objective is the most novel part of the paper. Pay attention to why it captures both importance and diversity, and how it differs from simpler signals such as routing frequency, router probability, or weight magnitude.

Next, read the main comparison sections: the 350-configuration sweep, the relative impact of scoring versus grouping, and the controlled MoE-to-dense versus dense-to-dense experiment. Those sections determine whether the paper is merely another compression recipe or a stronger statement about teacher choice.
