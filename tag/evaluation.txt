18:T650,The experimental design is simple and careful. The authors hold everything fixed and vary only the date string in the system prompt, sweeping every day from January 1 to December 31, 2024. They evaluate 9 recent models on 6 standard datasets spanning four task types: MCQA (MMLU, GPQA, ARC-Challenge), math reasoning (GSM8K), code generation (HumanEval), and machine translation (WMT English→German/Finnish/Czech). They explicitly verify that the MCQA questions are not time-dependent.

The setup is deterministic by construction, so the date effect can be isolated from known sources of instability such as hardware, batch size, and numerical precision. The task selection is also deliberate. MCQA tests a setting where the final decision often depends on a single answer token. GSM8K, HumanEval, and MT progressively increase the role of long autoregressive generation, execution-based evaluation, and full-output scoring. This lets the paper test whether a hidden prefix perturbation grows as generation length increases.

A useful mechanism proposed in the analysis is that longer generations give the date prefix repeated opportunities to bias intermediate token choices. Small perturbations then cascade through autoregressive decoding, producing larger score differences on reasoning, coding, and translation than on single-token multiple-choice decisions. The paper also checks common prompting strategies and finds that chain-of-thought and few-shot prompting do not remove the effect; chain-of-thought can even amplify it because it lengthens the trajectory over which the prefix perturbation propagates.19:T4d1,The evaluation covers Llama 3.1 Instruct 8B/70B, Gemma 3 Instruct 4B/27B, Qwen3 4B, Qwen3-Next 80B, Phi-4 14B, and GPT-OSS 20B/120B. Dates are swept across the full year of 2024. Metrics are accuracy and ECE for MCQA, accuracy for GSM8K, pass@1 for HumanEval, and BLEU/chrF for MT.

The effect sizes are large. On MCQA, changing only the date yields up to 6% accuracy variation and can reorder model rankings. On GSM8K, the average worst-to-best delta is reported as 7.75%, with some models reaching 14%. On HumanEval, the average pass@1 delta is 4.81%, with the most affected model reaching 7.32%. On MT, the average variation reaches 1.88 BLEU and 1.33 chrF, with a maximum BLEU swing of 2.84. These are not small numbers relative to typical benchmark improvements.

The paper also compares the date effect against other known instability sources and finds it larger than batch-size and precision effects. Model scale does not eliminate the problem. The correlation analysis further shows that there is no simple monotonic trend over the year and no stable set of universally good or bad dates, which suggests a complex interaction between hidden prompt metadata and internal model dynamics rather than a trivial seasonal artifact.1a:T710,The work has two technical pieces. First, behavioral auditing. The authors define the Behavioral Necessity Rate (BNR): among instances answered correctly under the full context, remove one annotated evidence target at a time and measure how often the model can no longer recover the answer. This turns the vague notion of “using the intended hop” into an intervention-based behavioral test. To avoid conflating evidence necessity with generic sensitivity to context deletion, they introduce a matched-background control. This is important: a raw accuracy drop after deletion could simply reflect that the example became noisier or shorter in an unfavorable way. The control isolates whether the failure is specific to the claimed evidence.

Second, RealHop, a diagnose-construct-verify framework for rebuilding multi-hop questions so that every step is required. The construction uses three main mechanisms: entity rebinding to break parametric-memory shortcuts and familiar associations; relation splitting / evidence rewriting to force composition across multiple premises rather than allowing a single-step inference; and complete competing paths to alternative answers that look structurally plausible but fail on a required relation, reducing shallow elimination strategies. The authors also separate construction from verification: they freeze the dataset after structural and semantic checks, then run post hoc deletion-based auditing. This avoids overfitting the benchmark to a particular model during creation. For datasets without native hop annotations, such as FRAMES, they reconstruct intervention-ready support units from source-linked evidence. For LongBench v2, they adapt long-context questions into auditable multiple-choice and repeated open-ended protocols to reduce evaluation noise.1b:T564,The central empirical finding is that standard multi-hop benchmarks have surprisingly low behavioral necessity. Across MuSiQue, HotpotQA, and 2WikiMultiHopQA, panel-mean BNR is only 16.6% to 27.4%. Even adversarially designed benchmarks intended to reduce shortcuts only reach 43.5% to 48.9%. This means that annotated chains and distractor-heavy construction do not guarantee that correct predictions depend on the intended evidence.

On rebuilt benchmarks, the picture changes sharply. On 790 paired MuSiQue questions, RealHop-MuSiQue raises panel-mean BNR from 27.4% to 94.4%, while full-context accuracy remains in the 68.5% to 83.5% range. That is an important result: the benchmark becomes much more diagnostic without collapsing answerability. On source-linked FRAMES questions, RealHop-FRAMES reaches 88.9% to 89.9% BNR despite the lack of native hop annotations. On 216 long-context questions derived from LongBench v2, the matched multiple-choice protocol expands the accuracy spread across 16 models from 13.9 points to 59.2 points. The wider separation persists under repeated open-ended evaluation, and targeted deletion yields 96.5% to 98.1% BNR for the three audited models. The ablations indicate that no single construction trick is sufficient; entity rebinding, relation splitting, and competing paths each contribute to making every step behaviorally necessary.1c:T426,The paper introduces an MMORPG-style sandbox plus a curated benchmark of collaborative tasks. The simulator supports up to 1,000 concurrent agents and includes more than 380 items, 144 mob types, over 70 NPCs, and 13 high-level API tools for actions such as combat, crafting, gathering, and navigation. The high-level API design is important: it removes much of the low-level control burden so that benchmark scores reflect collaboration rather than 3D manipulation skill. The benchmark contains 100 human-annotated tasks and 100 LLM-augmented variants across combat, crafting, gathering, trading, exploration, survival, construction, and coordination. Tasks require 3 to 20 agents over 25 to 55 rounds, with asymmetric roles and no access to other agents’ internal states. The proposed Causal Collaboration Effectiveness metric builds a causal action graph over trajectories and measures the fraction of actions that contributed to the final outcome, using LLMs mainly to help infer action-level causal links rather than to issue a single subjective judgment.1d:T457,The method uses a closed-testing construction. An independent proposal split nominates one candidate-specific floor c_k for each policy. A fresh certification split then runs an exact binomial test for H_k: P(S_k < c_k) ≥ α. Rejecting this null certifies that the candidate’s lower α-quantile is at least the proposed floor. Holm’s step-down procedure controls the family-wise error rate at δ across candidates. The selected policy is the certified candidate with the largest floor; the procedure abstains if no certified floor exceeds a predeclared operating threshold. The guarantee allows arbitrary within-unit dependence across candidates, such as correlated judge scores on the same prompt, while requiring i.i.d. evaluation units. Compared with matched-proposal Bonferroni, Holm gives a uniformly stronger rejection set on every dataset. Compared with exact COPS, it certifies a different statistical object, so uniform dominance is not claimed. The score map s_k is treated as a fixed measurable function; it need not be unbiased, Gaussian, asymptotically normal, or a mean off-policy estimator.1e:T8bf,论文建立了一个固定 cohort 的 repeated evaluation 框架。共有 M 个任务，每个任务预先规划 L 条重复路径，总共 N=ML 条路径；每条路径最多消耗 K 次模型响应或完整 agent episode。路径的结果用 first decisive event index T_ir 表示，若在 K 步内没有出现 decisive event，则终端标签 Y_ir=1，否则为 0。目标不是未来任务分布上的泛化均值，而是这个预先固定评测网格的经验均值 θ_C，并要求对任意可能实现的 cohort 都有 coverage 保证，也就是 fixed-cohort honest inference。

核心理论结果是两条 certification law。若设计要求每个 task 至少完成一条路径，也就是 task coverage 不能缺失，则最优最坏情形区间宽度阶为 Θ([M(t+1)]^{-1/2})；若允许省掉部分 task，把预算集中到少数任务的更多重复上，则最优阶变为 Θ([M(t+√M)]^{-1/2})。这里 t 可以理解为在覆盖每个 task 之后额外保留出来用于 replication/certification 的预算单位。作者强调 lower bound 覆盖了自适应 hard-budget policy，也就是说即便允许根据已观察结果动态调度预算，数量级上也不能突破这个界。

为了让理论可执行，论文给出固定随机子集设计和 disagreement-based certificate。直觉上，若同一 task 内计划标签高度一致，一次观测就足以把均值估得很准；但要证明这种一致性，需要额外抽样去测 disagreement。于是区间构造不只依赖 sampled mean，也显式依赖 disagreement count，形成所谓 Joint interval。这个设计比单纯把所有已观测路径当成 pooled Bernoulli 做统一推断更贴近“先覆盖任务、再花预算做认证”的实际评测流程。

论文还区分了两个目标：一个是对固定 benchmark grid 的诚实区间，另一个是对未来任务或未来运行的 population generalization。前者不需要对任务和运行结果做独立同分布假设；若要桥接到总体均值，附录里再加 Hoeffding 项。这种区分是合理的，因为很多模型发布和训练 gating 场景，真正关心的是“这次固定评测表上的分数是否足够可信”，而不是统计学意义上的外推。1f:T84b,The paper formalizes repeated evaluation on a fixed cohort. There are M tasks and L planned repetition paths per task, for N = ML total paths. Each path costs at most K model responses or full agent episodes. A path outcome is represented by a first decisive-event index T_ir, and the terminal label Y_ir indicates whether no decisive event occurred within K steps. The estimand is the empirical mean over this prespecified grid, denoted theta_C, and the requirement is fixed-cohort honest coverage: the interval must be valid for every realized cohort, without assuming IID outcomes across tasks or runs.

The main theoretical contribution is a pair of sharp certification laws. If the design requires at least one completed path per task, the optimal worst-case interval width scales as Theta([M(t+1)]^{-1/2}). If omission is allowed, so some tasks may be skipped in order to spend more budget on replication elsewhere, the optimal rate becomes Theta([M(t+sqrt(M))]^{-1/2}). Here t is the extra reservation beyond the minimum needed for broad task coverage. The lower bounds apply even to adaptive hard-budget policies, so the result is not an artifact of simple non-adaptive sampling.

To make the theory operational, the paper proposes fixed random-subset designs together with disagreement-based certificates. The key idea is that when planned labels within a task agree, one observation can estimate the task contribution very well, but certifying that agreement requires extra sampling. The resulting Joint interval depends not only on the sampled mean but also on the observed disagreement count. This is more aligned with practical benchmark design than treating all observed paths as pooled Bernoulli samples.

An important conceptual distinction in the paper is between fixed-grid inference and population generalization. The primary target is the fixed benchmark grid itself, which supports honest evaluation without stochastic assumptions on task outcomes. Generalization to future tasks or runs is treated as a separate inferential objective, with an additional Hoeffding-style term discussed in the appendix.20:T532,The empirical section uses an equal-budget LiveCodeBench replay. Under the same 1100-output budget, with M = 880, t = 220, and K = 1, the paper compares the proposed Joint interval against exact pooled uniform inference. Joint produces narrower mean intervals in 15 out of 16 model panels, with a median interval-width reduction of 30.6% and a median MSE reduction of 87.0%. This supports the claim that explicitly tracking disagreement is useful in the regime where within-task agreement is common but still needs certification.

The theoretical rates are also central empirical takeaways. With mandatory task coverage, the optimal width is Theta([M(t+1)]^{-1/2}); with omission, it is Theta([M(t+sqrt(M))]^{-1/2}). The paper highlights that at zero extra reservation, the omission-enabled regime changes the attainable order from M^{-1/2} to M^{-3/4}. That is a rate-level difference, not just a constant-factor effect, and it makes task coverage a first-order design choice. The paper also studies sufficient dominance regions and controlled departures from within-task agreement to show when the finite-sample gains should be expected and when they weaken.

Because this is a theory-heavy paper, the key numbers are interval widths, MSE, and rate behavior, not benchmark score improvements in the usual model-comparison sense.21:T7c0,This is a measurement study, not a new agent method. First, it separates channel from prompt. “Channel” means tool schemas are passed through the native tools= field and then rendered and parsed by the server. “Prompt” means the tool list and JSON call format are written directly into the textual prompt. This isolates server-side protocol handling from the model’s ability to follow tool instructions.

Second, it refines the label space. Each assistant turn is categorized as valid in-schema call, hallucinated call, unparseable text, no-call prose, or non-response. The non-response category is crucial. In Ollama, some models trigger HTTP 400 request rejection before inference; the harness retries, gives up, and writes a generic error string into the trajectory. If that error is not preserved as structured metadata, downstream analysis can count it as a model non-call. The paper therefore excludes non-responses from the fidelity denominator and reports them separately.

The same weights and the same request behave differently under Ollama, llama.cpp, vLLM, and SGLang: some stacks reject tools=, some accept it but return calls as plain text, and some require explicit parser configuration before a call is recognized.
