18:T426,The paper introduces an MMORPG-style sandbox plus a curated benchmark of collaborative tasks. The simulator supports up to 1,000 concurrent agents and includes more than 380 items, 144 mob types, over 70 NPCs, and 13 high-level API tools for actions such as combat, crafting, gathering, and navigation. The high-level API design is important: it removes much of the low-level control burden so that benchmark scores reflect collaboration rather than 3D manipulation skill. The benchmark contains 100 human-annotated tasks and 100 LLM-augmented variants across combat, crafting, gathering, trading, exploration, survival, construction, and coordination. Tasks require 3 to 20 agents over 25 to 55 rounds, with asymmetric roles and no access to other agents’ internal states. The proposed Causal Collaboration Effectiveness metric builds a causal action graph over trajectories and measures the fraction of actions that contributed to the final outcome, using LLMs mainly to help infer action-level causal links rather than to issue a single subjective judgment.19:T457,The method uses a closed-testing construction. An independent proposal split nominates one candidate-specific floor c_k for each policy. A fresh certification split then runs an exact binomial test for H_k: P(S_k < c_k) ≥ α. Rejecting this null certifies that the candidate’s lower α-quantile is at least the proposed floor. Holm’s step-down procedure controls the family-wise error rate at δ across candidates. The selected policy is the certified candidate with the largest floor; the procedure abstains if no certified floor exceeds a predeclared operating threshold. The guarantee allows arbitrary within-unit dependence across candidates, such as correlated judge scores on the same prompt, while requiring i.i.d. evaluation units. Compared with matched-proposal Bonferroni, Holm gives a uniformly stronger rejection set on every dataset. Compared with exact COPS, it certifies a different statistical object, so uniform dominance is not claimed. The score map s_k is treated as a fixed measurable function; it need not be unbiased, Gaussian, asymptotically normal, or a mean off-policy estimator.1a:T8bf,论文建立了一个固定 cohort 的 repeated evaluation 框架。共有 M 个任务，每个任务预先规划 L 条重复路径，总共 N=ML 条路径；每条路径最多消耗 K 次模型响应或完整 agent episode。路径的结果用 first decisive event index T_ir 表示，若在 K 步内没有出现 decisive event，则终端标签 Y_ir=1，否则为 0。目标不是未来任务分布上的泛化均值，而是这个预先固定评测网格的经验均值 θ_C，并要求对任意可能实现的 cohort 都有 coverage 保证，也就是 fixed-cohort honest inference。

核心理论结果是两条 certification law。若设计要求每个 task 至少完成一条路径，也就是 task coverage 不能缺失，则最优最坏情形区间宽度阶为 Θ([M(t+1)]^{-1/2})；若允许省掉部分 task，把预算集中到少数任务的更多重复上，则最优阶变为 Θ([M(t+√M)]^{-1/2})。这里 t 可以理解为在覆盖每个 task 之后额外保留出来用于 replication/certification 的预算单位。作者强调 lower bound 覆盖了自适应 hard-budget policy，也就是说即便允许根据已观察结果动态调度预算，数量级上也不能突破这个界。

为了让理论可执行，论文给出固定随机子集设计和 disagreement-based certificate。直觉上，若同一 task 内计划标签高度一致，一次观测就足以把均值估得很准；但要证明这种一致性，需要额外抽样去测 disagreement。于是区间构造不只依赖 sampled mean，也显式依赖 disagreement count，形成所谓 Joint interval。这个设计比单纯把所有已观测路径当成 pooled Bernoulli 做统一推断更贴近“先覆盖任务、再花预算做认证”的实际评测流程。

论文还区分了两个目标：一个是对固定 benchmark grid 的诚实区间，另一个是对未来任务或未来运行的 population generalization。前者不需要对任务和运行结果做独立同分布假设；若要桥接到总体均值，附录里再加 Hoeffding 项。这种区分是合理的，因为很多模型发布和训练 gating 场景，真正关心的是“这次固定评测表上的分数是否足够可信”，而不是统计学意义上的外推。1b:T84b,The paper formalizes repeated evaluation on a fixed cohort. There are M tasks and L planned repetition paths per task, for N = ML total paths. Each path costs at most K model responses or full agent episodes. A path outcome is represented by a first decisive-event index T_ir, and the terminal label Y_ir indicates whether no decisive event occurred within K steps. The estimand is the empirical mean over this prespecified grid, denoted theta_C, and the requirement is fixed-cohort honest coverage: the interval must be valid for every realized cohort, without assuming IID outcomes across tasks or runs.

The main theoretical contribution is a pair of sharp certification laws. If the design requires at least one completed path per task, the optimal worst-case interval width scales as Theta([M(t+1)]^{-1/2}). If omission is allowed, so some tasks may be skipped in order to spend more budget on replication elsewhere, the optimal rate becomes Theta([M(t+sqrt(M))]^{-1/2}). Here t is the extra reservation beyond the minimum needed for broad task coverage. The lower bounds apply even to adaptive hard-budget policies, so the result is not an artifact of simple non-adaptive sampling.

To make the theory operational, the paper proposes fixed random-subset designs together with disagreement-based certificates. The key idea is that when planned labels within a task agree, one observation can estimate the task contribution very well, but certifying that agreement requires extra sampling. The resulting Joint interval depends not only on the sampled mean but also on the observed disagreement count. This is more aligned with practical benchmark design than treating all observed paths as pooled Bernoulli samples.

An important conceptual distinction in the paper is between fixed-grid inference and population generalization. The primary target is the fixed benchmark grid itself, which supports honest evaluation without stochastic assumptions on task outcomes. Generalization to future tasks or runs is treated as a separate inferential objective, with an additional Hoeffding-style term discussed in the appendix.1c:T532,The empirical section uses an equal-budget LiveCodeBench replay. Under the same 1100-output budget, with M = 880, t = 220, and K = 1, the paper compares the proposed Joint interval against exact pooled uniform inference. Joint produces narrower mean intervals in 15 out of 16 model panels, with a median interval-width reduction of 30.6% and a median MSE reduction of 87.0%. This supports the claim that explicitly tracking disagreement is useful in the regime where within-task agreement is common but still needs certification.

The theoretical rates are also central empirical takeaways. With mandatory task coverage, the optimal width is Theta([M(t+1)]^{-1/2}); with omission, it is Theta([M(t+sqrt(M))]^{-1/2}). The paper highlights that at zero extra reservation, the omission-enabled regime changes the attainable order from M^{-1/2} to M^{-3/4}. That is a rate-level difference, not just a constant-factor effect, and it makes task coverage a first-order design choice. The paper also studies sufficient dominance regions and controlled departures from within-task agreement to show when the finite-sample gains should be expected and when they weaken.

Because this is a theory-heavy paper, the key numbers are interval widths, MSE, and rate behavior, not benchmark score improvements in the usual model-comparison sense.1d:T7c0,This is a measurement study, not a new agent method. The authors fix a ReAct-style coding-agent harness on a multi-tool aggregation task and evaluate protocol fidelity rather than task success. The design has three important pieces.

First, it separates channel from prompt. “Channel” means tool schemas are passed through the native tools= field and then rendered and parsed by the server. “Prompt” means the tool list and JSON call format are written directly into the textual prompt. The paper compares three settings: native, which uses tools= only; native+hint, which still uses tools= but also injects a plain-text tool list and strict JSON format instructions; and text-tools, which removes tools= entirely and parses calls from text. This isolates server-side protocol handling from the model’s ability to follow tool instructions.

Second, it refines the label space. Each assistant turn is categorized as valid in-schema call, hallucinated call, unparseable text, no-call prose, or non-response. The non-response category is crucial. In Ollama, some models trigger HTTP 400 request rejection before inference; the harness retries, gives up, and writes a generic error string into the trajectory. If that error is not preserved as structured metadata, downstream analysis can count it as a model non-call. The paper therefore excludes non-responses from the fidelity denominator and reports them separately.

Third, it performs a multi-stack comparison. The same weights and the same request behave differently under Ollama, llama.cpp, vLLM, and SGLang: some stacks reject tools=, some accept it but return calls as plain text, and some require explicit parser configuration before a call is recognized. The point is not to rank stacks, but to show that the measured outcome is stack-dependent. The constrained-decoding baseline adds another useful negative result: forcing valid call syntax can simply move the failure mode from malformed calls to endless looping.1e:T668,The experiments cover small local models including Qwen2.5-Coder 0.5B/1.5B/3B, Llama-3.2, Phi-3, and Gemma-3. Results are reported over 8 seeds using both turn-pooled rates and per-seed means, with seed-level bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. Four findings stand out. First, under Ollama’s default native function-calling path, Qwen2.5-Coder models accept tools= but return calls as text, Llama-3.2 returns native tool_calls, and Phi-3 and Gemma-3 are rejected with HTTP 400 before inference. Without structured error logging, those rejected models can be falsely reported at 0% fidelity. Second, for models whose requests are accepted, native+hint often recovers much of the fidelity, which suggests that some failures come from protocol rendering rather than inability to use tools. But Llama-3.2 degrades under a uniform text-only protocol, so the best setup is model-dependent. Third, the same weights behave differently across four serving stacks: models rejected by Ollama can run on llama.cpp, vLLM rejects similar requests by default, and SGLang accepts them but returns text calls. That directly shows the benchmark outcome is partly a serving-stack property. Fourth, turn-pooled and per-seed metrics can differ by roughly 55 points because a single looping episode can dominate the pooled denominator. This makes aggregation choice a major source of instability.

The paper also includes a useful negative result: at this measurement precision, the authors do not support broader claims about scale, model family, or reasoning ability and explicitly avoid making them. That restraint is one of the stronger parts of the study.1f:T418,For a skim, start with the introduction and the method section explaining the OpenAI-compatible tool-calling pipeline. Then read the comparison among native, native+hint, and text-tools, plus the four-stack behavior table or figure. Those sections make the confound concrete. The tested system first runs natively: it processes dialogue history, updates its memory store, retrieves context, and generates an answer. EvalMem then diagnoses the completed run using benchmark annotations. The Encoding Examiner checks whether the target fact is present in the memory store. The Retrieval Examiner checks whether the system’s native retriever returns usable evidence. The Generation Examiner tests whether the model can answer when given oracle evidence. First, it measures shared errors directly. Across multiple preference datasets, the authors collect decisions from both open-weight and frontier judges, compute pairwise error correlations, and summarize the amount of independent information using effective sample size. They use the design-effect approximation: once average correlation is nonzero, a bank of n judges behaves like a much smaller number of independent judges in variance terms. The paper is careful to note that this does not mean 10 judges can literally be replaced by 3.5 judges; it means the consensus contains only that much independent statistical information.

Second, it studies how dependence changes conclusions. The paper compares a pooled-vote significance test, which treats each judge vote as an independent observation, against an item-level test that accounts for correlated votes on the same examples. This isolates a common evaluation mistake: inflating evidence by counting redundant votes as if they were independent samples.

Third, it distinguishes different structures of shared failure. The paper defines global co-failure, where many judges fail on the same items, and vulnerable-subgroup failure, where errors are concentrated within a subset of judges. The key point is that average correlation alone is not enough to distinguish these cases, yet they affect majority voting differently and call for different filtering rules. To address this, the authors propose CorrFilter, which discounts agreement among judges with correlated errors, and a bias-cluster filter, which ranks examples by support from judges outside the vulnerable subgroup. A small trusted set is then used to choose among majority voting and these specialized filters.24:T731,The main quantitative result is straightforward and important. In the primary ten-judge bank, the average pairwise error correlation is 0.21. Under the design-effect approximation, this gives a variance inflation factor of about 2.85 and a variance-effective sample size of only n_eff ≈ 3.5. So ten judges do not provide ten units of independent evidence.

The paper repeats this analysis on two additional datasets and 73 alternative judge-bank compositions spanning prompt styles, model families, providers, and random seeds. The dependence is therefore not a one-off artifact of a single ensemble.

The frontier-judge result is even more striking. Three high-accuracy judges from different providers each reach 91% to 93% accuracy, yet their average error correlation is 0.56, and they make the same mistake together at 7.7 times the rate expected under independence. This directly challenges the naive intuition that stronger judges automatically make consensus more trustworthy. The paper also finds that judges from different providers are about as correlated as judges from the same provider, suggesting that shared blind spots are not just a same-family effect.

At the evaluation level, ignoring dependence changes significance conclusions in up to 28% of system comparisons: a pooled-vote test declares one system significantly better, while an item-level test that respects correlated votes does not. For filtering, the paper shows that no single fixed rule works best across both global co-failure and vulnerable-subgroup failure. A selector based on a small trusted set is tested on eight external factuality datasets, and in those transfers it always chooses plain majority voting.
