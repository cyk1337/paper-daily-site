First, it separates local perception from global spatial integration, which many existing video benchmarks do not. That matters for embodied pretraining, world modeling, and native multimodal training. Second, it shows that failure modes differ by model family: strong proprietary models improve sharply when the task is reduced to local perception, suggesting that their main weakness is cross-frame spatial integration; many open models do not improve reliably even then, indicating deficits in both perception and reasoning. Third, the paper goes beyond evaluation by introducing GST-Train and showing that targeted training data can substantially close the gap. Qwen3-VL-8B improves from 25.89 to 53.52 after fine-tuning, surpassing all zero-shot proprietary models in their setup. That is a useful signal for pretraining researchers: global spatial competence is not something to assume will emerge automatically from scale alone; data construction and task pressure matter.6:T5fc,The contribution is centered on benchmark and data design rather than a new VLM architecture. The pipeline is built on OmniGibson with BEHAVIOR-1K scenes. For each of 50 indoor environments, the system generates diverse egocentric camera trajectories and additional query viewpoints outside the trajectory. It records 3D coordinates of objects and viewpoints, projects them onto top-down maps, and automatically creates QA pairs. A key filtering rule removes any sample where the target object is visible from the query viewpoint, ensuring that object-localization questions cannot be solved from a single frame.

The task taxonomy is organized around three embodied questions: Where am I? Where is the target? What does the scene look like? These are instantiated as 12 subtasks covering self-localization, object localization, and scene-structure understanding. The top-down image is not cosmetic; it acts as an explicit probe of whether the model can map long-horizon egocentric evidence into a global scene representation.

A particularly strong part of the design is the controlled local variant. By making the target visible from the query viewpoint, the authors convert the task into one that can be handled largely by local perception. This creates a clean diagnostic split between failures caused by weak single-image understanding and failures caused by missing cross-view spatial integration. That makes GST-Bench more than a leaderboard asset; it becomes a tool for identifying what kind of spatial competence is absent.7:T584,The main evaluation covers 22 VLMs, including open-source, proprietary, and embodied-oriented models. The strongest zero-shot model, Gemini-3-Pro, reaches 42.68, far below the human score of 79.08. Open models are substantially weaker; the best reported open model, InternVL3.5-38B, stays around the low-30 range, and many models are close to random guessing. This gap is large enough that it is hard to dismiss as benchmark noise.

The local-variant analysis is one of the most informative results. Proprietary models can gain up to 39 points when the task is reduced to local perception, which strongly suggests that they already have decent local visual understanding but fail to maintain and use a global spatial state across frames. Open models, in contrast, do not improve consistently even in the local setting, pointing to a compound bottleneck involving both perception and reasoning.

The training-data experiment is equally important. Using the same generation pipeline, the authors build GST-Train from BEHAVIOR-1K, ArtVIP, HyperSim, and internal simulation scenes. Fine-tuning Qwen3-VL-8B on GST-Train raises its GST-Bench score from 25.89 to 53.52, a gain of 27.63 points, and pushes it above all proprietary models under zero-shot evaluation. That result suggests the benchmark is learnable with targeted supervision and that synthetic spatial data can materially improve global spatial reasoning.8:T5bb,The method is straightforward. The authors build MissingBench-Verified, a 118-image benchmark composed of real photos and carefully selected internet images. Each image is paired with a target element to remove. Most edits are produced with the image editing model Nano Banana 2, and the rest are manually edited with an eraser tool. All edited images are then human-verified to filter out unnatural or failed edits. The evaluation prompt is a three-way multiple-choice question asking whether the queried item is clearly visible, visible but hard to see, or not visible, with structured JSON output including reasoning and final answer.

There are four main evaluation settings. Direct query asks the model to judge visibility on its own. Pre-answer tool injection inserts a simulated perfect detector result before the model answers; the detector always reports the queried item as undetected with low confidence. This tests whether external evidence can steer the model. Post-answer tool injection provides the same evidence after the model has already answered, probing confirmation bias. Thinking with images gives the model access to basic image-processing tools such as cropping, brightness and contrast adjustment, and binarization, to test whether active inspection helps. The paper also varies reasoning effort for GPT-5.2 and includes OWLv2 and LLM-Det as detector-style references, though those comparisons are more sanity checks than direct task matches.9:T6ff,The main result is consistent across models: ten leading VLMs perform poorly on images where essential parts were removed. No model exceeds 75% accuracy on the edited set, and four out of ten fall below 50%. The lowest reported accuracy is 44.1% for Claude Sonnet 4.6. Some models show a strong existence bias. Qwen 3.5 Flash, for example, chooses the 'clearly visible' option in 51.7% of cases, which is even higher than its correct 'not visible' rate of 47.5%.

Tool injection helps only partially. In the pre-answer setting, Kimi K2.5 and Qwen 3.5 Plus and Flash improve by about 14.4% to 16.9%, but overall performance remains modest. The post-answer setting is more revealing because it tests whether models revise an already formed belief. Qwen 3.5 Plus shows the largest gain at 24.5%, but most models improve only slightly, and some even dip a bit, though the authors caution against over-interpreting small changes given the dataset size. Even with a simulated perfect detector, most models still remain below 75% not-visible accuracy; Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview is the only one reported above that threshold, reaching 82.2% in the post-answer setting.

On the original unedited images, models generally identify the queried object or part correctly, which matters because it shows the task itself is not inherently ambiguous. The failure is concentrated in the missing-part condition. The think-with-images setting, tested on only a few models, does not appear to reliably solve the problem. NegAwareQwen also performs poorly, suggesting that narrow finetuning on easier negative cases does not transfer well to this harder visual-negation setup. - Normalized schema: consecutive screenshots (s_t, s_{t+1}), logged action a_t (click/type/select), optional value v_t (typed text/selected option), and metadata.
- T1 Temporal ordering: given K screenshots (K=3/4/5) from the same trajectory in shuffled order, predict the chronological order.

1) Culture-first dataset construction: prompts are authored from local legal/religious/social norms across six Asia-Pacific locales, covering eight languages (including local English variants). This is intentionally not a translation of existing English safety sets.
2) Native multimodality: prompts pair text with benign images to elicit non-adversarial but contextually unsafe or culturally inappropriate responses. The design goal is to expose failures caused by applying globally averaged (often Western) safety priors to localized contexts.
3) Dual evaluation pipeline: culturally matched human annotations serve as ground truth; an automated, locale-conditioned LLM-as-a-Judge provides scalable scoring.
4) Multi-axis judge: the judge is designed to disentangle (i) safety compliance vs multimodal hallucination vs over-refusal, and (ii) universal safety vs locale-specific cultural appropriateness.
5) Root-cause taxonomy: the paper proposes a bottom-up categorization of why responses are culturally inappropriate, enabling more diagnostic analysis than a single aggregate score.
