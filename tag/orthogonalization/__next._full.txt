18:T8ce,The algorithmic proposal is DP-Muon, but the real contribution is the analysis. Muon can be viewed as applying approximate orthogonalization to momentum for matrix-valued parameters. More abstractly, it fits into a linear minimization oracle (LMO) framework. This unifies Muon with Scion, SignSGD, and normalized SGD as different geometries of direction selection over norm balls.

The key theoretical observation concerns the expected clipped gradient, μ(g)=E[clip_c(g+ξ)]. The paper shows that it can be decomposed into a positive scalar multiple of the true gradient g plus a residual term r(g) that changes direction. Since an LMO is invariant to positive rescaling, LMO(λg)=LMO(g) for λ>0, the dominant effect of clipping is invisible to the update rule. Only the directional residual enters the optimization error, and that residual is controlled by the centered gradient variance. This is the central idea of the paper. It avoids the usual strong assumption of bounded raw per-example gradients and does not require the clipping threshold to grow with the horizon.

Building on that decomposition, the authors derive a convergence guarantee for private momentum-LMO methods on smooth nonconvex objectives in any finite-dimensional norm. The bound separates optimization error, momentum tracking error, sampling noise, privacy noise, and a clipping floor. The floor scales as ρ1 min{σ, σ^2/c}, where ρ1 is the norm-conversion constant into the relevant dual norm. The interpretation is that passing clipped gradients through an LMO costs at most a geometric conversion factor relative to clipped SGD, rather than introducing a qualitatively new instability. At fixed noise scale, the rate is about O~(K^-1/4); with noise calibrated to an (ε, δ) privacy budget, the dependence matches the familiar DP scaling.

Algorithmically, DP-Muon still follows the standard central example-level DP pipeline: per-example clipping, Gaussian noise addition, averaging, then momentum and Muon orthogonalization. Privacy comes entirely from the first stage; the optimizer-specific part is post-processing. The novelty is therefore not a new privacy mechanism, but an explanation of why Muon-style post-processing remains analyzable under active clipping.19:T5e6,The empirical study has two parts: validation of the theory’s implications and optimizer comparisons under DP. The experiments cover CNNs and a 30M-parameter GPT language model, with sweeps over batch size, learning rate, and momentum, comparing DP-SGD, DP-Adam, DP-Muon, and Scion. Two findings stand out. First, under DP the optimal batch size shifts upward by one to two orders of magnitude relative to non-private training. That alone is an important recipe change. Second, optimizer ranking becomes batch-size dependent; unlike the non-private setting, where Muon tends to dominate more consistently, there is no single winner across the DP regime.

On CIFAR-10, DP-Muon achieves the lowest mean test loss at all but one batch size, although at intermediate batch sizes the gaps are often within one standard deviation. So the advantage is real but not overwhelming. On language modeling, DP-Muon is best at small batch sizes, DP-Adam is strongest near the optimal batch size, and Scion performs best at large batch sizes. This is consistent with the theory: once clipping and privacy noise interact, geometry, momentum dynamics, and batch-dependent noise scale jointly determine which update rule is preferable.

If the full paper provides more detail, the most useful pieces for LLM researchers are the GPT setup, the privacy budget, the token budget, and the computational overhead of Muon’s orthogonalization. The language-model batch-size sweeps are much more informative than the vision results.1a:T541,For a skim, start with Section 3. The goal is not to absorb every proof, but to understand two claims clearly: clipping mainly induces a positive rescaling along the true gradient, and an LMO is blind to that rescaling, so only the directional residual matters. Once those two points are clear, the rest of the theory becomes much easier to place. Then read Theorem 1 and the surrounding discussion to see how the error terms are separated, especially the clipping floor and the role of ρ1.

For experiments, prioritize the language-model results over the vision section. Look first at the batch-size sweeps, then at the optimizer comparisons near tuned hyperparameters. A useful skim should take around 25 minutes. For a deeper read, include the appendix discussion against concurrent work, especially the distinction between centered-variance assumptions and raw-gradient moment assumptions, and why this paper does not need a clipping threshold that grows with training horizon.

Three follow-up questions are worth keeping in mind. Can the LMO perspective extend to hybrid AdamW/Muon recipes used in large-model training? Is the norm-conversion constant too loose in high-dimensional matrix settings? And since the language-model experiment is only a 30M GPT, how far these conclusions extrapolate to billion-scale pretraining remains open.1b:T76c,[must_read: detailed technical analysis]
1) Main change (Muon2): apply Adam-style adaptive second-moment, element-wise scaling to the momentum matrix before the NS-based orthogonalization. Concretely: form momentum M_t, maintain a second-moment accumulator V_t (EMA of squared signals), compute a preconditioned momentum \tilde{M}_t = M_t / (\sqrt{V_t}+\epsilon) element-wise, then run K NS iterations on \tilde{M}_t to obtain an approximate polar/orthogonal factor Q_t, and update parameters with an additional \sqrt{m/n} learning-rate scaling.
2) Key insight: the practical difficulty in Muon’s polar approximation is not the need for exact orthogonality. The original Muon already tolerates fairly inexact orthogonalization (e.g., mapping singular values only roughly into [1-ε,1+ε] with ε up to ~0.3). The real issue is that the input momentum matrix can be ill-conditioned with an unfavorable singular-value spectrum, slowing NS convergence. Muon2’s second-moment preconditioning empirically improves the spectrum/conditioning, which:
- speeds up NS convergence to a practically sufficient orthogonalization;
- improves directional alignment of the finite-iteration update with the “true” orthogonalized direction.
3) Metric shift: the paper argues that orthogonality error metrics (e.g., \|Q^TQ-I\|) alone do not capture what matters for optimization; directional alignment and finite-iteration utility are more relevant.
