The authors introduce JUPE, a VQA-style benchmark where each item fixes the image, question, candidate answer, and correctness label, while varying only the explanation condition. This isolates the causal effect of explanation text on judgment. Judges output one of four ordinal labels—definitely incorrect, probably incorrect, probably correct, definitely correct—which are also collapsed into reject versus accept for binary analyses. To reduce prompt artifacts, the option order is randomized, the model answers with A–D choices, and the prompt uses an image-first layout. The benchmark spans three visually grounded domains: medical VQA, chart reasoning, and hallucination-prone visual questions, drawing from VQA-RAD, PMC-VQA, ChartQA, ChartQAPro, HaloQuest, and PRE-HAL. The main set contains about 180 queries, plus a 70-item induced-wrong easy set built from questions that profiling models usually solve correctly and then paired with intentionally wrong answers. This is a strong design choice because it tests whether explanations can flip judgments even on items that are otherwise easy. Explanation conditions include jointly generated chain-of-thought rationales, plain post-hoc justifications, and persuasion-oriented arguments built using 41 persuasion techniques. A key analytical move is the use of signal detection theory. Rather than relying on accuracy alone, the paper separates shifts in decision criterion from changes in discriminability. That matters because a judge can become more accepting of both correct and incorrect answers, leaving overall accuracy nearly unchanged while substantially weakening wrong-answer rejection.a:T5d0,The evaluation covers 14 MLLM judges from 5 model families. The main finding is consistent: ordinary plausible explanations are enough to materially reduce rejection of incorrect answers. On average, plain justifications reduce the rejection rate of wrong answers by 12.7 percentage points, chain-of-thought explanations by 13.8 points, and explicitly persuasive explanations by 12.5 points. The similarity across these conditions is important because it shows the problem does not require specialized persuasion attacks; normal-looking explanations already trigger it. A stronger result is that among wrong answers judged definitely incorrect without explanations, 36% to 39% become accepted once a plausible explanation is added. The signal-detection analysis indicates that the dominant effect is a shift in decision criterion toward acceptance, not a clear mean improvement in discriminability. In other words, the judges are not becoming better at reading the image; they are becoming more willing to trust the answer. The paper also checks robustness across domains, an out-of-distribution grounding set, different explanation generators, image degradation settings, and rating protocols. Common mitigations such as caution prompting and solve-first verification show limited or inconsistent gains.
