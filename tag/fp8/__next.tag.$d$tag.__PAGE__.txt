For a format with m mantissa bits, the spacing at coordinate wi is ULP_m(wi)=2^{floor(log2|wi|)-m}. Under round-to-nearest, a gradient step leaves wi unchanged whenever |Δwi| < 1/2 ULP_m(wi). This gives a per-coordinate freeze condition and leads to a measurable frozen fraction f, defined as the proportion of coordinates that remain bitwise unchanged after a step. The freeze time τ* is the first step where f reaches 1/2.

The key theoretical move is to rewrite the condition in dimensionless form as ρ_i < 2^{-φ_i}, where φ_i is the fractional part of log2|wi| and indicates where the weight lies within its binade. If φ_i is approximately equidistributed, the half-ULP boundary occupies a one-bit-wide band, whose median yields a parameter-free threshold ρ*=1/sqrt(2). This explains why the onset is an O(1) crossover rather than a sharp size-dependent phase transition.

The predictor is deliberately simple. Run one fp64 trajectory {w_t, g_t}. For any target mantissa length m, compute the predicted frozen fraction at each step as \hat f_t(m)=<1[η|g_{t,i}| < 1/2 ULP_m(w_{t,i})]>. The predicted τ* is where \hat f_t crosses 1/2. Since the predictor uses no low-precision data, successful prediction would imply that the phenomenon is governed primarily by arithmetic discretization rather than by altered low-precision dynamics.

The experimental design separates arithmetic effects cleanly. Real bf16 hardware establishes that the freeze is physically present. A mantissa-truncation emulator performs all arithmetic in fp64 but rounds weights to an m-bit grid after each step, allowing controlled sweeps over mantissa precision without confounding exponent range or denormals. The paper also emphasizes an important practical nuance: in multilayer networks, a single pooled L2-based ρ can become misleading because layers live at different scales and tiny bias vectors may never freeze.
