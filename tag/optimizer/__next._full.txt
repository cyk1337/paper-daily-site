18:T459,1. **Spectral Probing Analysis**: At checkpoints along real training trajectories, the momentum buffer is decomposed into singular directions, and the loss-optimal step size along each direction is estimated on held-out data. The spectral profile is found to be highly anisotropic and stable: a 'volatile head' at the 'Edge-of-Stability' supports only small step sizes, while a 'tolerant bulk' permits much larger steps.
2. **Unified Perspective**: SGD allocates step size proportional to singular values (opposite to landscape suggestions); Adam's coordinate-wise rescaling mitigates but doesn't solve the mismatch; Muon's whitening to a uniform scale reallocates step mass from the head to the bulk.
3. **SAMuon Algorithm**: To exploit the bulk's capacity for larger steps, Spectral-Aware Muon (SAMuon) is proposed. It imposes a static, head-anchored prior on the whitened update. The full SAMuon fits a log-rank-linear shape via randomized SVD; the simplified SAMuon-lite uses power iteration to pin the head and uniformly amplifies the bulk. Both add no persistent state and negligible computational overhead.19:T863,The method has two main components. The first derives Muon’s update direction from a constrained response model. The weight update is posed as a response of the parameter matrix to an applied force, with an output-side safety budget limiting how much the update may perturb the model’s outputs. Among all admissible responses, the preferred one is the maximally dissipative direction. Under the paper’s assumptions, this leads to the orthogonal polar factor of the momentum matrix, which is exactly the semi-orthogonalized direction used by Muon. This is a matrix-level geometric argument, not a coordinate-wise adaptive scaling argument. That is the key distinction from AdamW: AdamW relies on per-parameter second-moment preconditioning, while Muon chooses an update shape that is better behaved in spectral terms and less dominated by a few singular directions.

The second component derives a two-timescale momentum from stress relaxation. Standard momentum assumes a single exponential decay, which corresponds to a single relaxation mode. The paper argues that this is too crude for real training dynamics. Borrowing from Maxwell-type viscoelastic models, it decomposes internal stress into a fast and a slow component, each tracked by its own EMA coefficient, and combines them into the momentum that is later semi-orthogonalized. This yields the Bi-Maxwell optimizer. Importantly, the rest of Muon is kept unchanged: the orthogonalization step stays, and the benchmark recipe is otherwise held fixed. That makes the causal claim cleaner, because the observed gains can be attributed mainly to the memory structure rather than to a bundle of recipe changes.

A central conceptual point is that the optimal memory length should not be constant over training. Early on, gradient directions change quickly, so long memory mixes in stale directions and hurts responsiveness. Later, directions evolve more slowly, so longer memory improves denoising and accumulates consistent signal. Bi-Maxwell does not explicitly schedule beta over time; instead, it uses two fixed timescales to approximate a training-dependent effective memory length.1a:T773,The headline result is on the public modded-nanogpt optimizer speedrun benchmark. Replacing Muon’s single-timescale momentum with the Bi-Maxwell memory reaches the target loss at step 2635, improving on the standing public record of 2690 steps at the time. The paper also reports a 40-step improvement over a bare tuned-Muon baseline without extra add-ons such as SOAP. The absolute gain is modest, but on a benchmark that has already been heavily tuned by the community, a further improvement from changing only the momentum structure is meaningful.

The second important result is a read-only measurement study over 8 independent training trajectories, aimed at estimating the optimal momentum memory length during training. The measured trend matches the theory: the preferred memory is shorter early in training and becomes longer later. The value of this experiment is less about the exact numbers and more about turning a usually opaque hyperparameter into something that can be observed and discussed over the course of optimization.

The paper also makes a point about evaluation discipline. The comparison is not against a weak in-house baseline, but against a public standing record on a benchmark that has been tuned adversarially over time. Since the rest of Muon is held fixed and only the memory mechanism is changed, the attribution is tighter than in many optimizer papers where gains can be confounded by recipe drift.

At least from the provided full text, the direct empirical validation is still centered on the public small-scale speedrun setup. The paper cites prior evidence that Muon itself has scaled to billion-parameter and trillion-token settings, but the new Bi-Maxwell variant is not yet shown in a full large-scale pretraining run here. So the empirical status is best read as theory-driven validation on a strong public benchmark, not yet a finalized frontier-scale recipe.1b:T68d,Start with the Introduction to understand the framing: the paper is not merely rebranding momentum, but assigning distinct physical meanings to update direction and memory. Then focus on Section 3, especially the part around stress relaxation and memory length. That is where the paper’s real novelty sits. While reading, keep two questions in mind. First, what exactly is constrained by the output-side safety budget, and why does that lead to the polar-factor or semi-orthogonalized direction? Second, why is a single EMA equivalent to an overly restrictive single-mode relaxation assumption, and why is a two-timescale model the minimal useful extension?

In the experiments, prioritize the main benchmark result and the plots measuring optimal memory length over training. The former answers whether the method helps; the latter answers whether the theory is reflected in data. If time is limited, it is fine to postpone judging every physical analogy literally and instead inspect which assumptions are essential for the derivation and which are mainly there to obtain a tractable form. The appendix discussion on assumption boundaries and relaxations is worth reading because it determines how far this framework might generalize beyond the exact setting studied here.

Two follow-up questions are especially worth carrying into a deeper read. One is whether Bi-Maxwell should be extended to richer relaxation spectra, possibly continuous or layer-specific. The other is whether the optimal memory length could be estimated online and used to adapt beta directly, instead of approximating it with two fixed EMAs. The paper opens both directions but does not close them.1c:T51a,The baseline Sinkhorn matrix optimizer repeatedly normalizes the rows and columns of a matrix gradient and applies a scalar update scale. It is attractive because it removes moment state for dense two-dimensional weights. SAGE-like hybrids typically keep lightweight state for vocabulary tensors and use Sinkhorn for dense matrices. The paper shows that this dense-model role assignment breaks for MoE experts. For expert e, the gradient G_{e,t} is drawn from a changing conditional distribution p(x | e is selected by top-k routing), not from the full minibatch distribution. Since Sinkhorn normalization is nonlinear, Sinkhorn(E[G_t]) and E[Sinkhorn(G_t)] can behave differently. MESH addresses this by maintaining a hidden first-moment signal H_t for expert gradients and applying Sinkhorn to H_t rather than to G_t. Non-expert matrices and vocabulary tensors keep the SAGE/Sinkhorn hybrid treatment. The expert momentum is maintained through the gradient-buffer lifecycle instead of being stored as an explicit optimizer-state tensor. MESH-B adds a coarse block/neuron inverse-RMS multiplier, giving a limited form of adaptive scaling. The paper’s own interpretation is conservative: block-level adaptivity can help, but temporal smoothing before Sinkhorn normalization is the main causal ingredient.1d:T43b,Proposes MALT (Muon Augmented by Lightweight Two-sided preconditioning) and its adaptive variant MALTER: 1) Derives a preconditioned Muon formulation based on local quadratic approximation of the loss, using a positive-definite curvature surrogate to construct a transformed space with isotropic quadratic terms, then orthogonalizes preconditioned momentum and maps the direction back to the original parameter space to address both curvature and gradient anisotropy simultaneously; 2) Designs lightweight two-sided diagonal preconditioners: maintains exponential moving averages (EMA) of row-wise and column-wise squared gradient norms to construct left and right diagonal preconditioning matrices, preserving the matrix structure of parameters with minimal extra overhead; 3) Uses norm grafting to stabilize update scale; 4) MALTER further introduces a scalar step size adapted via Adam-style norm-based noise adjustment in the preconditioned space to improve robustness to stochastic gradient noise. Convergence guarantees for stochastic non-convex optimization are also provided.1e:T447,The central notion is gauge equivariance: an optimizer is equivariant if it behaves consistently under orthogonal rotations of the latent factor basis. A structure theorem shows that a memoryless update is equivariant iff it takes the form Δ=H(GG^T)G, a left preconditioner determined by the Gram matrix. Under this classification, GD, Polyak momentum, Adam with a shared scalar second moment, Muon (including its Newton–Schulz iterates), and Shampoo's Kronecker preconditioner with damping are equivariant; Adam, RMSProp, signSGD, Lion, and Adafactor break equivariance because they apply entrywise nonlinearities at zero state. An exact first-step defect is derived for zero-initialized Adam. A transfer theorem shows that dynamics under a shared scalar preconditioner equal gradient flow under a rescaled time, so existing low-rank/minimum-nuclear-norm results for gradient flow carry over. Equivariance is necessary but not sufficient: low-rank convergence also depends on the spectral schedule over singular values attracting dynamics to the low-rank manifold within the training budget.1f:T56c,The setup is intentionally controlled. Using the official Mamba implementation and the public Muon reference code, the authors compare a pure AdamW baseline against three hybrid assignments: Muon on the input projection group only, Muon on the output projection group only, and Muon on both groups, with all remaining parameters still trained by AdamW. This turns the question into a clean assignment study rather than a noisy optimizer bake-off.

Muon works by applying a fixed-step Newton-Schulz orthogonalization to matrix-valued momentum updates, approximating steepest descent under spectral norm geometry. AdamW, in contrast, is coordinate-wise adaptive. The paper goes beyond loss curves and tracks spectral diagnostics of the affected matrices: condition number, effective rank, and spectral norm. The goal is to test whether any optimization gain is accompanied by systematic spectral changes.

The main technical takeaway is that Muon is not uniformly helpful inside Mamba-2. Applying it only to the output projection group works better than applying it only to the input projection group, and better than applying it to both. The authors also test a natural explanation and reject it: although Muon does reduce the condition number of the matrices it touches, that alone does not explain the validation-loss differences. Functional role matters, not just improved conditioning.20:T602,The experiments use Mamba-2 130M on OpenWebText and FineWeb-Edu, with token budgets of 1B, 2.6B, and 50B. This is a sensible design because it checks both early token efficiency and behavior well beyond the usual compute-optimal regime, while also testing whether the effect survives across corpora.

The most consistent result is that Muon on the output projection group gives the best outcome among the tested assignments. The gain is primarily in token efficiency: it reaches a given validation loss faster than pure AdamW, rather than dramatically changing the eventual floor. Muon on the input projection is weaker, and applying Muon to both input and output projections does not improve further. That pattern suggests a localized architectural fit rather than a generic optimizer advantage.

The long-budget runs matter. The advantage persists even when training continues far past the nominal compute-optimal point, which makes the result more credible than a short-horizon comparison. On the diagnostic side, Muon systematically lowers the condition number of the matrices it updates and changes their spectral statistics, but those changes do not map cleanly to validation performance. That is an important negative result: cleaner spectra are not sufficient to explain better language-model training.
