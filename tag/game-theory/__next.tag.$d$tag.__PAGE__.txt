a:T461,VERDICT formulates verification as a coupled scoring problem among frozen, modality-specialized judges.
- Three frozen judges score each candidate step from different perspectives: Visual grounding, Logical validity, and Contextual relevance.
- Disagreement-aware consensus: instead of aggregating independently, they introduce coupling and interpret the interaction as a coordination game. The consensus-adjusted scores correspond to a unique Nash equilibrium with a claimed closed-form solution, so each judge’s adjusted belief accounts for others.
- Dual acceptance rule: a mean-confidence threshold ensures sufficient collective endorsement, while a dispersion (agreement) constraint rejects unstable steps with unresolved conflict. Among accepted candidates, they rank by consensus confidence to pick the most stable continuation.
- Key distinction vs mean/variance: averaging is symmetric and loses “who disagrees with whom”; variance ignores belief strength. Coupling introduces structured, asymmetric influence (e.g., a stubborn visual judge can dominate disagreement in a way fixed weights cannot capture).b:T5f8,There are three technical layers. First, the paper defines a sequential game with a prompt distribution, a reference policy π_ref, and a reward function r. The agent chooses a policy π to maximize utility, while a monitor performs sequential hypothesis testing on observed outputs to decide whether they came from π_ref or from a modified policy. If the monitor detects deviation, the agent is stopped. Second, the equilibrium analysis shows that the agent’s Nash equilibrium strategy coincides with the optimizer of the standard KL-regularized RL objective, maximizing expected reward minus β times KL(π || π_ref). The key point is not to invent a new objective, but to derive the old one from a statistical detection game. The KL term is interpreted as the cost of remaining hard to distinguish from the reference policy. The equilibrium also identifies an optimal coefficient β*, determined by the reward function, prompt distribution, and reference policy, rather than by manual tuning. Using the closed-form optimizer of KL-regularized RL, π*(y|x) proportional to π_ref(y|x) exp(r(x,y)/β), the equilibrium condition reduces to a scalar condition on β. Third, the paper proposes a stochastic bisection algorithm that estimates β* to ε precision by solving O(log(1/ε)) RL subproblems. The analysis draws on fractional programming and sequential hypothesis testing. Compared with target-KL heuristics, the logic is reversed: define the optimal detectability boundary first, then solve for the corresponding β.c:T5da,The paper introduces a stylized optimization model. Outputs live in a D-dimensional space. The first dimension is the detector-facing attribute, while quality depends on all dimensions. A user chooses two controls: how much work to delegate to the LLM, modeled as a convex combination of human-only output and fully LLM-generated output, and how much post-processing to apply to reduce the detected attribute.

The user maximizes utility, which combines output quality, detector penalty if flagged, production cost, and post-processing cost. The detector itself is modeled as a threshold-based classifier on the first dimension. With this setup, the paper derives conditions under which detector deployment changes optimal user behavior in unintuitive ways.

The contribution is not algorithmic complexity but analytical clarity. The model cleanly separates three quantities: the detected attribute, LLM usage, and output quality. The paper shows that detector deployment has a predictable directional effect on the first quantity, but not on the latter two. In other words, a detector can reduce the feature it sees while still increasing LLM reliance for some users or reducing final quality.

The theory is also connected to the empirically observed rise-then-fall pattern in LLM-associated words and stylistic markers. The interpretation is straightforward: LLM access initially increases the detector-facing attribute, and subsequent detection pressure or social avoidance pushes it back down.d:T4c3,The empirical component mainly serves two purposes: illustrating the theoretical mechanisms and checking whether the predicted qualitative pattern appears in real data. On the theory side, the paper uses figures and parameterized examples to show three effects: detector introduction can increase optimal LLM usage for some users, can reduce output quality even when the targeted attribute is quality-reducing, and can generate a rise-then-fall trajectory for the detected attribute.

These correspond to Theorems 1-4. Theorems 1 and 2 provide sufficient and necessary conditions for increased LLM usage; Theorem 3 gives sufficient conditions for reduced output quality; Theorem 4 shows that the detected attribute weakly increases when users gain LLM access and weakly decreases after detector deployment.

On the empirical side, the paper goes beyond earlier observations based on a few word lists and examines whether rise-then-fall patterns have become more prevalent in the LLM era relative to earlier time windows over the last decade. The answer is yes. The lower-level RM update depends on the data distribution induced by the current policy. By analytically unrolling this update, they decompose the true policy gradient into the usual policy-gradient term and an additional parameter-steering term. The latter measures how current policy samples change future reward-model parameters.

They then propose Foresighted Policy Optimization, or FPO. Rather than replacing the whole RLHF pipeline, FPO adds a targeted penalty during policy optimization to account for the missing steering effect. The analysis is first developed in a pointwise setting to expose the geometry, then extended to pairwise preference comparisons. The exact bilevel term would normally require inverse-Hessian computations.
