18:T61a,The method has three components. First, PRP, a Prefix Reuse Predictor, estimates whether a prefix will be requested again within a future horizon. It uses lightweight online features rather than offline profiling. The features include structural signals such as prefix length, modality, radix-tree position, and session metadata, as well as temporal signals such as recency, frequency, inter-arrival patterns, and time-of-day effects. Second, CARS, a Cost-Aware Retention Score, converts reuse probability into a keep/evict decision using a closed-form cost model. This is the key step. A miss cost includes recomputation on a prefill node plus transfer to a decode node. An admission cost includes the initial transfer into the shared KV pool and the opportunity cost of occupying pool memory. So the decision is not merely whether reuse is likely, but whether expected reuse value exceeds retention cost under the current system configuration. Third, ATC, an Adaptive Threshold Controller, adjusts the retention threshold as pool pressure, bandwidth conditions, and workload mix change.

The paper’s main strength is the alignment between prediction and systems objective. LRU/LFU can only encode recency or frequency; they cannot express that misses on long multimodal prefixes are much more expensive, while admitting short low-value prefixes may be wasteful. The theoretical section also formalizes when the optimal admission threshold must vary with prefix length, showing that a uniform threshold is structurally suboptimal even in an idealized setting.19:T578,The evaluation covers both language and multimodal workloads, with baselines including No-Cache, LRU-Pool, and Mooncake-LRU. The headline results are on end-to-end TTFT and inter-node transfer volume. KVLearn reduces TTFT by up to 56% versus No-Cache, 38% versus LRU-Pool, and 33% versus Mooncake-LRU. It also cuts inter-node transfer volume by up to 53% relative to LRU-Pool. On MM-Session-style multimodal workloads, it reaches near-Oracle throughput under realistic pool sizes. This suggests the gains come not just from more hits, but from avoiding low-value admissions and expensive misses.

The problem scale is also important. For a 13B model in bfloat16, a high-resolution visual prefix can consume roughly 128–512 MB of KV memory, and recomputing such a prefix on an A100 can cost around 100–400 ms. In that regime, a single wrong retention decision is expensive enough that simple recency-based policies become fragile.

The most informative ablations are likely the following. Removing the cost-aware scoring while keeping the reuse predictor should hurt noticeably, showing that reuse likelihood and retention value are different quantities. Replacing online learning with static heuristics should degrade under workload shift. And the gains should be larger on multimodal and long-prefix workloads than on short-text workloads, which is exactly where heterogeneous costs matter most.1a:T40d,For a quick read, start with the introduction and the formalization section, then move to the three modules, and finish with the main results. Three things deserve attention. First, check the objective carefully: the paper optimizes TTFT, throughput, and transfer volume, not cache hit rate in isolation. Second, focus on the CARS cost decomposition, because that is where the necessity of the method becomes clear. In disaggregated serving, both admission and miss have explicit costs. Third, pay special attention to the multimodal experiments, where the policy differences are most visible.

If time is limited, 25 to 35 minutes is enough for a useful pass. Prioritize the problem setup figure, the cost model equations, the main TTFT and transfer tables, and the ablations. If you want to go deeper, two natural follow-up questions are whether the predictor remains calibrated under workload shift or new modalities, and whether this policy can be jointly optimized with prefix-aware routing, speculative decoding, or KV compression.1b:T981,HEAT makes the iteration count at each approximation site learnable. For every nonlinear function replaced by an iterative solver, the paper considers the sequence of intermediate approximations F0, F1, ..., Fn produced by successive solver steps. The iteration count is discrete and not directly differentiable, so the authors introduce a learnable distribution over possible step counts at each site. Instead of choosing a hard step count during training, the forward pass uses the expected approximation under that distribution. This makes the objective differentiable with respect to both the halting distribution and the model weights.

To bias the solution toward shallow approximations, the loss includes a KL penalty that pulls each site’s learned distribution toward a negative-binomial prior whose mode lies at low iteration counts. Task loss pushes in the opposite direction when more precision is actually needed. The result is a site-specific equilibrium: many locations collapse to fewer iterations, while sensitive locations retain more depth. The method also uses activation-range regularization so that intermediate values stay in numerically favorable regions for approximation, reducing error amplification.

A key design choice is joint adaptation. The paper argues that merely learning per-site iteration counts is not enough. If weights remain fixed, many sites cannot be pruned aggressively because the original model was never trained under those approximation errors. HEAT therefore fine-tunes the pretrained model under the same approximations it will face at encrypted inference time, allowing the weights to absorb part of the error introduced by shallower solvers. This is the main distinction from prior FHE work that either hand-tunes approximation fidelity or redesigns the network to be encryption-friendly.

The training procedure is staged. The paper describes a three-phase schedule that first stabilizes the iteration distributions, then jointly optimizes distributions and weights, and finally converts the soft distributions into deployable discrete iteration counts. This staged recipe matters because fully joint optimization from the start would mix approximation-policy drift with weight adaptation and can be unstable. Implementation-wise, all Fi come from a single solver rollout, and backward recomputes intermediate states from checkpointed inputs, keeping memory overhead manageable.1c:T630,The headline result is on encrypted GPT-2. HEAT reduces the total number of approximation iterations by 3.1×, lowers end-to-end decoding latency from about 54 s/token to about 38 s/token, and improves top-1 agreement with the plaintext parent model from 74% to 83%. That combination is important: the method is not simply trading away fidelity for speed, but recovering part of the lost accuracy through co-adaptation of weights and approximation budgets.

The most informative ablations compare three settings: fixed uniform iteration counts, learned per-site iteration counts without weight adaptation, and full HEAT with joint weight training. Adam’s diagonal preconditioning provides an implicit second-order effect without explicit Hessian inversion.
