9:T6bb,CatchUp is a training-free framework for depth-aware visual-token re-admission. Instead of making a one-shot keep/drop decision, it re-evaluates query-conditioned visual relevance at selected decoder layers. At each routing layer, the model uses attention from the last text token to visual tokens as a relevance proxy, keeps only the selected visual tokens active, and defers the rest rather than deleting them. Between routing layers, only active visual tokens continue through the decoder, reducing attention, FFN, and KV-cache cost.

The key contribution is what happens when a deferred token becomes relevant again. Prior recoverable routing methods simply reinsert the old hidden state, which is stale. The authors first run an oracle analysis with full-token trajectories and show that replacing stale states with same-depth oracle states substantially improves grounding. This establishes that representation staleness is the real bottleneck. CatchUp then approximates the missed evolution using reference-to-current transition signals from semantically and spatially related active visual tokens. Intuitively, it borrows how nearby tokens changed from the reference depth to the current depth, and transfers that change to the returning token. So it does not replay all skipped layers; it estimates the missing state update from related tokens.

Compared with irreversible pruning, CatchUp can recover late-emerging evidence. Compared with recoverable routing without state correction, it explicitly addresses hidden-state mismatch at re-entry. The oracle replacement study is important because it validates the design target: the problem is not re-admission itself, but stale representations at the current depth.a:T614,The main experiments are built on LLaVA-1.5-7B, with referring-expression grounding and general multimodal benchmarks. The motivating analysis is one of the strongest parts of the paper. On 500 RefCOCO samples with 576 visual tokens and top-64 relevance sets, the average adjacent-layer volatility of the top-K visual token set is 28.85%, showing that query-relevant evidence changes substantially across depth. Using layer 2 as the early-selection reference, the late-emerging evidence ratio exceeds 50% by layer 7 and reaches 99.42% at layer 31. In other words, most tokens that matter later would be missed by an early irreversible selection.

On the main benchmarks, CatchUp achieves the best grounding retention among the compared efficient methods under matched routing budgets. Under matched prefill computation, it uses the fewest visual tokens and the smallest decoder KV-cache, while reducing end-to-end latency by 8.0% relative to dense inference. The paper emphasizes that the gain is not from more aggressive pruning alone, but from preserving accuracy better at the same budget.

The most informative ablation is oracle state replacement: if re-admitted tokens are given their full-token same-depth states, grounding performance improves markedly, confirming that stale states are the dominant issue in recoverable routing. Additional ablations indicate that semantic and spatial transition signals are complementary, and that routing frequency matters: too few routing points miss late evidence, while too many reduce the efficiency benefit.b:T40c,The main value for pretraining and multimodal foundation-model research is not just the pruning recipe itself, but the interface insight it reveals. First, encoder outputs already contain a strong signal about how the downstream LM will use audio tokens. That matters for audio tokenization, projector design, and encoder–decoder co-training. Second, the work shifts compression from inside the LM to before the LM, which is the only place where one saves both compute and context budget. That makes the result relevant to long-audio modeling and unified multimodal context allocation. Third, the finding that future all-layer attention is linearly decodable from encoder representations suggests a stable linear structure between encoder features and LM usage. That opens up natural follow-ups: auxiliary pretraining losses, distillation targets, or tokenizer learning objectives aligned with downstream LM salience. This is not a new modeling paradigm, but it is a useful systems-and-representation result with clear transfer value.c:T7e2,The method is simple, and the paper’s strength is in how carefully the problem is framed and validated. For each audio token i, the authors define a target score g_i: the total attention the token receives from audio positions, summed over all heads and all layers of the language model. In their prompt format, the question comes after the audio, so under causal attention the audio positions never see the question. That means g_i is independent of the question and can be used for pre-question pruning.

The key empirical finding is that g_i can be predicted from the encoder output e_i with a linear map, s_i = w^T e_i + b. This predictor, called the prior, is fit in closed form and does not require labels or a separately trained neural predictor. Triage then uses a two-stage pruning strategy. Stage 1 prunes before the LM runs, using the prior ranking; this is the stage that saves both prefilling cost and context slots. Stage 2 is used for multiple-choice settings: after two LM layers, the method refines the ranking using observed attention and prunes again. Importantly, the paper argues that for audio, mid-LM pruning should be a correction step rather than the main mechanism, because early-layer attention is still high and early rankings are not yet reliable.

Several technical details matter. The paper compares the prior against other signals available before the LM runs—position, loudness, self-information, encoder attention, and related waveform/word properties—and finds they are much weaker predictors of future LM attention. It also performs controlled analyses showing that after accounting for position and other confounds, the prior retains 95%–97% of its correlation on Qwen 3B, which argues against a trivial positional explanation. Finally, deletion studies show that removing tokens ranked highly by the prior hurts recognition more than removing energy-matched or position-matched tokens, making the predicted score look functionally meaningful rather than merely correlated.d:T78b,The evaluation spans 13 large audio language models. On 11 of them, the linear prior predicts the all-layer attention ranking with Spearman correlation ρ ≥ 0.69. By contrast, common heuristics such as loudness, self-information, and encoder attention reach at most |ρ| = 0.31 on seven tested models. This is the central empirical result: future LM usage is much more predictable from encoder representations than from standard salience heuristics.

The paper first establishes why image-style pruning intuition does not transfer to audio. On Qwen3-Omni-30B, at layer 2, text positions allocate 16.2% of their attention to audio, versus only 3.6% to images and 3.9% to video. In a controlled comparison using the same RACE passage rendered as speech versus as a page image, audio receives 2.3× the page’s layer-2 attention on Qwen 30B and 2.1× on Qwen 3B. Meanwhile, the agreement between layer-2 ranking and final all-layer ranking is only ρ = 0.39, so early LM attention is not yet a reliable ranking signal for audio.

On transcription, under a conservative budget, Triage keeps WER within 0.04 of full-audio inference. Under an aggressive budget, it achieves lower WER than every baseline, including mid-prefill methods, in all 12 transcription settings. On multiple-choice tasks, at 2.2× to 5× compression, Triage outperforms DART—the strongest baseline on average—by 0.043 mean accuracy. The systems impact is also concrete: on Qwen2.5-Omni-3B, a 2.86× pre-LM cut increases the amount of audio that fits in context from 21.8 minutes to about 62 minutes. On natural recordings of 20–43 minutes, aggressive Triage beats truncation, one-minute window voting, and DART at matched compression. At the most compressive setting, one GPU can serve 4× as many concurrent streams.

These results matter because they show gains not only on offline accuracy metrics, but also on context capacity and serving throughput.e:T537,For a quick but useful read, focus on three parts.
