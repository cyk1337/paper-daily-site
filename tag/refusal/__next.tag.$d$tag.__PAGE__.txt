The data is built with a dual-track scientific misuse generation pipeline. For seven material domains, the pipeline extracts 1,548 controlled substances from regulatory sources such as CWC, CDC/APHIS Select Agents, DEA schedules, EU REACH, and UN conventions. These substances are treated as hazard primitives. Questions are expanded along the weaponization lifecycle: upstream acquisition and substitution, midstream optimization and scale-up, downstream stabilization and storage, and delivery and dissemination. The pipeline also incorporates technical bottlenecks, such as stability, screening evasion, or transmissibility. For non-material domains such as computer science, psychology, sociology, economics, and law, the benchmark uses a scenario-driven route because the risk comes from exploiting rules, information flows, or systemic logic. The scoring framework, DeHarm-Score, decomposes harmfulness into query hazard severity, refusal behavior, and response-level risk. For non-refused answers, response-level risk is split into Executability and Net-New Risk. Executability is measured with dynamic importance-weighted checklists and critical blocking steps. Net-New Risk uses retrieval-augmented claim extraction and synthesis-barrier verification to estimate whether the response provides new operational capability.b:T679,For each behavior, the authors build a matched contrastive dataset with positive examples that elicit the behavior and negative examples that suppress it. At a chosen layer of a decoder-only Transformer, they take the last-token residual-stream activation and compute paired contrast vectors between positive and negative examples. These vectors are centered and decomposed with SVD. Instead of representing a behavior by a single steering direction, the method represents it as a top-k low-rank subspace.

A key step is removing decision-aligned variance. The leading singular components can reflect the model’s final positive-vs-negative decision rather than behavior-specific structure. The paper defines a scalar decision margin using the log-probability difference of label strings, identifies components aligned with this margin, and separates them from the behavior subspace analysis. This is meant to avoid conflating decision readout with the internal representation of the behavior.

The diagnostic then studies two quantities. The first is geometric overlap between behavior subspaces, measured through projection-style or principal-angle-style comparisons. The second is decision coupling, which measures how strongly a behavior subspace is connected to the model’s decision direction. Projection-based interventions are applied to a source behavior subspace, and the induced changes are measured on target behaviors. Overlap explains whether an intervention can reach another behavior; decision coupling explains whether that shared representation actually affects the output.
