18:T415,DScout introduces two decompositions. For training, it factorizes each position’s loss into inclusion, weighting, and prediction loss. This separates which positions receive supervision, how much they matter, and how the local prediction objective is computed. The resulting recipe combines mixed-horizon training, intra-block unmasking, and acceptance-weighted loss. Mixed horizons let one drafter support multiple inference block sizes. Intra-block unmasking exposes part of the block and asks the model to predict the rest, matching the conditional structure of block-diffusion drafting. Acceptance weighting reflects the prefix-survival probability that determines contribution to MAT. For inference, the paper writes throughput as G = MAT / T and uses this to choose target-conditioning depth, drafter depth, and block size. The implementation builds on DFlash: a frozen target model provides hidden states from selected layers; these features are concatenated, linearly fused, and used to condition a lightweight block-diffusion drafter.19:T6d3,The method is training-free and does exactly what its name suggests: it reanchors the existing drafter at shifted positions using verifier states that would otherwise be thrown away. In DFlash-style drafting, the drafter conditions on a target-generated bonus token at the last verified position plus projected prefix hidden states, and predicts a fixed-size block in one pass. If verification rejects early inside that block, the target hidden states computed for later positions are not valid as reusable target caches because they depend on rejected tokens. However, the paper observes that they still contain useful local information for the pretrained drafter.

ReAnchor exploits this by launching additional draft blocks from later positions in parallel with the baseline block. These shifted anchors come from the discarded verifier hidden states in the post-rejection suffix. Each reanchored block preserves the drafter’s original trained block length, so the method does not push the drafter outside its training configuration. To combine the blocks, ReAnchor matches the starting tokens of a reanchored block to the baseline block and, when they align, attaches the continuation at the matched position. This yields two effects: in-block refinement, where nearby predictions inside the original block are improved, and block extension, where draft coverage goes beyond the original endpoint.

A key property is that decoding remains lossless. Any attached continuation is still verified by the target model under its new prefix, so the target distribution is preserved. Another strength is portability: the method does not require retraining DFlash and transfers directly to DFlash-based variants such as Domino, DSpark, and Draft-OPD.1a:T52c,The headline numbers are clear. With DFlash drafters, ReAnchor improves average acceptance length by 15.2% and average decoding speed by 13.4% across four target models and eight benchmarks at T=0. The largest gains are on Gemma 4-12B, where average acceptance length improves by 19.4% and end-to-end speed by 17.9%.

The method also transfers well. Across three DFlash-based variants—Domino, DSpark, and Draft-OPD—evaluated on two Qwen3 target models and eight benchmarks, for a total of 48 comparisons, ReAnchor improves average acceptance length by 11.8% and end-to-end decoding speed by 9.8% over the corresponding baselines. This supports the claim that the gains come from verifier-state reuse rather than from a quirk of one specific drafter.

The ablations separate the benefit into two components: in-block refinement and block extension, and both contribute to the final speedup. The introduction also points to an important supporting result, likely in Table 1: verifier hidden states beyond the first rejection remain useful for subsequent drafting. The experimental story is coherent: first establish that discarded states carry signal, then show that reanchoring converts that signal into longer acceptance and faster decoding, and finally show that the effect generalizes across multiple drafter variants.1b:T595,For a quick read, start with the introduction and Figure 1 to understand the limitation of block diffusion drafting relative to autoregressive speculative decoding: fixed block length, wasted computation after early rejection, and no natural continuation after full acceptance. Then read the preliminaries carefully enough to track the notation around the anchor token, bonus token, prefix context, and sample-wise verification; otherwise the reanchoring procedure is easy to misread.

The most useful parts of the paper are threefold. First, the analysis showing that discarded verifier states still carry useful conditioning information, likely around Table 1. Second, the exact matching-and-attachment mechanism used to merge reanchored blocks with the baseline block; this is where the refinement and extension effects come from. Third, the main result tables and ablations, especially whether acceptance length and end-to-end speed improve together and how stable the gains are across target/drafter combinations.
