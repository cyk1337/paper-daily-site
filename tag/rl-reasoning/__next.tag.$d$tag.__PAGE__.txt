9:T463,HIPPO (Hint-Injected Pairwise Policy Optimization) explicitly instantiates a contaminated/shortcut policy and penalizes it.
- Formulation: denote the current policy as πθ and a hypothetical shortcut-reliant policy as πC. Directly maximizing divergence (KL/TV) between them is intractable/unbounded, so the paper derives a bounded surrogate objective (via Pinsker/TV duality) that reduces to reward expectations over samples from both behaviors.
- Instantiating πC on-policy: use answer-level hint injection at the input level to simulate the information asymmetry of overlap (knowing the answer before reasoning), producing shortcut-like trajectories without accessing internal parametric memory.
- Reward/judging: use a Bradley–Terry pairwise comparator to prefer standard rollouts over hint-injected ones, avoiding issues common in pointwise reward models (miscalibration, intransitivity) and enabling lightweight judges.
- Training compatibility: plugs into group-based RL pipelines (e.g., GRPO/DAPO/PPO-style sampling and advantage standardization), replacing the scalar reward with anchored pairwise aggregation.a:T572,The method has three main components. First, problem generation: each instance contains a set of axioms and several candidate conclusions, and the model must choose which conclusion is logically derivable. This multiple-choice formulation is deliberate. Instead of asking for free-form proof generation, it keeps verification exact and cheap while forcing the model to evaluate several candidates that require comparable reasoning effort, reducing shortcut opportunities. Second, difficulty control: the framework exposes two explicit axes. One is proof-tree depth, the minimum reasoning depth needed to derive the correct answer. The other is logical expressiveness, starting from implication-only logic and progressively adding conjunction, disjunction, negation, and universal quantification. This separates “longer reasoning chains” from “richer proof search structure.” Third, RL post-training: the authors train on this verifiable environment with outcome-based RL and compare multiple RL methods and training distributions. A key design choice is curriculum training rather than mixing all depths and logic families uniformly. The paper’s novelty is less about inventing a new RL algorithm and more about reparameterizing the study of reasoning RL into a clean experimental system where scaling laws, curriculum effects, and transfer can be measured with much less confounding.b:T507,There are three headline results. First, scaling: the number of RL training steps T needed to reach 90% validation accuracy follows T∝D^γ with proof depth D, with R^2 above 0.99 over the observed range. More importantly, γ rises monotonically with logical expressiveness, from 1.04 to 2.60. In the simplest logic, training cost grows close to linearly with depth; in more expressive logics, the growth is much steeper. Second, robustness across methods and training distributions: the same power-law pattern appears across different RL algorithms, suggesting that the phenomenon is tied to task structure and training dynamics rather than a single optimization recipe. Curriculum training improves both efficiency and stability. Third, downstream transfer: training on ScaleLogic improves average accuracy across eight real-world math and general reasoning benchmarks by up to +10.66 points over the base model in the most expressive setting. A notable qualitative result is that low-expressiveness training plateaus early, while high-expressiveness training yields a steadily improving transfer curve.
