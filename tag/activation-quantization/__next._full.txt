18:T871,TORQ stands for Two-Level Orthogonal Rotation. The method decomposes MXFP4 activation quantization error into two nearly orthogonal subproblems and addresses each with a dedicated orthogonal transform.

The first component is macro-equilibrium rotation, which targets inter-block variance imbalance. Using a construction motivated by the Schur-Horn theorem, the method applies an inter-block orthogonal transform to redistribute activation energy more evenly across blocks. The intuition is simple: if block variances are more balanced, the shared exponent is less likely to be dominated by a few extreme blocks, so fewer small values are rounded to zero. The paper further argues, under its error model, that variance equalization is optimal or near-optimal for minimizing global quantization error. This is a meaningful departure from prior rotation-based PTQ methods such as FlatQuant or OSTQuant, which were largely designed for uniform integer grids and focused on suppressing coordinate-wise outliers.

The second component is micro-alignment rotation, which targets intra-block codebook utilization imbalance. After the macro-level balancing, TORQ performs another orthogonal rotation within groups or blocks so that local values spread more evenly across the MXFP4 quantization intervals. The paper frames this through a maximum-entropy principle: under a fixed energy budget, a distribution that occupies codebook bins more evenly uses the available representational capacity more effectively. In other words, the macro stage decides how energy is distributed across blocks, while the micro stage decides how the codebook is used within each block.

Implementation-wise, TORQ is a retraining-free PTQ method. The rotation matrices are constructed in closed form or near closed form from a small calibration set, without gradient-based retraining. At inference time, the rotations can be fused around linear layers with minimal parameter and compute overhead. A key point throughout the paper is that TORQ is not a direct transfer of integer-oriented rotation methods to MXFP4; its objective is explicitly tailored to the block floating-point structure.19:T5b3,From the provided full-text excerpt, the experiments cover mainstream open LLM families including LLaMA and Qwen, and compare against methods such as SingleQuant, DARTQuant, FlatQuant, and OSTQuant. The main empirical claim is that TORQ preserves model quality more reliably under MXFP4 activation quantization, especially in layers where heavy-tailed activations make shared-exponent quantization particularly fragile.

The most informative results are likely not just end-task scores, but the intermediate diagnostics that support the paper’s mechanism:
1. whether block variance histograms become flatter after the macro rotation;
2. whether the fraction of small-magnitude values quantized to zero drops materially;
3. whether MXFP4 codebook occupancy becomes more uniform and codebook collapse is alleviated;
4. how much gain comes from macro-only, micro-only, and the full two-level method.

The introduction already gives two important quantitative observations that motivate the method: more than 90% of small-magnitude elements in a block can be zeroed out when the shared exponent is driven up, and roughly 50% of the codeword range is activated by only 10% of the data during real inference. Those numbers are central to the paper’s argument. When reading the full paper, it is worth checking the exact perplexity or downstream accuracy gaps in the main tables, as well as robustness across block sizes, calibration-set sizes, and model scales.1a:T543,Read this one by following the error model first, not the final benchmark table.

Start with the introduction and the motivation figures. The paper stands or falls on whether the two claimed mismatches are real and consequential: inter-block variance imbalance and intra-block codebook collapse.

Then go to the theory section around the Schur-Horn-based construction. The key question is not every proof detail, but why block-energy equalization can be cast as an orthogonal rotation problem, and what assumptions are needed for the optimality claim. Those assumptions define the method’s real scope.

Next, inspect the micro-alignment section carefully. Check whether the maximum-entropy argument is a strict optimization target or mainly a design heuristic, and how it translates into an actual rotation matrix. If this part is approximate, the practical gains may come mostly from the macro stage.

In the experiments, prioritize three kinds of figures: block variance histograms, codebook occupancy or zeroing-ratio plots, and the main quality tables. That sequence makes it easier to connect the proposed mechanism to the final performance.

A serious read, especially if the goal is to adapt the ideas to quantization-aware training or quantization-friendly pretraining, will take around an hour.1b:T7f6,The method has two main components. The first is LAAQ, a layer-aware activation quantization scheme. Instead of forcing all saved activations into a uniform 4-bit format, the paper analyzes sensitivity across layer types and pipeline stages, then assigns different bit-widths accordingly. The motivation is straightforward: attention blocks, MLP blocks, normalization paths, and residual routes do not react to quantization noise in the same way. A uniform policy damages the most fragile activations first. LAAQ therefore treats activation quantization as a structure-aware allocation problem: more sensitive activations keep higher precision, more robust ones are pushed lower, and the average storage cost approaches 4 bits per element without the instability of true global 4-bit activation storage.

The second component is QuanGrad for 8-bit gradient storage and communication. Much of the prior gradient compression literature reduces All-Reduce traffic but still keeps local gradient buffers and accumulation in higher precision, so memory savings are limited. QuanGrad is more aggressive: gradients are stored in FP8, used for local accumulation, and communicated through a precision-preserving 8-bit All-Reduce path. The central issue is that naive FP8 accumulation introduces rounding error that slows or destabilizes training. The paper therefore adds scaling and error-control mechanisms to preserve effective accumulation precision while keeping the communication path in the same low-bit regime. Compared with earlier FP8-gradient efforts, the emphasis here is end-to-end stability of accumulation plus system-level gains.

From a systems perspective, AGoQ is additive rather than replacement-oriented. It sits on top of existing distributed training frameworks: activation quantization reduces forward-saved memory, gradient quantization reduces backward buffers and communication cost, and optimizer-state quantization can still be combined with it. That makes the proposal relatively practical for pretraining pipelines.1c:T590,The experiments cover LLMs from 8B to 34B on a 64-GPU cluster, with comparisons against Megatron-LM, Megatron with ZeRO, DeepSpeed, and COAT. The headline result is up to 52% memory reduction and up to 1.34× training speedup, while maintaining training loss and accuracy comparable to higher-precision baselines. That matters because the gain is not only “it fits”; throughput also improves.

The experimental story appears to validate three points. First, uniform low-bit activation quantization hurts convergence, while the layer-aware allocation in LAAQ recovers stability, supporting the claim that activation sensitivity is strongly layer-dependent. Second, low-bit gradient communication alone is insufficient because local storage remains a bottleneck; only when storage and communication are both quantized does the full system benefit appear. Third, compared with methods such as COAT that focus on 8-bit activations, AGoQ pushes average activation precision closer to 4-bit while still preserving training behavior, which is the most important empirical claim in the paper.
