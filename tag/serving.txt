18:T76c,The main idea is affix-oriented cache reuse. Instead of restricting reuse to shared prefixes, the paper generalizes it to any shared contiguous span, or affix, which may appear at the beginning, middle, or end of the context. This is a natural systems opportunity created by bidirectional attention: unlike AR models, DLLMs are not inherently prefix-centric.

The key challenge is deciding which affix-token KV states become meaningfully stale as the masked generation tokens evolve. ACache introduces Anchor Tokens for this purpose. It measures the cross-attention importance from masked generation tokens to affix tokens, identifies a small request-specific subset of influential affix tokens, and recomputes KV states only for those anchors. The remaining affix-token cache is reused. In effect, the method turns a coarse full-span refresh decision into a sparse token-level refresh decision.

Implementation-wise, ACache is built on top of Fast-dLLM. It replaces periodic full-cache recomputation with selective anchor-token recomputation, so it is an incremental but targeted improvement over existing approximate caching frameworks rather than a completely new inference stack. The authors also implement a shared-prefix prototype in Nano-vLLM to test whether the gains survive under a more realistic serving environment with paged memory management and continuous batching.

Relative to prior work, the distinction is clear. dLLM-Cache uses asymmetric prompt/response refresh and feature-similarity-based partial recomputation for response tokens. dKV-Cache focuses more on decoded-token reuse with delayed block recomputation. Fast-dLLM improves the overall inference framework but still relies on periodic refresh. ACache is novel in treating shared context itself as the optimization target and shrinking the recomputation unit from an entire shared span to a request-specific token subset.19:T504,The main results operate at three levels. First, quality recovery: compared with direct affix-cache reuse, which causes noticeable degradation, ACache recovers most of the lost accuracy across multiple benchmarks while recomputing only around 20% of affix tokens. That number matters because it suggests the stale-cache problem is sparse enough to be exploitable. Second, system gains: in the Nano-vLLM shared-prefix prototype, ACache reduces recomputation latency by up to 55.7% and improves end-to-end throughput by up to 1.68x. This indicates that the benefit is not just a FLOP-level argument but can survive a realistic serving stack. Third, scope: the method applies to shared spans beyond prefixes, which is practically relevant because reusable text in production often appears as retrieved snippets, tool outputs, templates, or summarized dialogue history rather than only as a left prefix.

For a researcher, the most informative part is likely the ablation structure rather than any single headline number: how stable the anchor-selection rule is, how quality changes as the recomputation ratio varies, whether affix position matters, and how stale-cache error accumulates across denoising steps. If the paper includes those plots, they are the ones worth studying closely.1a:T8e2,The paper first performs a systematic diagnosis, then proposes a mitigation method. The study covers two representative MLLMs: Wan2.2 for multimodal generation and Qwen3-VL for multimodal reasoning. It evaluates multiple low-precision formats, including MXFP8, MXFP4, and HiF4, across a wide set of components: vision encoders, text encoders, attention blocks, feed-forward layers, multimodal projection layers, and MoE modules. The analysis shows that FP8 is nearly lossless, while FP4 degradation is highly heterogeneous. In Wan2.2, the visual generation backbone is the main bottleneck and the UMT5 text encoder is relatively robust. In Qwen3-VL, the vision encoder is especially sensitive, though the language backbone also degrades under FP4. Crucially, ablations separating weight-only from activation quantization indicate that activation quantization is the dominant source of performance loss.

Based on this diagnosis, the paper proposes Residual Fallback Quantization (RFQ). Unlike threshold-based outlier fallback methods, which detect large values and route them through a higher-precision path, RFQ uses a fully low-bit and differentiable decomposition. It first quantizes the activation into a primary FP4 representation, then explicitly computes the quantization residual and sends that residual through a secondary low-bit quantization path for reconstruction. Conceptually, the approximation is q(x) + q_res(x - q(x)), rather than a hard split between normal values and outliers.

This design has several practical advantages. It is differentiable and suitable for end-to-end training. It avoids threshold tuning, which is difficult when outlier behavior varies by module and evolves during training. It does not require full-precision fallback, making it more compatible with true FP4 execution. And because it models the current quantization error directly, it is better aligned with dynamic training-time activation statistics than PTQ-style calibration methods.

Methodologically, RFQ sits between static PTQ smoothing and mixed-precision QAT fallback. It accepts that FP4 cannot faithfully represent the full activation range in many MLLM modules, and therefore models the error instead of forcing the original distribution through a single quantizer.1b:T644,From the provided full text, several empirical conclusions are already clear.

1. Precision formats separate cleanly in quality: MXFP8 is close to lossless, while MXFP4 and HiF4 incur substantial degradation. This suggests that 8-bit training/deployment is already fairly mature for current MLLMs, whereas 4-bit remains the hard regime.
2. Activation quantization hurts much more than weight quantization. The paper runs extensive ablations to isolate their contributions, and the main takeaway is that compressing weights alone is often manageable, but pushing activations to FP4 causes the major collapse.
3. Sensitivity is strongly module-dependent. In Wan2.2, the WanDiT visual generation backbone is the main failure point, while the text encoder is relatively stable. In Qwen3-VL, the vision encoder is the most sensitive, though language modules are not immune. This argues against a single uniform quantization strategy across the whole MLLM stack.
4. RFQ recovers a meaningful portion of the FP4 loss without using high-precision fallback. The abstract excerpt does not provide exact numbers, but the framing makes clear that RFQ primarily addresses activation-outlier-induced instability rather than offering a small average gain everywhere.

If the full paper includes detailed tables, the most informative numbers to inspect are the relative gaps among FP8, FP4, and HiF4 on both generation and reasoning tasks, and the decomposition into weight-only, activation-only, and joint quantization. For pretraining researchers, those diagnostics are more useful than a single final benchmark score.1c:T574,Read this paper with diagnosis first and method second. Start from the experimental setup and module-level ablations before diving into RFQ details.

First, read the introduction and the main experimental tables to confirm that the study spans both generative and reasoning MLLMs rather than a single architecture. That context matters when interpreting which conclusions are likely to transfer.

Second, focus on the activation-versus-weight ablations and the ranking of module sensitivities. This is the highest-information part of the paper because it answers why FP4 fails, not just whether it fails.

Third, move to the RFQ formulation and implementation details. Pay attention to how the residual is quantized, what extra storage or bandwidth it introduces, and what the training and inference overheads are. If your interest is low-precision pretraining, these system costs matter as much as the recovered accuracy.

Fourth, if the paper includes activation histograms, outlier statistics, or layerwise error heatmaps, those are worth careful inspection. They usually explain the mechanism more directly than the final benchmark tables.

As a skim paper, 30 to 45 minutes is enough to capture the main message. If you are working on FP8/FP4 training, multimodal pretraining systems, or hardware-aware model design, it is worth spending 1 to 1.5 hours on the ablations and appendix details.1d:T5f7,The core mechanism is Expert Folding. Instead of dropping an excluded source expert or rerouting it to a similar target expert without correction, ExFold projects the source contribution onto a retained target expert. The method is motivated by two empirical findings: many experts produce directionally aligned outputs on the same inputs, but their output magnitudes differ substantially. Directional alignment makes substitution plausible; magnitude mismatch makes naive rerouting inaccurate. Based on this, each layer is calibrated with two matrices from a single frozen forward pass on unlabeled text. The scalar-projector matrix stores directed source-to-target scaling factors. The projection-loss matrix measures reconstruction loss for folding one expert into another. At inference time, retained experts are selected according to the phase budget: K_pre dominant experts per token for prefill, and D dominant experts per batch for decode. Every excluded expert is then assigned to the minimum-loss retained target, and its contribution is folded into that target by absorbing the scalar projector into the router weight. This gives one shared folding operator across both phases. Compared with token-wise sparsification, the method does not only reduce executed experts; compared with expert-set consolidation, it does not bake compression into a fixed submodel; compared with similarity-based rerouting, it explicitly corrects scale mismatch. The implementation is integrated into vLLM with a lightweight CUDA kernel.1e:T4a9,The evaluation covers multiple MoE architectures and real vLLM serving workloads, focusing on TTFT, TPOT, and quality retention. The headline numbers are up to 1.41× TTFT speedup and 2.45× TPOT speedup while preserving about 99% of the original average quality. The larger decode gain is consistent with the method’s focus on reducing the active expert set under memory-bound conditions. The more informative part is the comparison structure. Against pruning or skipping baselines, ExFold degrades quality more slowly at the same budget, which supports the claim that explicit reconstruction matters. Against static merging methods, it remains adaptive to token-level or batch-level budgets instead of committing to one permanently compressed model. The first major addition in V2 is a mean correction term. In the original truncation-based approximation, unselected blocks are simply dropped from both the softmax denominator and numerator. Intuitively, the method no longer treats pruned blocks as nonexistent; it represents their residual effect through block means. The second major change is a Hopper-oriented kernel redesign. The paper highlights PackGQA memory access, warp specialization, and pingpong pipelining. PackGQA improves memory organization for grouped-query attention and reduces bandwidth waste. Warp specialization assigns different roles to different warps to improve overlap and utilization.
