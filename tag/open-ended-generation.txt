18:T693,The method is simple. For a given input, sample multiple generations independently from the model. Map each generation into an embedding space using a sentence-level or sequence-level representation. Then estimate agreement geometrically. Their Embedding-Based Agreement (EBA) does not train a new scorer; instead, it uses clustering or concentration in representation space as a proxy for consistency. The core hypothesis is that semantically compatible and higher-quality generations form dense regions, while bad or inconsistent ones are more isolated.

From the full text, the pipeline is roughly: (1) sample n outputs; (2) encode each output with an embedding function; (3) identify dominant semantic regions via clustering or neighborhood structure; (4) select a representative generation from the largest or most central region. The paper compares different representation sources, including external embedding models and native hidden representations from the generator itself. An important point is that embeddings are not used merely as a semantic similarity heuristic; cluster mass and centrality are interpreted as a continuous analogue of self-consistency.

Relative to prior work, the differences are clear. Unlike exact-match majority voting, EBA does not require canonical answer forms. Unlike LLM-based evaluators, it avoids an extra judge model and the associated bias and cost. Unlike uncertainty-based selection, it does not score each sample in isolation using token-level confidence; it reasons over the geometry of the whole sample set. The paper also argues that majority voting in closed-form tasks can be seen as a discrete limit of the same geometric view.19:T5d5,The experiments cover MATH500, HumanEval, and CNN/DM, aiming to test whether representation-space agreement works across both verifiable and open-ended tasks. The main findings are consistent throughout the paper: EBA improves over random selection and several existing selection strategies, and remains fairly robust across model families, model scales, embedding spaces, and native hidden-state representations.

The most useful takeaways are in the analyses rather than the headline numbers. First, agreement becomes stronger as the number of sampled generations increases, which supports the claim that the signal emerges from the collective organization of samples. Second, EBA shows smoother scaling behavior than LLM-evaluator and uncertainty-based alternatives. Third, generations near central regions of representation space are much more reliable than peripheral ones, which is an interesting observation about the geometry of sampled outputs. Fourth, on closed-form math tasks, EBA aligns closely with classical self-consistency, supporting the paper’s unifying interpretation.
