9:T808,Vega is built around a shared vocabulary and a hybrid AR-diffusion design. Text and visual inputs are mapped into a unified discrete token space, but the model does not autoregress over dense pixel tokens. Instead, the AR component predicts semantic visual representations at the keyframe level, modeling high-level scene dynamics and state transitions. A diffusion decoder then acts as a renderer, turning those semantic embeddings into continuous, high-fidelity video. This division of labor is the core design choice: AR is used where discrete semantic transitions matter, while diffusion is used where local detail, realism, and temporal smoothness matter. The paper argues that this is better suited to video than pure AR, which suffers from excessive token counts and poor visual quality, and better integrated than prior hybrid systems that rely on frozen understanding backbones. On the generation side, Vega adopts a video-centric formulation in which multiple tasks are cast as video generation; image editing is treated as a special case of a two-frame video. To compensate for the detail loss of semantic tokens, the model uses noise-controlled visual conditioning, which preserves fine-grained appearance information and improves temporal consistency during rendering. On the understanding side, the paper introduces a dual-flow token selection mechanism inspired by the slowfast idea. A detail flow uses stronger spatial compression to capture rapidly changing local content, while a pivot flow uses lower compression to retain slower, structurally important temporal anchors. This is not just token dropping; it is a hierarchical temporal allocation strategy meant to preserve useful dynamics under a tight token budget. Another notable component is the generative visual loss used as auxiliary supervision for understanding. Second, the key contribution is codec-stream tokenization. Instead of treating a video as a fixed-GOP frame sequence, the model views the compressed stream as a continuous bit-cost signal. Temporal group boundaries are chosen adaptively from bit-cost dynamics, under the intuition that bitrate changes often correlate with scene transitions, motion changes, or semantic novelty. Within each temporal group, motion-residual cues are used to select salient spatial evidence and condense it into compact visual canvases aligned with the merge structure.
