The argument revolves around three objects: a moral subspace, a refusal direction, and a moral-judgment direction.

The moral subspace is built from three independent moral-content sources: Moral Stories, Understanding Fables, and ETHICS. For each source, the authors compute a moral-versus-neutral mean-difference direction, then orthonormalize the resulting set into an effective rank-3 subspace. This design choice is important. The goal is not simply to maximize dimensionality, but to obtain a construct-diverse, source-distinguishable representation that is less vulnerable to single-dataset contamination.

The refusal direction is extracted in the standard representation-reading style from the mean difference between refused and complied requests. A judgment direction is defined analogously for moral judgment. The authors then examine how these directions relate to the moral subspace across layers and positions, with special attention to the decision site: the assistant header or end-of-prompt control token where the refusal gate is read out.

A major strength of the paper is that OLMo-3 provides both base and post-training checkpoints, allowing the authors to track representational formation over training stages. They report that the moral subspace is already present in pretraining and remains nearly the same object through the final model, with a checkpoint-to-final cosine of 0.999. Post-training mainly rotates it once, by about 40 degrees, rather than rebuilding it. By contrast, the refusal gate has only a weak precursor in pretraining: proto-refusal-to-gate cosine is 0.155, about twice the matched null but still far from a mature gate. This asymmetry supports the paper’s central distinction between deep comprehension and shallow control.

The next step is geometric analysis at the decision site. Across four models, the control token is an 8-to-15 dimensional bottleneck. Within that bottleneck, the refusal-decision direction is largely separated from the moral-judgment direction. On OLMo, refusal projects only 0.10 of its norm onto the moral subspace, with mean absolute cosine around 0.06, below the moral-family band. This suggests that refusal is not broadly reading the moral subspace where comprehension lives.

The strongest evidence comes from a nested interchange rank sweep on OLMo. The authors progressively expand the rank of the patched moral basis and compare how refusal coupling and judgment coupling respond. If refusal depended on broad moral content, its transferred effect should keep increasing with rank. Instead, judgment coupling rises with rank and reconstructs about 0.66 of the subspace patch effect, while refusal coupling quickly saturates at the rank-1 harm level. That is the key causal result behind the title: refusal reads only a slice of what the model knows.
