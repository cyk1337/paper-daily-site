18:T42f,The evaluator state is decomposed into three typed sources: grounds for evidence, norms for decision rules, and authority for whether a rule applies to the case. For an old and revised evaluator state, each source can be taken from either version, producing a 2^3 intervention table called the judgment cube. Each hybrid state is executed through a trusted adjudicator. A source replacement set is a reason when it is sufficient to reproduce the revised verdict from the old state, and it is kept only if it is inclusion-minimal. The complete set of such minimal sufficient replacements is the judgment receipt. A key design choice is the separation between prediction and certification: a model can emit a receipt, but only execution of the counterfactual hybrids certifies it. The work also introduces paired consistency as an evaluation primitive. Control transformations carry known metamorphic relations; when a pair violates the relation, at least one prediction must be wrong. This gives an error lower bound without needing gold labels for every transformed item.19:T5fc,The evaluation covers 56,476 inferences: 4 models, 2,017 items, and 7 budgets. Non-monotone behavior appears in roughly 3% to 19% of items under the main controlled analyses, and reaches 25.8% in some all-item settings. It is also model-specific: the same item rarely induces overthinking across models, with cross-model overlap around 6% to 14%. Model rankings reverse across budgets. On GSM8K, LLaMA-3.3 70B leads at 256 tokens with 62.4% accuracy, while GPT-OSS 20B reaches 94.8% at 4,096 tokens and becomes the strongest model under that budget. On GPQA, LLaMA-3 8B is nominally first at 512 tokens with 21.2%, while GPT-OSS 20B reaches 51.0% at 4,096 tokens, close to another model at 50.0%; the small GPQA sample limits significance at that endpoint, but several intermediate reversals are significant. Truncation is a major confound: Qwen-3 32B remains truncated on 59.7% of GPQA items even at 4,096 tokens, while LLaMA-3.3 70B is truncated on only 0.5%. Oracle analysis finds large complementarity. On GPQA, the oracle exceeds the best single model by up to 27.8 percentage points. On GSM8K, the oracle gap peaks at +16.9 points at 256 tokens and then declines as models converge. The budget-aware router improves cross-domain GPQA performance by +2.67 points over the best-per-budget baseline, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.94, 4.40], capturing 14.1% of the oracle gap. Budget features help within-domain by +1.6 to +5.7 points but hurt cross-domain transfer by 1.2 points, indicating domain-specific budget effects.1a:T68f,The pipeline has three stages. First, CoT trajectory generation: for each question, a teacher LLM produces a zero-shot chain-of-thought trajectory. The key premise is that questions with different wording but similar logical structure become closer once their reasoning paths are made explicit. Second, contextualized trajectory embedding: the question and its generated reasoning trace are embedded into a dense space intended to reflect the latent reasoning manifold better than plain question embeddings. Third, isomorphic clustering and selection: clustering or geometric covering is performed in that space, and representative items near cluster centers are selected under a fixed budget.

A useful design choice is the decoupling between subset selection and score estimation. CoT-Core only constructs the subset. The selected coreset can be used with simple standard aggregation in a fully training-free setup, or combined with stronger estimators such as GP-IRT when historical logs are available. This is more flexible than IRT-style methods, which typically tie item calibration and score reconstruction together.

Compared with lexical embedding plus k-center selection, the novelty is not the sampler itself but the representation space: neighborhood structure is induced by generated reasoning traces rather than surface wording. Compared with IRT-based approaches, it avoids the cold-start dependency on historical correctness matrices. The trade-off is also clear: it is training-free, but not cost-free, because CoT trajectories must be generated for the benchmark upfront. The method assumes that this one-time cost is amortized over many future evaluation runs.1b:T58c,The experimental section is organized around three questions: whether CoT-based representations are better than plain text embeddings for coreset construction, whether the selected subsets remain faithful in cold-start score estimation, and when the gains disappear.

The paper compares against two baseline families: history-dependent approaches such as IRT/GP-IRT, and training-free geometric methods based on question embeddings, including k-center-style selection. The main result is that in cold-start settings, CoT-Core consistently outperforms text-only selection, especially on benchmarks with nontrivial multi-step reasoning. The selected subsets better preserve full-benchmark score estimates and model ranking. The paper also states a boundary condition: when tasks are dominated by factual recall, template matching, or shallow judgments, the benefit of reasoning-aware pruning becomes smaller because the underlying reasoning manifold is weak to begin with.

The most informative ablations vary the use of CoT, the embedding construction, the clustering/selection strategy, and the downstream estimator. The main takeaway is that the gain comes primarily from the reasoning-aware representation rather than from a particular clustering heuristic. The provided content does not expose complete tables or reliable numeric values, so it would be inappropriate to invent exact percentages or correlation scores.1c:T60b,WorldReasoner turns each resolved event question into a reproducible historical forecasting task by freezing the information environment at a simulated date. Each instance includes the question, a forecast window (earliest forecastable date t0, simulated date tsim, resolution date tres), the resolved outcome y, and a timestamped evidence repository A. A Temporal Gateway exposes only evidence items with timestamps <= tsim, enforcing a strict temporal boundary while keeping evaluation reproducible.

Agents submit: a predicted answer with probability p, cited/accessed sources C, an optional natural-language rationale, and—when enabled—a forecast-time causal event graph GF. After resolution, a Hindsight Agent constructs reference evidence CH and a reference causal graph GH using evidence up to tres.

Scoring is explicitly multi-axis:
1) Outcome quality: accuracy plus proper scoring rules (Brier/log score) to evaluate probabilistic forecasts.
2) Evidence quality: checks time validity (no post-tsim sources) and relevance/precision of cited or accessed evidence, separating “right for the wrong reasons” from grounded forecasting.
3) Reasoning quality: compares GF against GH to measure key-event recovery and structural alignment, aiming to quantify whether the agent’s causal story covers hindsight-validated drivers.

The paper also describes a scalable construction pipeline sourcing questions from prediction markets/news streams, collecting timestamped evidence, and auto-building hindsight graphs to expand the benchmark.1d:T5a8,The paper introduces a three-level taxonomy of STC. Benchmark Metadata Leakage refers to cases where the agent’s search is steered toward benchmark-related artifacts such as dataset pages or mirrored repositories. Question-Context Leakage refers to retrieved content that reveals the source, framing, or near-duplicate context of the benchmark item without necessarily exposing the final answer. Explicit Answer Leakage is the strongest case, where the correct answer or uniquely identifying answer evidence is directly retrieved.

Methodologically, the work is about contamination detection and impact measurement rather than building a new agent. The authors log full search trajectories, including queries, visited URLs, page contents, and turn-level behavior, and then apply detection algorithms to identify leakage at both the question level and the turn level. This allows them to ask not only whether contamination occurred, but also when it occurred and whether the agent’s reasoning trajectory shifted after the leaked evidence was observed.

A useful conceptual distinction in the paper is between training-time contamination and inference-time contamination. STC is not primarily about memorized test data inside model parameters. It is about benchmark artifacts being pulled into the context window through tool use during evaluation. That makes it especially relevant for deep research agents built on ReAct-style iterative search.1e:T4fb,The study covers six public benchmarks, with a strong focus on medical and clinical settings because those questions are widely exposed across educational websites, forums, repositories, and exam-prep platforms. All evaluated benchmarks show some degree of STC. Several numbers stand out. On MedMCQA, nearly one quarter of the questions have answers retrievable from the web. On the biological and chemical subsets of Humanity’s Last Exam, STC-induced score inflation reaches up to 4%. Once an explicit leaked answer is retrieved, the agent’s accuracy rises above 95% regardless of task difficulty, and its trajectory rapidly shifts away from its earlier reasoning path toward the leaked answer.

The paper also inspects commercial systems. For Gemini Deep Research, in a sample of 100 MedQA questions, the agent visited pages containing both the original question and its answer in 60% of cases. This suggests the issue is not limited to academic prototypes.

The most informative part of the experiments is not just the contamination rate, but the behavioral effect after contamination. Answer leakage leads to both higher accuracy and faster convergence, which supports the claim that retrieval is replacing reasoning rather than merely providing weak auxiliary hints.1f:T411,This is a skim paper. Read the introduction, the three-way STC taxonomy, the detection procedure, and the case studies. Around 20 minutes should be enough to capture the main contribution.

Two questions are worth keeping in mind while reading. First, how cleanly does the paper separate metadata leakage, context leakage, and explicit answer leakage? Second, does the detection only check whether leaked content appeared on a page, or does it also examine whether the agent’s behavior changed after retrieval? The latter matters more because seeing leaked content and actually exploiting it are not the same thing.

If you only inspect one part of the experiments, prioritize MedMCQA, the HLE subsets, and the commercial-agent examples. Those sections make the evaluation fragility most concrete. The mitigation discussion at the end—sandboxed environments, transparent search traces, private test sets, and dynamically generated evaluation instances—is also worth reading because it directly affects future RL and agent-tuning loops.20:T67c,The work has two components. First is the MixRea benchmark. Each example contains a story context with implicit information, a hypothetical question with explicit information, and four candidate event sets. Each candidate consists of two events, and the correct choice must satisfy both the explicit query and the latent contextual cues. The benchmark is built on Possible Stories, which is a good substrate because multiple questions share the same context and answer options, making it easier to recombine explicit and implicit constraints in a controlled way. The authors filter conflicting question pairs, then use GPT-4o for rewriting, pairing, validation, and quality control, producing 2,246 multiple-choice questions across 9 reasoning types with varied explicit/implicit information distributions.

Second is PRCP, Potential Relation Completion Prompting. Instead of asking the model to simply reason longer, PRCP explicitly asks it to surface possible latent relations before making the final choice. The paper’s error analysis suggests that many failures happen early: the model never completes the hidden causal relation needed to connect the context to the task. PRCP is designed to externalize that step. Compared with standard CoT, it is more targeted at relation elicitation; compared with one-shot prompting, it reduces the tendency to answer by shallow pattern matching. The prompt itself is not the main contribution. Its real role is causal diagnosis: if performance improves when latent relation completion is forced, then the bottleneck is likely in cue retrieval and multi-source integration rather than only in final answer selection.21:T58f,The benchmark is evaluated on 21 models from 7 families, including Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash, DeepSeek-R1/V3, GPT-4o/4o-mini, Claude 3.7, Qwen variants, LLaMA 3.x, and Gemma 2/3. The headline result is that performance is low across the board. Even the best model, Gemini 2.5 Pro, reaches only 42.8% consistency, which indicates that explicit-implicit integration remains unreliable even for strong reasoning-oriented models. Reasoning models generally outperform non-reasoning ones, but not by enough to remove the failure mode, suggesting that longer or more polished reasoning traces do not automatically translate into better use of implicit cues.

PRCP outperforms both CoT and one-shot prompting more consistently. The abstract does not expose the full gain table, but the full text presents the improvement as broad rather than model-specific. The authors also run ablations on three additional tasks involving explicit and implicit information, and similar failures persist there, which argues against MixRea being a narrow dataset artifact. The error analysis is the most informative part: models often understand the explicit question and can restate the context, yet fail to connect latent causal cues to the task objective. Without that analysis, one might misread the problem as weak commonsense knowledge; the paper makes a more precise case that the issue lies in relation completion and multi-source integration.22:T448,The contribution is platform design and maintenance, not a one-off dataset. - A lifecycle model: frequent benchmark launches/updates, human grading where needed, UI/API stability, and ongoing analyses (illustrated by the year-long timeline in Fig. 1).

- Task families: nine classical puzzles/search problems (SAT, cryptarithmetic, graph coloring, river crossing, Tower of Hanoi, water jug, checker jumping, Sudoku, Rubik’s cube), each parameterized so complexity can be increased monotonically while preserving semantics.

- Cross-domain generalization: compare collapse behavior across structurally different task families to test whether improvements transfer (algorithmic skill) or remain task-specific (heuristics/pattern matching).
