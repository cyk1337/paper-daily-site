18:T566,The technical contribution is mainly a clean formalization. The paper separates the actor from the critic and grader. Under Pass@K, the actor samples K candidates independently from the original prompt. Under Seq@K, attempt t is conditioned on the interaction history, including previous outputs and feedback messages. So both metrics ask whether the task is solved within a budget of K attempts, but they probe different capabilities: independent coverage versus conditional refinement.

The paper goes beyond the metric definition by varying the feedback mode. Feedback can come from verifiers, rubric-based critiques, and different levels of error specificity. The way prior history is retained also matters. An important framing choice is that Seq@K is not meant to be a full multi-turn agent benchmark. It intentionally isolates refinement from feedback, rather than mixing it with planning, tool use, or long-horizon exploration. That isolation is useful because many existing multi-turn benchmarks entangle too many abilities at once.

Methodologically, the paper turns an often informal notion—'this model improves when corrected'—into a reproducible protocol. It also makes a useful distinction between two improvement routes: increasing first-try accuracy and increasing recovery after failure. Pass@K is more sensitive to the former; Seq@K is designed for the latter.19:T6bc,The evaluation spans seven benchmarks across coding, complex instruction following, domain reasoning, and medical tasks, including MBPP Pro, HumanEval Pro, AdvancedIF, CLBench DKR, and HealthBench.

The first main result is that model rankings change. Some models look strong under Pass@K but gain little under sequential feedback, suggesting good first-shot performance but weak revision ability. Others are less dominant initially yet improve more reliably after feedback, making them more attractive in interactive deployment. The paper also studies cost frontiers and shows that the preferred model can change once sequential correction is allowed.

The second result is behavioral. Models differ in when they improve: some gain mostly on the first retry, while others improve later. More importantly, revisions are often poor in specific ways. Some models barely change their answer. Others address the explicit critique but break requirements that had already been satisfied. This is a useful diagnosis because it shows that 'responding to feedback' and 'repairing without regression' are distinct skills.

The third result is about the feedback itself. Informative feedback helps unevenly depending on both the benchmark and the model. Critiques written with access to the grading rubric tend to repair more failed requirements than generic feedback. Compressing prior attempts into summaries can reduce success, which means the history-retention protocol should be reported alongside Seq@K.
