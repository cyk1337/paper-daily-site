9:T491,AcquisitionSynthesis trains a generator–student loop. The generator produces a training example (prompt+response). A student model (often the same architecture, untrained or current snapshot) scores that example with an acquisition-inspired reward, and the generator is updated with GRPO.

Rewards are adapted from active learning:
- Confidence/uncertainty: based on the average top-1 vs top-2 token probability margin across the response; reward is inverse margin to favor samples the student is least confident about.
- Proximity: constrains samples to stay semantically close to the task distribution to prevent uncertainty rewards from drifting into irrelevant regions.
- Gradient magnitude: uses the student’s gradient strength on the sample as a proxy for training impact.
- Diversity: encourages coverage and reduces mode collapse.
- Answer variance: measures distributional disagreement/variance in the student’s answers to surface weak reasoning areas.

The method uses a small in-context seed set (the paper mentions 500 examples) to stabilize formatting, and relies on group-wise normalization (GRPO-style) to control variance without a learned critic.a:Ta9b,The method is a target-aware, uncertainty-aware sequential selection strategy. The setup assumes observations follow y = f(x; θ*) + ε, where f is a parametric scaling law, x is a configuration, y is the measured outcome, and c(x) is the experiment cost. The key departure from classical optimal design is that the acquisition objective is not built around generic parameter variance reduction. Instead, it asks which candidate run is expected to reduce prediction error in the target region the most.

The approach has three important pieces.

First, it maintains a set of plausible fits under the current observations, rather than relying only on a single local optimum with a local Gaussian approximation. The paper explicitly distinguishes two sources of uncertainty: local uncertainty around a fit, and disagreement among multiple plausible parameter settings that explain current data similarly well but extrapolate differently in the target regime. This is a strong design choice for scaling laws, because the practical failure mode is often not local noise but non-identifiability: several parameterizations fit the cheap region, yet diverge sharply at larger scales.

Second, it defines experiment utility in terms of downstream extrapolation. A candidate run is valuable if observing it is expected to resolve ambiguity that matters for target-region predictions. From the paper’s description, the acquisition combines how much plausible fits disagree at the candidate point and how informative that point would be for collapsing disagreement over the target region, while also accounting for experiment cost. In effect, the method prefers runs that are cheap but highly discriminative among competing extrapolation hypotheses, instead of simply chasing the most uncertain or most expensive points.

Third, the design is sequential. After each selected run is executed, the model refits, updates the plausible parameter set, and chooses the next point accordingly. This matters because early rounds can eliminate grossly wrong extrapolation shapes, allowing later budget to focus on the most decision-relevant regions. For low-dimensional but highly nonlinear scaling-law fitting, that is more robust than one-shot static design.

Relative to prior approaches, the differences are clear: the objective is target-region extrapolation rather than in-sample fit; the setting uses heterogeneous experiment costs rather than equal-cost assumptions; and uncertainty is represented as ambiguity across multiple plausible fits, not only local curvature around one optimum. The paper evaluates this framework against random selection, grid-like heuristics, and classical design baselines on a unified benchmark.b:T970,The experimental section is designed to test robustness across scaling-law scenarios rather than to showcase a single benchmark win. The paper builds a diverse benchmark spanning multiple scaling settings, different law families, and task-specific cost structures. One explicitly mentioned example is Step Law, covering learning-rate and batch-size scaling. On that task, the method reaches the low-loss regime with only 1% of the original fitting budget, approaching the extrapolation ground-truth optimum. That is a strong signal that large pilot sweeps can often be replaced by much more selective probing.

A key strength of the evaluation is the metric choice. The paper does not optimize or report only fit quality on observed points; it measures extrapolation accuracy on a held-out target region. That is the right criterion for this problem, because many baselines can fit the observed low-cost region reasonably well while still making poor predictions where the final training decision lives. The use of heterogeneous costs also makes the comparison more realistic: the question is not how many runs are selected, but how much budget is spent to achieve a given target-region error.

Several empirical patterns stand out. First, the gains are largest in the low-budget regime. When budget is abundant, many methods eventually recover a decent fit; the practical value lies in how quickly a method reduces target-region error when only a small fraction of the original pilot budget is available. Second, modeling ambiguity across multiple plausible fits appears important. A purely local approximation can commit too early to a misleading extrapolation shape, while the proposed method actively seeks points that separate competing hypotheses. Third, cost-awareness matters. Without normalizing utility by cost, the strategy can over-invest in a few expensive runs and lose overall efficiency.
