18:T76d,The main contribution is to treat EOS handling as a semantic alignment problem in the training objective, not merely a decoding-interface issue. The paper studies sampled-token OPD, where the student samples y_t at prefix s_t and the teacher scores only that sampled token. Contributions are averaged over valid generated tokens, without reward-to-go. In this setup, if the student samples its native EOS e* but the teacher prefers another functionally equivalent EOS, the student is explicitly pushed away from its own stopping action.

To formalize this, the paper defines an equivalence set of termination tokens, E_EOS, under the rollout protocol. It then compares four termination-handling strategies. The provided text does not list all four in full detail, but the key contrast is clear:
1. aligning the decoding stopping set alone;
2. aligning termination inside the distillation signal;
3. aggregating functionally equivalent EOS tokens into a shared semantic stopping action.

The main finding is that decoding alignment alone is insufficient. The problem is not just when rollout halts, but how the training signal evaluates the act of stopping. The effective correction is to aggregate equivalent EOS tokens semantically, so that any valid stop token is rewarded as the same action rather than treated as competing surface forms.

A second strong component is the stage-wise analysis using K2-Horizon checkpoints spanning pretraining, midtraining, SFT, and final post-training. This shows that EOS preference is not a static tokenizer artifact; it shifts across training stages. That explains why a base student and a post-trained teacher can strongly disagree on EOS usage despite sharing the same vocabulary. The paper also notes prompt-template sensitivity, which is practically important because formatting choices can alter both measured response length and apparent task performance.19:T6ab,The main ablation uses Qwen3-1.7B-Base as the student and Qwen3-4B in non-thinking mode as the teacher on single-turn mathematical reasoning. To test generality, the paper also evaluates Llama-3.2-3B Base/Instruct and Gemma-3-4B PT/IT pairs. In each pair, student and teacher share the tokenizer and vocabulary, which rules out the trivial explanation that EOS mismatch comes from incompatible tokenization.

One particularly informative detail is that in the Qwen3 setup, the teacher-preferred alternative EOS token has extremely small probability in the initial student, around 10^-11. That makes transfer through sampled-token OPD very difficult: the student rarely samples the teacher’s preferred EOS, so the teacher cannot reinforce it effectively, while the student’s native EOS keeps getting suppressed.

Empirically, the paper reports that:
- aligning the decoding stopping set alone does not fix length inflation;
- aligning termination semantics in the distillation signal, especially via semantic EOS aggregation, substantially mitigates mismatch-induced inflation across Qwen3, Llama, and Gemma;
- stage-wise K2-Horizon analysis shows how termination preferences shift over training;
- even after the teacher-preferred termination form is transferred, a later inflation phase can still emerge, with response length increasing again and termination probability collapsing.

The provided text does not include the full numeric tables for average length, truncation rate, or task accuracy, so those should be read directly from the paper. Next, focus on the section defining the four termination-handling strategies and the main ablation table. Pay close attention to how the equivalence class of EOS tokens is defined and where the aggregation is applied: logits, probabilities, or token-level advantages. Then read the K2-Horizon stage analysis, especially the phase where inflation reappears after EOS transfer has already happened. That part is more interesting than the mitigation itself if your goal is to understand OPD dynamics rather than just patch one bug.

Finally, scan the prompt-template and evaluation-protocol sensitivity results. They are a useful reminder that longer outputs should not be interpreted naively as stronger reasoning.
