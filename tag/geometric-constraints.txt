18:T6b4,The method, FactoSR, follows a two-stage recipe: supervised spatial fine-tuning first, then factorized reinforcement learning. In the SFT stage, the model is taught to produce structured outputs that separate observation, localization, reasoning, and final answer. In the RL stage, the authors build on RLVR with GRPO-style optimization, but replace the usual single correctness reward with a multi-objective reward design.

There are five reward components. A format reward keeps outputs structurally valid during RL. An accuracy reward preserves standard final-answer supervision. The key novelty lies in three geometry-aware rewards. The XY reward enforces cross-view planar consistency and correspondence, encouraging the model to identify the correct region before answering. The Z reward targets 3D localization and relative depth ordering, which is critical for front/back, near/far, and occlusion reasoning. The T reward enforces temporal cycle consistency and reversible camera-motion reasoning, pushing the model to remain coherent over sequences rather than treating frames independently.

This is not an architectural change to the backbone. It is a change in task decomposition and training signal design. Relative to prior spatial RLVR work, the differences are clear: it moves from final-answer-only rewards to factorized rewards, from mostly single-view reasoning to multi-view and temporal settings, and from implicit language-space reasoning to explicitly geometry-constrained optimization. The trade-off is also clear: the approach depends on datasets and annotations that support verifiable XY/Z/T rewards, so scalability and transfer will depend on how broadly those rewards can be instantiated.19:T59b,The headline results reported in the paper are gains of 5.9% on VSI-Bench and 4.5% on All-Angles-Bench, while maintaining decent general multimodal capability. The experimental story is less about one benchmark and more about whether the factorized rewards produce separable gains on separable spatial skills.

The key ablations compare SFT only, SFT plus standard RLVR, and SFT plus FactoSR-RL. The paper’s main claim is that answer-only RL helps somewhat, but remains unstable on depth, cross-view correspondence, and temporal consistency. Adding XY/Z/T rewards yields larger improvements on tasks that require explicit geometric reasoning chains. Reward ablations further support the decomposition: removing XY hurts cross-view localization and correspondence; removing Z weakens relative depth and occlusion judgments; removing T degrades camera-motion and temporal reverse-reasoning tasks. The authors also emphasize stronger generalization than pure SFT, arguing that RL encourages strategy learning rather than memorization of static patterns.

From the provided full-text excerpt, there is no reliable complete table of model size, training steps, or dataset scale, so those details should not be overstated.
