作者对每个模型分别拟合了 mixed-effects model 来估计 exposure 和 lag 的联合影响。结论是：base model 的 facilitation 对 lag 更稳健，instruct model 的 facilitation 随 lag 衰减，在更大规模时甚至从 facilitation 反转为 interference。Qwen 2.5 1.5B→14B 的成对 base/instruct 对比尤其关键，因为它控制了家族和架构差异，显示这种分化会随规模单调扩大。

三类机制实验也支持这个解释。移除先前 response context 后，base 仍保留部分 facilitation，而 instruct 更容易转成 interference；标签重映射后，base 仍有部分 transfer，instruct 基本没有；消融与 prior-occurrence retrieval 相关的 attention heads 后，base 的 priming 明显下降，而 instruct 受影响较小。这说明 base 的重复效应更依赖对先前词项的直接回收，instruct 则更依赖后续任务语境中的再判断。

人类实验给出第三种轮廓：和 instruct 一样对 lag 敏感，但整体保持 facilitation，不会像部分大 instruct 模型那样反转成 interference。这个结果很有意思，因为它说明 instruct 并不是简单“更像人”，而是形成了另一种受 post-training 强烈塑形的处理模式。

当前片段没有完整 cloze completion 数字和全部统计表，但主结论已经足够清楚：base 与 instruct 的差异是定性的，不只是幅度不同。1a:T7f0,The paper adopts the classic automatic-versus-controlled processing framework from cognitive psychology. Automatic processing is fast, stimulus-driven, and robust to interference. Controlled processing is more context-dependent and more sensitive to lag and disruption. To operationalize this for LLMs, the authors run two experiments.

Experiment 1 is semantic categorization. Models receive a zero-shot prompt and classify a concrete noun as living or nonliving. Each target word appears four times within a session, with a fixed number of filler trials between repetitions, producing lag conditions of 1, 3, 7, and 15. The authors measure a log-probability margin for the classification decision and define priming as the change from the first exposure to later exposures. This isolates the processing benefit of repetition from raw task accuracy.

Experiment 2 is cloze completion, where weakly constraining contexts test whether repeated exposure helps open-ended retrieval of the target word. This is closer to real generation behavior than binary classification.

The strongest part of the paper is the set of mechanistic probes. First, prior response context is removed to see whether priming survives. If base models retain facilitation, that suggests partial word-level reactivation rather than full dependence on the original context. Second, classification labels are remapped to test whether priming transfers beyond a specific output template. Third, attention heads associated with prior-occurrence retrieval are identified and ablated to test whether priming depends on those circuits. These probes support the interpretation that base-model priming is more stimulus-driven, while instruct-model priming depends more on contextual reevaluation.

This is different from standard probing work. Instead of measuring static representation similarity, the paper uses lag manipulation, context removal, label remapping, and head ablation together to distinguish direct lexical reuse from task-conditioned reinterpretation.1b:T89c,The study covers 15 models from 5 families, ranging from 1.5B to 14B parameters, including LLaMA 3, Mistral, Qwen 2.5, Gemma 2, and Phi-4 instruct. In semantic categorization, all 7 base models show strong and uniformly positive priming. By the final exposure, the E4 minus E1 effect ranges from +5.04 to +6.89, with all effects reported as significant. Instruct models are weaker and more variable, and some already show interference rather than facilitation; the excerpt explicitly mentions Mistral-Instruct and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct.

The authors fit per-model mixed-effects models to estimate the joint effects of exposure and lag. The qualitative result is consistent: base-model facilitation is stable across lags, while instruct-model facilitation decays with lag and can reverse into interference at larger scales. The controlled Qwen 2.5 series is especially informative because it holds family and architecture relatively constant. There, the base-versus-instruct divergence widens monotonically from 1.5B to 14B.

The mechanistic probes reinforce the behavioral pattern. Removing prior response context leaves residual facilitation in base models but tends to induce interference in instruct models. Label remapping preserves partial transfer in base models but not in instruct models. Ablating attention heads linked to prior-occurrence retrieval sharply reduces priming in base models, with much smaller effects in instruct models. Together, these results support the claim that base models rely more on direct retrieval of prior lexical traces, whereas instruct models rely more on downstream contextual evaluation.

The human experiments show a third profile: lag-sensitive like instruct models, but uniformly facilitative like base models, never reversing into interference. That is an important nuance. It suggests instruct models are not simply becoming more human-like; they are adopting a different post-training-shaped processing regime.

The excerpt does not include the full cloze-completion numbers or all statistical tables, but the main conclusion is already clear: the difference between base and instruct models is qualitative, not merely a smaller or larger priming magnitude.1c:T51e,This is a good skim for mechanistic understanding. Start with the introduction and make sure the mapping from automatic versus controlled processing to LLM behavior is clear. Then go straight to Experiment 1, because semantic categorization gives the cleanest signal and makes the base-versus-instruct split easy to see. After that, read the three mechanistic probes: context removal, label remapping, and attention-head ablation. Those sections determine whether the paper is just reporting a behavioral effect or actually supporting a mechanism. Finish with the scaling analysis and the human comparison.

A practical reading order is: last two intro paragraphs, Experiment 1 setup, main figure and results table, the three probes, Qwen 2.5 scaling, then human experiments. About 30 minutes should be enough to capture the main argument.

Three follow-up questions are worth thinking about. First, which attention or MLP circuits are altered by instruction tuning so that repeated words shift from direct reuse to reevaluation? Second, is the observed interference tied to alignment-induced context sensitivity, formatting dependence, or refusal-related policies? Third, should pretraining or mid-training explicitly preserve efficient lexical reactivation mechanisms that post-training may otherwise weaken?1d:T5a7,The study uses paired comparisons between base and instruction-tuned models from three families: Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Mistral-7B-v0.3. Evaluation is done on three multiple-choice QA benchmarks: ARC-Easy, MMLU, and CommonsenseQA. Confidence is measured in two complementary ways. First, the authors compute likelihood-based answer uncertainty using the entropy of normalized answer probabilities over the candidate options. Second, they elicit verbalized confidence with a two-stage protocol: the model first selects an answer via likelihood, then in a separate pass reports a numerical probability that the chosen answer is correct.

For rationale diversity, the model generates K=5 chain-of-thought rationales per question. Diversity is measured using Unique-2, which captures lexical richness through distinct bigram proportion, and Self-BLEU, reported as 1-SelfBLEU so that larger values indicate greater variation across sampled rationales. A strong part of the design is the controlled comparison restricted to examples where base and instruction-tuned models choose the same answer, with rationale length matched across variants. DFT starts from the observation that standard SFT has inverse-probability gradient amplification: rare or low-probability tokens can dominate updates. DFT rewrites the objective so that updates scale with a bounded term proportional to (1 + log pt), avoiding the blow-up as pt approaches zero.

CADFT argues that token-level correction is not enough because instruction datasets are highly heterogeneous at the sample level. Some demonstrations are well aligned with the model’s current capabilities, while others are too hard, too mismatched, or too noisy. To address this, the method introduces a dynamic compatibility signal, computed from the model’s own likelihoods over a sample, to estimate how compatible a demonstration is with the current policy.

This compatibility score is then used to modulate sample-level update strength. High-compatibility samples retain stronger supervision; low-compatibility samples are downweighted rather than discarded. Three design choices matter.

First, compatibility is treated as a relative quantity, not a fixed threshold. It is normalized adaptively so that the weighting remains meaningful as the model improves over training.

Second, sample-level compatibility is combined with DFT’s token-level correction. So the final update depends both on whether individual tokens are pathological and on whether the sample as a whole lies within the model’s current feasible learning region.

Third, the paper adds delayed rewriting for persistently incompatible samples. Instead of rewriting from the start, the method waits for a warm-up period and triggers rewriting only at low frequency. The rationale is to avoid early self-reinforcement, where a weak model would rewrite difficult but valuable demonstrations into easier yet lower-quality targets.
