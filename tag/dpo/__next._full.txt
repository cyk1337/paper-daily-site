1a:T702,The method has two layers. The first is the diagnostic metric, Contextual Preference Gain (CPG). For the same response pair, the model is evaluated with and without additional helpful context. CPG measures how much the preference margin increases once that context is added. The margin follows the usual DPO or Bradley-Terry view, based on the relative reward or log-probability gap between preferred and dispreferred responses. If a model truly leverages context, the correct response should become more strongly preferred when grounding information is available, so CPG should be positive and meaningfully large. Empirically, the authors show that higher CPG is strongly associated with lower hallucination, while standard DPO and several variants produce CPG distributions concentrated near zero.

The second layer is C²-DPO. On top of the original preference objective, the authors add a calibration term that explicitly rewards the preference margin under enriched context being larger than the margin without that context. This is the key difference from vanilla DPO. The change is not about a new reward parameterization; it is about writing context sensitivity directly into the optimization target. The same idea is also attached to SimPO and RDPO, which suggests that the proposal is a fairly general calibration add-on rather than a method tied to one specific DPO implementation.

Mechanistically, the method is well matched to settings where the preferred answer is only clearly preferred once grounding evidence is present. If chosen and rejected responses are already easy to separate without extra context, the gain may be limited. But for visual grounding, factual evidence, and other evidence-conditioned preference tasks, the objective is well aligned with the failure mode.1b:T5aa,The experimental story is straightforward. First, the paper validates CPG as a diagnostic: models with higher CPG consistently show lower hallucination rates. That gives the proposed optimization direction a credible empirical basis. Second, C²-DPO reduces hallucination on multimodal benchmarks by a clear margin. The headline numbers reported in the paper are a 36% reduction in response-level hallucination rate and a 60% reduction in mention-level hallucination rate on Object HalBench, with consistent gains on HallusionBench as well. Third, the paper checks whether this comes at the cost of general multimodal ability and reports that performance on ScienceQA, MM-Vet, and TextVQA is preserved.

Two additional results are worth noting. The calibration idea transfers to SimPO and RDPO, which supports the claim that the issue is objective-level rather than implementation-specific. The authors also test a text-only setting and find improved factual alignment there too, suggesting that context blindness is a broader weakness of DPO-style training, not only a multimodal problem.

When reading the paper, the main tables to verify are the CPG correlation plots, the hallucination benchmarks, and the cross-objective transfer results.1c:T5eb,Two pillars.
(1) MIST-1000 benchmark: each item is instantiated into four matched variants where the question, answer space/type, gold answer, and a plausible wrong answer are held fixed; only the surrounding context changes: clean (no added context), misleading (context nudges toward the plausible wrong answer), correct-context (context supports the gold), and irrelevant (natural related text that does not contain the answer). The dataset contains 1,000 items (800 adapted from existing QA/math/reasoning benchmarks, 200 newly written longer scenarios) with a two-pass human QA process to control ambiguity, leakage, and plausibility.
(2) Metrics + training:
- SC2W (signal-induced correct-to-wrong): computed on the subset of items the model answers correctly in the clean condition, measuring how often misleading context flips them to wrong—separating susceptibility from base competence.
- SCOPE (Selective Context Preference Optimization): a signal-counterfactual preference framework. It mines the critical failures from a base model: cases where the model is correct in clean but wrong under misleading context. For each, it forms a preference pair (truth-consistent response preferred over signal-following response). The key is not changing DPO’s objective, but balancing the training mixture: reuse the same preference pair across all four context conditions and weight misleading pairs equally against clean/correct/irrelevant controls, preventing the “learn to ignore all context” shortcut.1d:T8c1,The method, StaIF, is a stage-wise optimization framework rather than a single unified objective. The paper splits constraints into two categories. Soft constraints are subjective and preference-like, such as tone, style, or encouraging phrasing. Hard constraints are objective and programmatically checkable, such as valid JSON, required fields, word-count limits, language requirements, or explicit formatting rules.

Stage 1 uses preference optimization to learn soft constraints. A notable design choice is the use of multiple negative responses for the same instruction rather than a single chosen/rejected pair. This matters because failures on complex instructions are often partial: a response may satisfy some constraints while missing others, or may be semantically correct but stylistically off. Multiple negatives expose the model to a richer set of near-miss errors and sharpen its sensitivity to soft-constraint violations.

Stage 2 applies reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, implemented with GRPO. The key idea is to optimize hard constraints directly using deterministic verifiers instead of relying on a learned reward model or a holistic preference objective. For complex instruction following, this is a natural fit because many important failures are discrete and checkable.

On the data side, the paper proposes an automated synthesis pipeline and builds StaInstruct. The construction is not just random constraint concatenation. It explicitly models task-constraint compatibility and filters invalid, redundant, or conflicting constraints. Each example includes a complex instruction, a high-quality positive response, multiple negative responses targeted at soft constraints, and rule-based verification code for hard constraints. This data structure aligns cleanly with the two training stages: preference learning in Stage 1 and verifier-based RL in Stage 2.

Compared with standard DPO or single-stage RL, the novelty is less about a new optimization formula and more about decomposing the training signal to match the structure of the task. The paper’s claim is that a holistic objective tends to optimize overall acceptability, while stage-wise training improves strict per-constraint satisfaction.1e:T666,The evaluation is conducted on three instruction-following benchmarks: IFEval, IFBench, and CFBench.
