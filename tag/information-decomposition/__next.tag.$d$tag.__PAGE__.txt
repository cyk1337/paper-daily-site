9:T508,FlowGuard runs the target MLLM in three modes on the same example: text-only, vision-only, and full multimodal inference. It then compares the resulting predictive distributions and compresses their relationships into a low-dimensional FlowVector. The features are designed to capture three effects: how far the fused prediction moves away from each unimodal prediction, how much the two unimodal predictions agree with each other, and whether fusion reduces or amplifies uncertainty. The paper interprets these as proxies for modality-specific, redundant, and synergistic information. In practice, this is not a full partial-information decomposition; it is an operational approximation built from computable statistics such as divergence measures and entropy changes. Detection is framed as one-class classification trained only on benign examples, so the model learns a manifold of normal multimodal fusion and flags deviations at test time. Compared with prior defenses, the key difference is where the signal comes from: not input heuristics, not post-hoc output verification, but distributional behavior induced by multimodal integration. The trade-off is extra inference cost from multiple forward passes and a dependence on stable, comparable distributions across the three modes.a:T44d,The headline result is that FlowGuard reduces attack success rates from above 90% to below 15% across multiple MLLMs and unseen multimodal jailbreak settings, while keeping benign utility within 3% of the baseline and running about 6x faster than diffusion-based verification methods. The more informative part is the ablation story. Input- or output-level confidence features alone are clearly weaker than the FlowVector, which supports the claim that the anomaly lies in the fusion relation rather than in isolated uncertainty. Removing either the text-only or vision-only branch hurts detection substantially, suggesting that the three-way comparison is essential. The benign-only one-class setup also generalizes better to unseen attacks than attack-supervised binary classifiers, which is consistent with the paper’s argument that normal fusion behavior is easier to model than the space of attacks.
