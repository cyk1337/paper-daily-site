9:T41a,The paper introduces Approval Laundering as a taxonomy of approval–execution binding failures. It organizes the space into six failure modes: Scope, Argument, Temporal, Tool, Delegation, and Semantic laundering. The unifying idea is that a human approves action A under an explicit policy, but the harness executes A' without requesting renewed approval. The contribution is primarily formal and empirical rather than algorithmic. The authors define a falsifiable approval policy, distinguish it from subjective operator intent, and then design reproducible tests around different credential-binding surfaces. A key point is that many failures do not involve the model changing the literal command string at all. Instead, the mismatch arises from repository-local hooks, session-scoped grants, tool indirection, delegated execution, or UI/rendering mismatches that alter the downstream effect of an otherwise approved action. This makes the work different from prior benchmarks centered on command risk classification or over-broad scope inference.a:T422,The paper contributes a taxonomy, a benchmark, and a detector harness. Instead of explicitly enumerating all possible payloads and injection sites, the paper treats the attack surface as a latent space induced jointly by the environment, the benign user task, and the malicious injection task. To search this space, the attacker is given reconnaissance tools of two kinds: tools for understanding the environment and task context, and tools for understanding candidate injection vectors and their constraints.

The attacker then performs structured reasoning over possible strategies, explores promising subspaces, and uses victim-agent feedback plus environment-state changes to adapt future attempts. A key design choice is explicit strategy management. The paper argues that without tracking explored directions, pruning unproductive branches, and maintaining search state, larger compute budgets lead to redundant exploration and weaker scaling. In that sense, the search harness is what turns prompt injection from one-shot payload generation into an iterative attack process. Second, ablations indicate that explicit strategy management matters: when search tracking and redundancy control are removed, gains from larger budgets degrade because the attacker wastes compute revisiting similar attack directions.
