9:T519,For each intended theorem T, the authors construct a complete shadow set 𝒮={S1,...,Sn}. Each shadow theorem Si is chosen so that T implies Si, giving a forward checker. The set is complete only if the conjunction S1∧...∧Sn implies T, giving a backward checker. Both directions are encoded and mechanically verified in Lean, so completeness is not an informal annotation claim but a machine-checked property. During evaluation, the generated candidate theorem \hat{T} is appended with these checkers. It receives credit only if it type-checks and passes the implication tests induced by the shadow set. A notable design choice is the annotation workflow: experts do not write everything from scratch. LLMs first draft candidate shadows, experts revise them, and Lean verifies completeness. This keeps expert labor focused on correction and validation rather than blank-page authoring. The method is especially well suited to multi-conclusion theorems, bundled conditions, existence/uniqueness statements, and other structures where partial formalization can still compile. Compared with BEq+-style reference-based equivalence checks, SA-Pass is less tied to one canonical expression. Compared with compile rate, it explicitly tests whether the generated statement is neither too weak nor too strong.a:T51b,Three results matter most. First, the benchmark: ShadowBench contains 178 Lean 4 full-autoformalization problems across eight mathematical areas and three difficulty levels, ranging from postgraduate to research level. The reference formalizations are on average about 1.6× longer than those in earlier short-form benchmarks, reflecting a stronger emphasis on multi-conclusion and structurally richer theorems. Second, metric validity: across outputs from six agentic configurations, SA-Pass achieves 98.8% binary agreement with expert judgments, versus 17.8% for compile rate and 80.6% for BEq+. The low agreement of compile rate is the key point: it cannot separate semantically wrong but compilable statements from genuinely correct ones. Third, model performance: Claude Code (Opus 4.8) with Numina-Lean-Agent gets 61.8% compile rate but only 11.2% SA-Pass on ShadowBench, a drop of more than 50 points. The paper also analyzes common failure modes, including proving only part of a bundled conclusion, turning existential claims into universal ones, omitting side conditions, and replacing equivalence with one-way implication.
