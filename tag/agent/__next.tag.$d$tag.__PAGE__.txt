9:T465,ContextPilot expands the context-management action space and changes the RL procedure. On the tool side, it adds planning, long-term memory, and soft context offloading beyond search, deletion, and summarization. Planning gives the agent a global structure before later actions. Long-term memory stores scattered evidence in a reusable form. Soft offloading acts as adaptive compression or temporary externalization rather than hard deletion. On the training side, the paper introduces context-aware partial rollout. It identifies influential context-management actions using changes in context and policy entropy, then branches rollouts around these critical actions instead of exploring every action uniformly. For credit assignment, it estimates the advantage of an intermediate context-management action using rewards from subsequent branches. This gives a finer signal than assigning the final trajectory reward to every step. The training also uses trajectory snapshots and token-level loss masking so that edited contexts are represented without repeatedly optimizing tokens that already appeared in earlier snapshots.a:T792,GRAIN has three main components. First, it uses an explicit pipeline. Rather than letting the model mix extraction, graph construction, and computation inside unconstrained chain-of-thought, the task is decomposed into semantic parsing of the graph and task arguments, followed by tool execution with the appropriate graph algorithm. The motivation is straightforward: shortest path, matching, coloring, and related computations are deterministic; the real weakness of LLMs is the language-to-structure mapping.

Second, the method enforces structural invariance during training. The authors construct multiple isomorphic views of the same underlying graph: node identifiers may be random, semantic, aliased, or noisy, and the task description may range from standardized templates to more realistic narratives. The model is trained to recover the same intermediate graph despite these surface changes. This is more than ordinary augmentation, because the reward directly checks whether the predicted intermediate graph matches the ground-truth topology. That gives denser and more targeted feedback than final-answer supervision alone.

Third, GRAIN uses single-agent RL as a form of robustness distillation. The goal is not to build a multi-agent system, but to absorb into one model the robustness that would otherwise require repeated decomposition and verification across multiple agents. The RL objective jointly rewards correct structure recovery and correct tool invocation. The intended effect is to learn identifier-invariant and formulation-invariant parsing rules instead of memorizing templates.

The accompanying benchmark, GRIT, is also a substantial part of the contribution. It covers six graph problems across 31 real-world prototypes, with controlled multi-view variants for naming and narrative style. That makes it possible to separate failures of structure extraction from failures of downstream algorithmic solving.b:T6da,The experiments are built around robustness diagnosis rather than a single leaderboard number. The first result is that the robustness gap is real: under identifier shift and task-form shift, performance varies significantly even when the underlying graph is unchanged. Closed models tend to retain stronger average accuracy but still show unstable variance across naming schemes. Open models degrade much more sharply under non-canonical identifiers and realistic narratives. This is an important finding because it shows that strong performance on sanitized graph benchmarks does not imply reliable real-world graph reasoning.

Against baselines, GRAIN is reported to outperform standard CoT, SFT, and more expensive multi-agent approaches in out-of-distribution robustness while keeping latency and token cost lower. The most informative results are likely the breakdowns by naming scheme, narrative type, graph size, and task complexity. If the gains are consistent across those slices, that supports the claim that the reward is improving the language-to-structure stage rather than exploiting a narrow template.

A key ablation concerns the structural reward itself. The paper argues that rewarding intermediate graph correctness is better than rewarding only the final answer, and better than static supervision alone. The reason is intuitive: final-answer rewards are sparse and can be accidentally correct even when parsing is wrong, while structural rewards directly supervise semantic parsing quality.
