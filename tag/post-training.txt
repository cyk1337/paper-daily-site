The Teacher sees the problem plus privileged information. Instead of feeding the full reference solution directly, the method provides only a suffix of the reference reasoning and frames it as optional working notes, while also varying the surrounding prompt style. This serves two purposes: it reduces dependence on the wording and trajectory of the reference, and it pushes the Teacher to re-solve the problem from scratch, using privileged information mainly for verification and correction. Training alternates paired rollout generation and bidirectional preference updates. Teacher-origin trajectories supervise the Student, encouraging it to follow more reliable reasoning paths. Student-origin trajectories then provide compatibility feedback to the Teacher, shaping future Teacher rollouts toward trajectories that remain reproducible and learnable without privileged information. Relative to standard DPO-style preference learning, the preference signal here is not just about answer quality; it is about teacher–student transferability. Synthetic-document training may induce out-of-context generalization where the CoT looks plausible but no longer tracks the action. The paper reframes this as a representation transfer problem. CoT is an intermediate representation learned for the source task of producing high-reward outputs; monitoring is a downstream transfer task that tries to infer behavioral properties from that representation. The central question is therefore not how to make CoTs longer or more human-like, but how to train them so they retain output-relevant information that unknown future monitors can still use.1c:T60c,The method optimizes conditional mutual information between the chain of thought and the model output, given the prompt: I(CoT; Output | Prompt). The intuition is straightforward. If observing the CoT sharply reduces uncertainty about the output beyond what the prompt already tells us, then the CoT is carrying output-relevant distinctions that a downstream monitor may exploit. The paper does not stop at maximizing average mutual information. It emphasizes a lower-tail objective that focuses optimization pressure on the least informative trajectories. That design choice is sensible because monitorability failures are often tail failures: a small fraction of opaque trajectories can dominate safety risk.

A second ingredient is a conditional chi-squared regularizer. Its role is to limit changes in the output distribution conditioned on a given CoT, making it costly for the model to arbitrarily remap the semantics of CoT strings. Without such a constraint, a model could in principle improve the objective by creating a more tightly coupled but privately coded relationship between CoT and output. The regularizer is therefore a safeguard against semantic drift and selective legibility. Another notable design choice is that the procedure uses neither behavioral labels nor monitor feedback. This distinguishes it from direct monitor-reward approaches, which can overfit to a specific monitor and provide sparse supervision. For a problem x, the current policy samples K trajectories. When training on a student-generated prefix, the method selects M same-problem peers and one cross-problem solution as a control. A frozen copy of the initial model predicts next-token distributions under two conditions: the student prefix plus a same-problem peer, and the student prefix plus the cross-problem control. Let q be the question and a the correct answer. Forward CoT (FCG) samples reasoning from the teacher conditioned only on q. Rejection sampling (RS) keeps only those forward samples whose extracted final answer matches a. Reverse CoT (RCG) instead conditions the teacher on both q and a. The authors argue that the student should ideally learn from the RS distribution, not from raw RCG outputs.

To do this, they build a small paired dataset. For each question, they sample one reverse CoT response x, then sample forward CoTs until they obtain the first accepted response z with the correct final answer. This yields paired examples (x, z). A translator model G_phi is then fine-tuned with teacher forcing to predict z from x.

A crucial design choice is that the translator only receives the reverse CoT text x, not the original question q or answer a. This serves two purposes. First, it matches the paper’s distributional derivation, where the goal is to map source responses to target responses. Second, it blocks a direct question-answer shortcut and forces the model to learn how to rewrite answer-conditioned rationales into outputs closer to accepted forward reasoning.

Once trained, the translator is used at scale: each new question requires one reverse teacher call to produce x and one translator call to produce z-hat, and the student is then trained on (q, z-hat). Compared with RS, which may need many forward teacher calls per accepted sample, this pipeline compresses the expensive part into one cheap teacher sample plus one translation step.

Relative to prior semantic suppression or structural-guidance methods, the difference is conceptual. Those methods still generate under answer access and try to reduce leakage. CoTranslator instead learns an explicit distributional correction from RCG-style outputs toward RS-style outputs. The appendix further provides a distribution-recovery analysis to justify this framing.38:T609,The experiments cover six student models and six math benchmarks, with transfer tests to a new math dataset, a different teacher model, and STEM problems.

The first headline result is quality. For students such as Qwen3-4B-Base, CoTranslator improves average accuracy over semantic suppression by up to 5.82 percentage points. That indicates that merely hiding answer cues is not enough; explicit translation toward the verified forward distribution works better.

The second result is the cost-performance trade-off. The two 4B students retain 95.0% of the average accuracy of rejection sampling while using only 35.9% of its estimated compute. This is the central empirical claim of the paper. RS remains the cleaner target, but it is expensive; CoTranslator uses a small paired set to train a translator and then amortizes that cost over large-scale data generation.

The third result is transfer. Without retraining, the translator generalizes to a new math dataset, a different teacher, and STEM tasks. Average STEM gains are 4.88 points for 4B students and 4.31 points for 8B students. This suggests the translator is learning a reusable transformation from answer-conditioned rationales to more question-grounded ones, rather than memorizing a dataset-specific template.

The paper also includes analysis showing that translated outputs are closer to RS responses and exhibit fewer answer-dependent shortcuts than raw reverse CoTs.
