18:T4a7,ACLArena builds a fixed sequential pipeline rather than evaluating arbitrary task permutations. The order is Math → Search → E-commerce → Instruction Following. Math strengthens reasoning, Search uses a single tool, E-commerce involves up to 15 tools, and Instruction Following is introduced last for adherence and alignment. Sequential training is used as the diagnostic baseline. The analysis has two levels: a model-level view that tracks capability changes across stages, and a token-level view that studies how individual training signals shape behavior and optimization. The method comparison covers three practical strategies. On-policy distillation uses previous checkpoints as teachers on the student’s current state distribution. Self-distilled fine-tuning replays filtered specialist trajectories offline. Model merging reuses prior models directly in parameter space. The proposed Mixture of Low-Rank Experts first uses SDFT to learn valid behaviors across domains, then applies RL through stage-specific LoRA adapters. A router composes the resulting experts. The design reduces interference by avoiding a single dense parameter update path for all heterogeneous skills.19:T69a,The method combines paired task synthesis with two-stage post-training. The authors distinguish two safety settings. In environment-risk tasks, the user goal is benign but the interface contains hazards; the correct behavior is safe continuation, not refusal. In explicit-misuse tasks, the goal itself is harmful and should be refused. This distinction is central, because collapsing both into a single refuse/not-refuse label would push the model toward conservative shutdown behavior.

To support this, they introduce SCOPE-Gen, an automated pipeline that starts from normal GUI tasks and injects detectable forbidden events or risky interface elements while preserving the original user goal and completion evaluator. This produces paired tasks and three complementary trajectory types: capability trajectories for ordinary execution, risk-handling trajectories for safe continuation under environmental hazards, and refusal trajectories for harmful goals or no-safe-path cases. The reward is defined as the product of completion reward and safety reward, so full credit requires both successful completion and hazard avoidance.

Training proceeds in two stages. First, joint SFT teaches the model all three behaviors together, establishing a conditional execution policy. Second, capability-oriented online RL further improves task completion while monitoring whether safety is retained. The design choice is deliberate: use supervised data to shape the decision boundary first, then use RL to push capability, rather than expecting RL to discover the right safety-capability tradeoff from scratch. The instantiated model is based on Qwen3.5-9B, and the trajectory dataset is SATraj-OS.1a:T510,The main evaluations are on OSWorld for capability and OS-BLIND for attack avoidance. The headline numbers are clear. Capability-only training improves execution but does not reliably induce attack avoidance. After joint capability-safety SFT, SCOPE-SFT reaches 49.72% on OSWorld and 66.30% attack-avoidance on OS-BLIND, with a capability-safety harmonic mean of 56.83%. After subsequent capability-oriented online RL, SCOPE-RL improves OSWorld to 54.17% while retaining 64.30% attack avoidance, yielding the best aggregate score of 58.80%.

The ablations are the most informative part. Refusal trajectories account for most of the gain in attack avoidance on OS-BLIND, meaning that explicit supervision is effective for teaching when to stop. Risk-handling trajectories play a different role: they preserve task utility at comparable attack-avoidance levels by teaching the model to continue safely instead of over-refusing. Together, the two trajectory types produce a more balanced conditional policy.

The broader takeaway from the training path—capability-only, then joint SFT, then capability-oriented RL—is that safety does not emerge automatically from capability optimization, but once a safety-aware boundary is learned, further capability improvement does not necessarily erase it.1b:T4bf,ArenaFlow starts with tournament-based relative ranking. For each query, the policy samples a group of trajectories. A seeded single-elimination tournament compares candidates, with a greedy decoding trajectory used as an anchor for the initial ranking prior. The judge does more than choose the better trajectory. It also marks pivotal success steps, extracts strategy-like skills, and attributes whether retrieved historical skills contributed to decisive reasoning. The framework then propagates credit at two levels. For step-level optimization, tournament survival depth is used as a confidence signal: pivotal steps that support wins in later rounds receive higher weight because they have survived comparison against stronger candidates. Depth-aware weighting and intra-trajectory normalization convert trajectory-level advantages into more targeted step-level training signals. For skill-level propagation, a global memory stores candidate skills. Their utility is estimated from group-level usage attribution; skills repeatedly used in pivotal success steps are reinforced, while stale or ineffective ones are pruned. During later rollouts, high-utility skills are retrieved as priors to guide exploration.1c:T444,The paper first defines a Reward-Resolution Protocol to separate unlearnable numerical jitter from credible cardinal gaps. It uses bounded binning, a dead zone, and zero-gap skipping. Gaps below the trusted reward resolution are merged before group-relative updates. If a group has no credible gap, both reward and KL updates are skipped for that prompt, preventing noise from being written into the policy. The main operator is MaxNorm-AC, a minimal intervention that only replaces the within-group scale denominator. It uses the maximum absolute raw advantage, combined with a reward-resolution floor, as the denominator, and freezes the resulting loss weight for the current optimization step. This preserves credible cardinal gaps while bounding reward amplification, the effective-KL imbalance, and the tail of prompt-level batch weights. MaxNorm-RLOO is one instance that attaches this bounded-scale operator to the RLOO numerator. KL regularization, policy ratios, clipping, token aggregation, and length normalization are left unchanged, which makes the mechanism easier to attribute.1d:T44d,The authors build AMPLE-Math, a controlled supervision suite derived from OpenThoughts-114k. It contains 5,319 math problems. Each problem has one verified answer and six answer-matched views, ranging from Answer Only to Full Trace. This design holds answer information fixed while varying the form and amount of reasoning representation. In training, the student sees only the problem and generates direct-response rollouts. A frozen copy of the same model acts as the teacher; it sees the problem, the selected privileged view, and the student prefix, then produces a next-token distribution under a thinking-enabled configuration. The loss is a teacher-to-student forward KL with per-vocabulary-term clipping at 0.05, following the gKL variant used in prior OPSD work. The reference-free baseline removes the privileged view but keeps the thinking-enabled teacher and direct-response student. The paper also profiles teacher scores on student responses, including correctness alignment and pressure on correction markers, then checks whether apparent intervention gains survive checkpoint matching.1e:T76d,The main contribution is to treat EOS handling as a semantic alignment problem in the training objective, not merely a decoding-interface issue. The paper studies sampled-token OPD, where the student samples y_t at prefix s_t and the teacher scores only that sampled token. Contributions are averaged over valid generated tokens, without reward-to-go. In this setup, if the student samples its native EOS e* but the teacher prefers another functionally equivalent EOS, the student is explicitly pushed away from its own stopping action.

To formalize this, the paper defines an equivalence set of termination tokens, E_EOS, under the rollout protocol. It then compares four termination-handling strategies. The provided text does not list all four in full detail, but the key contrast is clear:
1. aligning the decoding stopping set alone;
2. aligning termination inside the distillation signal;
3. aggregating functionally equivalent EOS tokens into a shared semantic stopping action.

The main finding is that decoding alignment alone is insufficient. The problem is not just when rollout halts, but how the training signal evaluates the act of stopping. The effective correction is to aggregate equivalent EOS tokens semantically, so that any valid stop token is rewarded as the same action rather than treated as competing surface forms.

A second strong component is the stage-wise analysis using K2-Horizon checkpoints spanning pretraining, midtraining, SFT, and final post-training. This shows that EOS preference is not a static tokenizer artifact; it shifts across training stages. The main challenge is not the existence of labels, but how to make those labels valid. The paper identifies three constraints. First, supervision must be applied under rollout-consistent draft-conditioned states. The k-th drafted token is predicted conditioned on previous drafted tokens, not on the final executed prefix after verification. To avoid train-test mismatch, GrowMTP records the draft trajectory and reconstructs the hidden states corresponding to the actual draft-conditioned context that produced each proposal. Second, learning across drafting depths is coupled. A deeper token only matters if earlier drafted tokens are accepted, so depth-wise training should reflect that dependency rather than treating each position as an independent classification target. The paper introduces a depth-coupled acceptance (DCA) loss based on an acceptance-chain surrogate, with a logarithmic transform to increase relative gradient weight on low-chain-value cases. Third, supervision beyond the first rejection is invalid for execution, because deeper drafted tokens are conditioned on a rejected token and therefore lie off the executed prefix. GrowMTP gates the loss after the first rejection and excludes those suffix positions. A further design choice is to detach draft-head updates from the policy backbone, so the online drafting module does not directly perturb policy learning. This sacrifices possible gains from joint optimization, but keeps the method modular and stable. An important practical point is that no offline warm start or depth curriculum is required: the first draft position always yields valid supervision, so training can begin from random initialization.24:T4a3,The headline result is from-scratch online growth on a headless model.
