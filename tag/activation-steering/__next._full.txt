18:T78d,The method has four stages.

1) Direction estimation. For each layer, the model collects hidden states from reflective and non-reflective examples and computes a standard mean-difference vector as an initial reflection direction. This follows the usual activation-engineering assumption that a behavior can be approximated by a linear direction in residual space.

2) Direction purification. This is the key technical step. The authors argue that the raw contrast direction mixes reflection with activation structure shared by reasoning states more broadly. They therefore apply PCA-based denoising to reduce sample noise and low-value variation, then orthogonalize the candidate reflection direction against a rank-one proxy for shared reasoning structure. A central empirical claim is that the raw direction has substantial overlap with this shared proxy, while the orthogonalized direction reduces that overlap to roughly an isotropic-random baseline.

3) Layer calibration. The method does not assume every layer is a good intervention site. On a small calibration set, it sweeps candidate layers and intervention strengths, then keeps the layers that consistently reduce reflection-like behavior without destabilizing generation quality.

4) State-dependent projection attenuation. Instead of adding or subtracting a fixed vector at every token, the method removes only the component of the current hidden state that lies along the learned reflection direction, with bounded magnitude. This makes the intervention conditional on how much reflection-associated signal is present in the current token state. Compared with constant shifts, this is a more selective suppression mechanism and is less likely to damage ordinary reasoning.

The design choice is deliberate: no retraining, no controller network, no modified decoding. The trade-off is that it needs a small reflective/non-reflective contrast set and a calibration pass.19:T6e1,The evaluation covers three open-weight Qwen-family checkpoints on two benchmarks, MATH-500 and GPQA-Diamond, with matched answer parsing and token accounting. The main comparison is against two closely related representation-level baselines, CREST and ReflCtrl.

Across six model-benchmark settings, Reflection Steering reduces reasoning tokens by 16.9% on average while largely preserving task performance. The cleanest result is on Qwen3-30B-A3B for MATH-500: a 21.8% token reduction with only a -0.1 point change in accuracy. The paper also reports paired equivalence testing and finds support for equivalence to the raw model within a ±1 point margin, which is stronger evidence than simply reporting a small average drop.

The paper’s own framing makes clear that the strongest accuracy-preserving evidence is on mathematical reasoning. On GPQA-Diamond, the token savings remain meaningful, but the trade-off is less clean. That makes sense: in math, reflection often appears as repeated verification after the solution path is already established, whereas in scientific reasoning, revision and reconsideration may be more tightly coupled to useful inference.

The ablations that matter are: (i) raw mean-difference direction versus purified direction, showing that orthogonalization against shared reasoning structure materially improves the accuracy-efficiency trade-off; (ii) fixed vector shifts versus projection attenuation, where the latter better preserves accuracy; and (iii) layer calibration, showing that intervention quality depends strongly on depth. This is not a paper about raising benchmark accuracy. Standard contrastive activation addition often compares conforming and nonconforming forced answers under the same nudged prompt. That direction can mix sycophancy with answer identity and option-position artifacts. THESIS-MoE instead builds matched nudged and no-nudge prompts for the same question. Holding the forced answer fixed, it compares activations with and without the user’s stated belief. The resulting difference estimates a belief-induced shift. The provided full-content excerpt does not include exact model names, parameter counts, or benchmark deltas.1e:T776,The setup is simple but careful. The authors freeze model weights and intervene only at inference time. They study Qwen 3.5-2B (24 layers, d=2048) and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (16 layers, d=2048).

The language axis is built layer by layer. First, they collect hidden states from 20k held-out bilingual sentence pairs and subtract the layerwise mean. Second, they run PCA on the centered activations and keep the top 150 components. Third, instead of taking PC1, they score each principal component by Cohen’s d between the two language distributions and select the component with the largest absolute separation, orienting it so the positive direction corresponds to the target language. This gives one unit vector per layer. Compared with a raw mean-difference vector or a probe weight, this procedure constrains the search to high-variance subspaces and then explicitly selects the component that best separates the language pair.

They then perform two intervention types. Steering adds a scaled offset along the language axis during the forward pass. Ablation removes the projection onto that axis. Steering tests sufficiency: can the model be pushed into another language? Ablation tests necessity: does removing the axis impair the original language behavior? The paper also includes strong controls, especially equal-magnitude random perturbations, to rule out the trivial explanation that any large perturbation can flip the output.

Two technical findings stand out. First, the geometry is pair-specific rather than a single universal “language direction”: English-Chinese and English-Spanish require distinct axes. Second, the effect is strongly layer-dependent. Mid layers often resist steering, while later layers are more sensitive, suggesting that language realization is not decided only at the final logits, but surface-form commitment becomes much easier to alter in later representations.1f:T58f,The experimental sweep is fairly large for an activation intervention paper. The main evaluation uses FLORES-200, with 500 English-Chinese pairs and 100 English-Spanish pairs. Axis calibration uses 20k held-out sentence pairs from MultiUN. In total, the study reports 1.26 million generations.

The main results are qualitative but consistent. Steering along the PCA+Cohen’s d language axis reliably induces language switching in both cross-script and same-script settings, while random perturbations of the same magnitude generally do not. The key empirical finding is that raw susceptibility is not a clean reasoning signal because it is strongly coupled to prompt length. In many programmatic and algorithmic tasks, harder examples are simply longer, and the raw response attenuates with length.

To address this, the paper introduces length-controlled susceptibility. Instead of using the raw response, it compares each example to the expected response among inputs of similar length and uses the residual as a corrected activation signal. A positive residual indicates stronger-than-expected internal activation given the length; a negative residual suggests under-activation. SPARK then combines this corrected signal with cross-layer coordination to separate reasoning-active anchors from under-activated hard targets.

The steering stage extracts a reasoning direction from the active anchors, representing the latent shift from a baseline state to an active reasoning state. That direction is injected only into selected layers and only for the under-activated hard examples. This is the main methodological distinction from generic activation steering: intervention is diagnosis-guided, example-selective, and derived from active reasoning cases rather than from a global handcrafted direction.
