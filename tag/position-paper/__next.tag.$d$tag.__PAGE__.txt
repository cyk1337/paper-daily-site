5:T99e,There is no new model or training recipe here, but the argument is structured carefully. The paper starts by criticizing the “fix it in post” mindset: when undesirable behavior appears, the default response is finetuning, RLHF, or input/output filtering, rather than asking how the behavior was produced during pretraining or intermediate training. The authors use examples such as brittle safety finetuning and superficial bias mitigation to argue that post-hoc interventions often change outputs without addressing the underlying causal mechanism.

The paper then borrows from Kuhn’s criteria for scientific theories and asks what a science of AI should look like. The answer is not just better benchmark optimization. A scientific account should support empirical prediction, internal consistency across optimization/data/architecture/representation changes, compatibility with adjacent theory, broad scope beyond one benchmark, simplicity where possible, and the ability to generate new experiments.

The most actionable part is the three-level agenda around training dynamics. First is prediction: using early training signals to forecast later capabilities, failure modes, and alignment properties, not only final loss. This points toward studying gradient statistics, representation geometry, sample learning order, subdistribution absorption rates, and the timing of capability emergence. Second is intervention: if a trajectory is going wrong, one should be able to steer it through data mixture changes, sampling schedules, optimizer settings, regularization, or stage transitions. Third is design: replacing large amounts of trial-and-error with training procedures that are intentionally constructed to yield target properties.

Methodologically, the paper argues for longitudinal measurement rather than endpoint-only evaluation. That means linking checkpoints, internal states, behavior, and exposure history across training time. This stands in contrast to much of mechanistic interpretability, which often studies final models, and much of alignment work, which often studies post-trained behavior without tracing its developmental origin. The paper implicitly calls for stronger experimental design as well: controlled data-mixture studies, repeated runs to estimate trajectory variance, sample-level learning event logging, and tests of whether post-training actually changes internal representations rather than only decision boundaries.6:T7d2,This is a position paper, but it is structured as a disciplined methodological argument rather than a loose essay. Its contribution has three layers.

First, it separates the objects. The paper defines S as surface CoT, the explicit natural-language intermediate trace; Z as task-relevant latent-state trajectories, meaning hidden-state paths that carry intermediate commitments during multi-step inference; and B as generic serial compute, meaning extra iterative budget for search, looping, sampling, or repeated computation without assuming any privileged representational form. This decomposition matters because many existing experiments move S and B together, or Z and B together, making causal interpretation weak.

Second, it formalizes three hypotheses. H2 says reasoning is primarily mediated by explicit surface CoT. H0 says most apparent reasoning gains are better explained by extra serial compute than by any privileged representational object. H1 says reasoning is primarily mediated by latent-state trajectories, with surface CoT acting only as a partial interface that may sometimes report latent commitments and sometimes perturb them. The key evaluative principle is causal leverage: after controlling the other factors, which object provides the strongest and most stable explanatory and interventional handle?

Third, the paper proposes an adjudication program. It outlines a two-tier framework: a controlled regime matrix that compares surface, latent, and compute families under audited budgets, and a naturalistic transport suite that tests whether conclusions survive in more realistic settings. The authors also discuss boundary regimes carefully. H2 may re-emerge when visible traces are constitutive of the task itself, and H0 may dominate when gains are driven mainly by larger search or sampling budgets. This makes the paper more precise than a blanket “latent is everything” claim; the recommendation is a default for ordinary reasoning regimes, not a universal law.7:T75f,The paper is primarily a synthesis and adjudication argument, not a standard empirical training paper. It does not present a new large-scale model with fresh benchmark tables in the usual sense. Instead, it reorganizes evidence from recent work on mechanistic interpretability, latent reasoning, CoT faithfulness, and test-time scaling, and asks which hypothesis that evidence supports.

The core argument runs as follows. First, the case for H2 is weaker than often assumed: ordinary CoT can improve performance, but it is not reliably faithful, and its role varies sharply across tasks. If surface CoT were the main reasoning object, it should provide more stable causal control and explanation. Second, H0 is also insufficient as a default explanation: extra budget alone does not account for results showing that specific internal states, features, or trajectories can predict, monitor, or alter reasoning behavior. If generic compute were the whole story, such latent-state interventions should not matter so much. Third, the current body of work on latent monitoring and latent reasoning fits H1 better: task-relevant commitments can form in hidden-state dynamics before, beyond, or partly independent of what is verbalized on the surface.

One of the paper’s most useful contributions is procedural: future reasoning evaluations should explicitly audit compute budgets and disentangle surface traces, latent states, and serial compute instead of letting them vary together. The line in the paper that “the resulting evidence shows that H1 receives the strongest support in ordinary regimes” should be read as a synthesis of existing evidence, not as a single new benchmark result. So readers should not expect the usual tables of parameter counts, dataset sizes, or exact percentage gains; the main payload is the argument structure and the proposed experimental discipline.8:T4fb,Read this paper as a methodological reset for reasoning research. Start with the Introduction and Section 2 to understand the S/Z/B decomposition and why the authors think current literature often confounds these objects. Then spend most of your time on Section 3, where H1, H2, and H0 are compared as competing explanations. After that, read Sections 4 and 5 carefully, because they contain the most operational content: how future experiments should be designed, what a controlled regime matrix should look like, and how a naturalistic transport suite could test external validity. Section 6 is useful for positioning the paper relative to adjacent surveys and empirical work.

A serious skim takes about 30 minutes. The most important thing to extract is not the headline claim, but the causal-leverage criterion: after controlling the other variables, is the strongest explanatory and interventional handle in the visible trace, the latent trajectory, or the compute budget? For pretraining researchers, two follow-up questions are especially relevant: whether reasoning supervision should target latent processes rather than only verbalized CoT, and whether future reasoning benchmarks should bake compute auditing and representational disentanglement into the
