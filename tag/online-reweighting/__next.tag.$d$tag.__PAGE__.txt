9:T752,The proposed method, ADAPT, is a validation-guided online reweighting framework for both pretraining and fine-tuning. The paper starts from a general scoring function s(x; θ, D_val), where x is a training example, θ is a model state or proxy model, and D_val is a validation set representing the target distribution. In offline curation, this score is computed once before training and converted into fixed weights or a selected subset. ADAPT instead updates the signal during training and uses it to assign continuous per-sample loss weights.

The key design choice is that weighting is driven by alignment with the validation set rather than by raw training loss alone. That is an important distinction. Loss-based online curricula often overemphasize hard examples, but high loss can come from noise, corruption, or out-of-domain content. ADAPT tries to estimate whether a sample is useful for the target distribution, not merely whether it is difficult. Operationally, the method acts like an adaptive per-sample step size: all data remain in the corpus, but their gradient contribution is scaled according to their current relevance.

Another useful aspect is the paper’s unified view of prior work. Offline selection becomes a 0/1 weighting scheme. Domain mixing becomes grouped weighting with fixed or slowly varying coefficients. Balancing methods are also reinterpreted as constrained reweighting. ADAPT differs along three axes: it is online rather than precomputed, sample-level rather than only domain-level, and soft rather than hard. Those choices explain why it can preserve diversity while still steering optimization toward the validation distribution. The paper also emphasizes that the method is global rather than merely batch-local, and that it adds almost no extra overhead because it does not require explicit resampling or rebuilding the dataset.a:T6f6,The experimental section is strong mainly because it evaluates the method under a unified protocol and across both pretraining and instruction tuning. The headline result is up to 7.2% higher accuracy per FLOP than offline baselines such as LESS, DoReMi, and RegMix, as well as prior online reweighting methods. It also reports up to 6.1% better out-of-domain generalization across benchmarks. Accuracy-per-FLOP is the right lens here, since an online method should be judged not only by final accuracy but also by whether it avoids the repeated proxy-score-and-retrain cycle of offline curation.

The experiments support several concrete claims. First, online soft weighting is more robust than offline hard filtering. Offline methods can look good on the validation target they optimize for, but they are more brittle once the evaluation distribution shifts, because they have already discarded part of the training support. Second, retaining the full dataset helps generalization. Weakly relevant or long-tail examples are not uniformly useless; their value changes over training. Third, dynamic weights outperform static weights, which directly supports the paper’s premise that sample importance is not fixed. Fourth, the gains transfer from pretraining to instruction tuning, suggesting that the method is not just a corpus-cleaning trick but a more general data allocation mechanism.

The ablations appear to focus on the right questions: sample-level versus group-level weighting, dynamic versus static weights, the choice of similarity or quality signal, update frequency, and the effect of keeping the full corpus versus filtering. The main takeaway is that the benefit comes from dynamic, fine-grained, continuous reweighting rather than from a particular scorer alone.b:T578,Start with the introduction and the unified formalization section.
