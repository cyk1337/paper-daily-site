c:T5a9,The method, FINEVERIFY, has a proposer-verifier structure. A proposer first runs agentic search and samples multiple trajectories, producing a set of candidate answers. The verifier does not assign one global score per candidate. Instead, it first decomposes the original question into a set of checkable sub-questions, each corresponding to a requirement, attribute, or constraint in the task. Then, for every candidate answer, it verifies each sub-question against retrieved evidence and labels the result as supported, not_found, or contradicted. These per-check judgments are aggregated into an evidence-grounded verification score, and the highest-scoring candidate is selected.

Relative to best-of-N or confidence-based reranking, the design changes the problem in three ways. First, it replaces one difficult global judgment with multiple simpler local judgments. Second, all candidates are evaluated against the same explicit checklist, which makes scores more comparable across candidates. Third, the aggregation rule is explicit rather than implicit, so the final decision is inspectable.

The paper also uses a practical early-stopping rule: if a candidate receives full support on all sub-questions, sampling can stop because further compute is unlikely to change the decision. Another useful byproduct is the verification trace itself, which can be used to audit benchmark quality and diagnose why a candidate was accepted or rejected.d:T57f,The evaluation covers four agentic search benchmarks and two models. With only 4 sampled trajectories, FINEVERIFY improves average accuracy for GPT-5-mini from 59.2% Pass@1 to 67.4%, an absolute gain of 8.2 points. For Gemini-3-flash, it improves from 71.3% to 76.9%, a gain of 5.6 points. It consistently beats standard test-time scaling baselines such as Majority Voting, Weighted Voting, Best-of-N, Solution Aggregation, and Confidence Verify.

The more important result is that it keeps improving as the number of samples grows. On BrowseComp-Plus, Figure 1 shows that with 12 samples, FINEVERIFY allows GPT-5-mini to surpass GPT-5, with a 20.5-point gain over GPT-5-mini Pass@1. That matters because it indicates the method is not just a small reranking trick at low sample counts; it converts additional test-time compute into accuracy more effectively than the baselines.

The paper also includes a verification-accuracy analysis: when the correct answer is present in the candidate pool, FINEVERIFY is more likely than coarse candidate-level scoring methods to actually select it. The goal is not to answer the query directly, but to discover what a good answer should contain: domain facts, structural constraints, likely omissions, and typical failure patterns. The interesting training dynamic is that the first bootstrap step tends to specialize the model toward reasoning, while later bootstrap rounds partially restore broader agentic performance.10:T80b,The central idea is an answer-grounded graph-progress prior. The authors view world knowledge as a latent world graph and each information-seeking task as a query-conditioned latent task graph whose terminal node is the answer. A good intermediate step should move the agent closer to that answer node. Since the true latent graph is unavailable, they build a training-time Entity-Relation graph for each QA instance as a proxy, with entities as nodes and semantic or relational links as edges.

On top of that graph, they define Graph-Distance Contribution Reward, or GDCR. The reward has two components. One scores newly retrieved entities at the current step; the other scores newly cited entities that appear in the current thought. This distinction matters because retrieval alone is not enough: an entity may be fetched but never used in subsequent reasoning. Each entity receives a contribution score based on its shortest-path distance to the answer node, with closer entities receiving higher reward. The intuition is simple: graph distance acts as a proxy for whether the step makes meaningful progress toward the answer.

They then introduce Step Advantage Policy Optimization, SAPO. SAPO normalizes and bounds GDCR within each trajectory, then combines trajectory-level outcome advantages with GDCR-based step advantages during policy optimization. This preserves the final-answer objective while giving denser reinforcement to critical intermediate behaviors. Compared with tree-sampling methods such as ARPO, SAPO avoids expanding many continuations from each step, which is where the efficiency gain comes from.

The assumptions are important. The method needs answer supervision during training, a reasonably good ER graph, and reliable entity extraction plus citation detection. It is also better suited to fact-centric multi-hop search where progress can be represented as movement toward an answer entity. For tasks requiring abstract planning or where the answer is not naturally an entity node, shortest-path distance may be a weak proxy.11:T618,The paper evaluates SAPO on four challenging benchmarks and compares it against trajectory-level RL baselines as well as ARPO, a representative tree-sampling step-level method. The headline result stated in the paper is that SAPO outperforms ARPO in both performance and training efficiency. The key point is less about one benchmark number and more about the demonstration that a cheap graph-based proxy can recover much of the benefit of expensive step-level rollout estimation.

The analysis also supports the need for both reward components. The authors note that some retrieved entities are answer-relevant but never explicitly cited later, which means they do not actually contribute to the final reasoning chain. This motivates rewarding newly cited entities separately from newly retrieved ones. In other words, the method does not only reward retrieval recall; it rewards retrieval that gets incorporated into reasoning.
