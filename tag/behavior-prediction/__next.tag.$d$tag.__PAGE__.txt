9:T44f,The protocol represents each behavioral evaluation as a set of conditions with measured rates. A condition may be an MMLU subject in a sycophancy test, a decision-topic and demographic cell in a discrimination evaluation, or an agentic scenario. A prediction method outputs one rate per condition, and performance is scored by correlation and calibration on frozen splits. The paper varies three axes independently: information available to the model, from abstract descriptions to exact items and behavior history; the subject of the prompt, including “you,” a named model, and “capable AI agents in general”; and the question format, either direct rate prediction or forced choice. The crucial design choice is the subject control. If self-directed prompts perform no better than generic-agent prompts, positive correlations cannot be interpreted as privileged self-knowledge. Additional controls include noise ceilings, cross-model consensus, predictions from other models’ measured behavior, other models’ answers about themselves, identity transfer, and re-elicitation after finetuning.a:T599,The study covers nine public behavioral evaluations, 15 prediction methods, and 12 models from six labs, including frontier models with and without reasoning. Direct self-report has almost no predictive power for the reporting model’s behavior, with r=+0.04. Showing the model its own measured history raises this to +0.19, and showing exact evaluation items raises it to +0.24. However, the same item-informed question about capable AI agents in general reaches +0.28, and other models’ self-reports predict the target model at least as well as the target model’s own answers, with +0.35 versus +0.24 in the reported comparison. Each model’s answers fit other models’ behavior nearly as well as its own; the self-advantage is only +0.02. The paper also estimates how much reliable variance is model-specific: about 0.04 for capability-style evaluations, where models mostly agree on what is hard, and about 0.97 for agentic policy evaluations, where models differ more. Scaling improves predictions about AI assistants in general, but not identification of the model’s own tendencies. Naming the model induces a self-flattering shift: on harmful behaviors, self-reports are about 0.15 lower than generic-agent reports.
