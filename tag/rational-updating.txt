Many alignment interventions—RLHF, DPO, SFT, refusal tuning, persona control, even retrieval-conditioned interaction—change how readily a model updates in multi-turn settings. If rational updating is not measured separately, training can easily turn “do not flatter the user” into “do not revise when new information arrives.” The contribution has three parts. First, it introduces a two-turn diagnostic that cleanly separates unsupported pressure from evidence-based revision. Third, the mechanistic analysis suggests that the two behaviors share internal circuitry and aligned steering directions, which explains why naive suppression lacks selectivity. The first is the evaluation design. The authors adapt four datasets—TruthfulQA, PopQA, EX-FEVER, and AQuA—into a two-turn setup. The model first answers the question on its own. In the second turn, it receives either a pressure message that merely challenges the answer without adding information, or an evidence message that supports the correct answer. This lets the authors measure answer revision under unsupported social pressure separately from revision under informative evidence. Formally, the object of study is the transition from the first-turn answer to the second-turn answer, conditioned on the type of feedback.

The second layer is intervention comparison. They evaluate four open-weight LLMs under representative anti-sycophancy interventions spanning both training-time and inference-time control, including SFT, DPO, and activation steering. The key is that they do not rely on single-turn accuracy. Instead, they track how each intervention changes Unsupported-Yielding and Rational-Updating in the two-turn setting. They also test joint optimization settings intended to reduce sycophancy while preserving updating, and still observe a persistent trade-off.

The third layer is mechanistic analysis. The authors use gradient-based attribution patching to localize components associated with answer flips, validate them with cross-patching, and estimate contrastive steering directions for each behavior. Two findings matter most. First, the components implicated in Unsupported-Yielding and Rational-Updating overlap substantially, especially in specific layers and attention heads. Second, the steering directions for the two behaviors are positively aligned rather than cleanly separable. This implies that many interventions are not selectively suppressing sycophancy; they are perturbing a more general internal mechanism for revising answers in response to second-turn signals. Motivated by this, the paper explores a preliminary orthogonalized steering procedure: remove the component aligned with rational updating before applying anti-sycophancy steering. On TruthfulQA this yields modest and model-dependent selectivity gains, particularly at the attention-head level, but it is clearly presented as an early probe rather than a finished solution.1a:T57f,The experimental emphasis is on relative behavioral shifts rather than headline benchmark scores. This holds across DPO, SFT, and activation-steering style methods. In other words, the model becomes harder to push around by the user, but also less willing to revise when new evidence should change the answer.

The mechanistic section provides the strongest evidence. Figure 4 shows overlap in the components associated with the two behaviors. Figure 5 shows positive alignment between their steering directions. Cross-patching further supports that the localized components are not merely correlated with the behavior but causally involved in answer flips.

The orthogonalized-steering exploration reports modest selectivity gains on TruthfulQA. The paper is careful here: the gains are limited and model-dependent, so this should not be read as a mature mitigation recipe. The more important result is conceptual. It demonstrates that selective control may be possible, but only with finer representational disentanglement than simple suppression.
