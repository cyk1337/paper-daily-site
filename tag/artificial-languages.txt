19:T7a7,The contribution is primarily experimental, but the design is careful. The authors use two families of artificial languages: k-Dyck, which encodes strictly nested dependencies, and k-Shuffle Dyck, which relaxes well-nestedness and allows cross-serial dependencies. They test k=64 and k=128. Bracket symbols are represented as integers, and this turns out to be important because the two tokenizers treat those strings very differently: the Llama tokenizer tends to preserve multi-digit numbers more compactly, while the Gemma tokenizer often splits them into individual digits. That changes the effective surface statistics of the artificial corpus and likely changes what the model actually learns during the pre-pretraining phase.

On the natural-language side, they study English, Albanian, Czech, Danish, Dutch, and Finnish. Training data is a monolingual multi-domain mixture of FineWeb/FineWeb2, OpenSubtitles, and EUBookshop in an 8:1:1 ratio. The artificial-language phase is fixed at 16,000 sequences of length 2,048, i.e. 500 steps with batch size 32. The natural-language phase is fixed at 320,000 sequences of length 2,048, i.e. 10,000 steps with batch size 32. Models are three Llama-3-based sizes paired with two tokenizers, yielding six model variants overall, roughly spanning the low-hundreds-of-millions to sub-billion range.

The main metric follows prior work: token-efficiency gain, defined as how many natural-language training tokens earlier the pretrained model reaches the same validation loss as the corresponding baseline. Negative values indicate harm. Crucially, each setup is run with three random seeds, and the paper also correlates gains with quantified linguistic properties such as sentence length, morphological richness, dependency-tree depth, branching, and crossing dependencies. The technical novelty is not a new algorithm; it is a systematic decomposition of a previously positive result into setup-dependent effects.1a:T837,Four findings matter most.

First, variance is high, and not only in fringe settings. Across both small and larger models, identical configurations can produce noticeably different token-efficiency gains under different seeds. This directly limits the claim that the method reliably saves training tokens.

Second, the earlier English result is broadly reproducible for the smallest models. The paper states that, in English, most artificial-language pretraining settings improve the smallest model, with 64-Shuffle Dyck as the main exception. So the prior observation is not invalid, but its scope is narrower than one might infer from English-only evidence.

Third, the most stable positive case is a small model with the Llama tokenizer pretrained on 128-Dyck. The paper repeatedly highlights the 154M+LT+128-Dyck setup as consistently beneficial. By contrast, Gemma-tokenized models are weaker on average and less stable, which strongly suggests that tokenization is not a minor implementation detail here; it is a major determinant of whether the artificial-language signal is preserved in a learnable form.

Fourth, Dyck is usually more reliable than Shuffle Dyck, at least for the Llama-tokenizer models. A plausible interpretation is that strict hierarchical closure provides a cleaner training signal for attention and state tracking than the more permissive crossing-dependency variant, which may be harder and noisier under the tested tokenization and scale regime.

In terms of scale, the artificial-language phase is about 16,000×2,048 ≈ 32.8M tokens, followed by roughly 320,000×2,048 ≈ 655M natural-language tokens. The authors note that these are not Chinchilla-optimal token/parameter ratios, but the loss curves are stable in the final ~3,000 steps, so the relative ordering between baseline and pretrained runs is unlikely to flip with more training. The paper also includes additional experiments on unstable setups and attempts to relate gains to linguistic properties. Those analyses explain some trends, but not enough to turn the method into a predictable or robust recipe.1b:T51b,Start with the Introduction and Figure 1 to get the main message right: the paper is not saying the method never works; it is saying the effect is conditional and high-variance. Then read the dataset and setup sections carefully, especially how the artificial languages are encoded as number strings, how LT and GT tokenize those strings differently, and how small the artificial-language phase is relative to the natural-language phase.

Next, inspect the main multi-seed result plots for the smallest models, then the larger-model plots in the appendix. The key question is not the mean gain alone, but whether run-to-run variance is already comparable to or larger than the average benefit. Also pay attention to the highlighted stable case, 154M+LT+128-Dyck, because it shows the phenomenon is real in at least one regime.

For a 20-30 minute skim, use this order: Introduction → setup tables → Figure 1/3/4 → English results table → appendix plots on tokenizer differences and larger models.
