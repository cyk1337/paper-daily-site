9:T8b7,The central idea is environment evolution. The paper starts from a multi-turn learning objective, models agent-environment interaction as a Markov process, and then introduces a higher-level decomposition of execution into alternating scenarios and skills. From this view, environment difficulty is decomposed into three factors: scenario novelty, skill rarity, and execution length. Intuitively, an environment becomes harder when it requires the agent to enter less common scenarios, apply rarer skills or skill combinations, and sustain a longer meaningful execution chain.

A key step is to move from model-dependent negative log-likelihood to a policy-independent notion of difficulty under a reference distribution T. This is meant to separate environment difficulty from model weakness. In other words, the authors do not want to define an environment as difficult merely because the current policy fails on it. With this formulation, environment generation no longer needs to depend on on-policy rollouts.

The implementation uses a loop-engineered multi-agent harness that incrementally modifies a seed environment along one of three directions: increasing scenario novelty, increasing skill rarity, or increasing execution length. Each modification must preserve executability, task validity, and the verifier. This produces verified lineages of environments with gradually increasing difficulty. The lineage design matters: instead of generating entirely new environments from scratch, the method performs local, controlled, traceable edits on already verified environments, which makes curriculum construction more manageable. Training then schedules these evolved environments generation by generation so that the policy continues to receive non-saturated learning signals over time.

Compared with co-evolution, the main differences are that the process is off-policy and that difficulty increase is explicit and structured rather than driven by current rollout failures. The trade-off is that the quality of the difficulty definition and the evolution harness becomes central: if those are weak, the system may generate environments that are harder in superficial ways but not actually more learnable or more useful.a:T612,The paper reports 200-step long-horizon RL experiments on Qwen3.6-27B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, environment evolution improves performance by 14.4 and 18.0 percentage points, respectively. The authors also use Hy4 preview, Claude Opus 5, and GPT-5.6 Sol for rollout-based difficulty estimation, showing that the evolved environments remain challenging across different models rather than being overfit to a single generator or policy.

Another important result is that evolved environments continue to provide useful RL signal even after the seed environments have already been used for SFT. That suggests the method is not merely replaying the original distribution, but is actually pushing the environment family into new difficulty regimes. Compared with agent-environment co-evolution and ensemble baselines, the paper claims that off-policy evolution yields longer-lasting learning signals and slower saturation during training.

From an evaluation standpoint, the paper is trying to validate three things: whether the evolved environments are genuinely harder, whether that hardness generalizes across models, and whether the harder environments translate into downstream benchmark gains. For credibility, the most important details are likely in the validation of lineages, preservation of verifiers, and the separate effects of the three evolution directions. Compared to prior open terminal-agent efforts, the novelty is less about a new algorithm and more about a coherent, stability-first training stack paired with harder, taxonomy-controlled environments.e:T7d9,The central object is a scenario-mediated skill graph. Skills are collected from ClawHub and public GitHub repositories, with the intent of grounding the graph in real command-line practice rather than synthetic taxonomies. Each skill is annotated with a precondition scenario, describing when the skill is applicable, and a postcondition scenario, describing the state after execution. Semantically compatible scenarios are then linked, producing a directed graph where scenarios are nodes and skills are transitions.

A sampled path in this graph defines a high-level workflow: a sequence of skills together with the intermediate states they should traverse. The key design choice is to control trajectory structure first and derive concrete tasks second. This is different from prior synthesis methods that start from a task seed and hope the resulting trajectories are diverse. Here, the workflow is the primary object, and the task instance is generated to realize that workflow.

The second major component is a multi-agent harness that turns an abstract workflow into an executable task instance. Different agents generate the environment, formulate the natural-language goal, produce an intended solution, and verify solvability and quality. Two validation layers matter: oracle-based verification ensures the task is actually solvable, and rubric-based evaluation checks task quality, consistency, and alignment with the sampled path. The paper also includes practical graph-construction steps such as scenario deduplication/merging and LLM-verified bridges, which are important because a graph that is too sparse will not support long workflows, while a graph that is too noisy will produce incoherent compositions.

A notable modeling choice is using scenarios as intermediate nodes instead of directly connecting skills to skills. This makes the graph closer to a state-transition abstraction, which is better suited for expressing applicability conditions, compositionality, and future expansion.f:T678,The strongest evidence is in the scale and verification statistics of the synthesis pipeline. The resulting graph contains 57,214 filtered skills, 82,073 deduplicated/merged scenarios, and 185,529 LLM-verified bridges. This is large enough to function as a reusable synthesis substrate rather than a small proof of concept.
