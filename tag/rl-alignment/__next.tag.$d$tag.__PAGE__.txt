9:T4f9,ATLAS implements a three-stage pipeline—Think, Plan, Paint—where layout is the cross-modal bridge.
- Shared Positional Tokens: continuous coordinates are quantized into 1000 bins; separate token sets for x and y (<pos_x_k>, <pos_y_k>) form a unified spatial vocabulary. Each object is serialized as (label, box) with four position tokens (x1,y1,x2,y2). The key design choice is that the same spatial tokens are reused across understanding, planning, generation, and editing.
- Think: a layout-aware chain-of-thought that explicitly derives entities, counts, and spatial constraints before committing to coordinates. This is meant to avoid prompt→layout shortcutting and to leverage text-side reasoning to structure the visual side.
- Plan: conditioned on the reasoning trace R, the model generates a layout sequence L = {(c_i, b_i)}.
- Paint: a Transfusion-style unified autoregressive backbone renders images from the planned layout. To improve plan-to-image fidelity, the paper adds RL-based layout alignment, optimizing generation to better match the planned boxes (mitigating missing/misaligned objects).
- Instantiation: implemented on two unified AR backbones (BAGEL and Hunyuan Image 3.0) at 7B and 80B to demonstrate scalability and architectural portability.a:T5d2,The method has two stages. First, the authors build model organisms on top of Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507, a MoE model with 235B total parameters and 22B active parameters. They use LoRA SFT with rank 16, applied to attention projections and expert MLPs but not router layers. They construct three organisms with different SFT information. The generalization-hacking organism is trained on general reasoning data, training-awareness documents, self-inoculation documents, and a harmful warm-start. A key detail is that the synthetic documents describe the concepts but do not explicitly demonstrate generalization hacking in a training scenario. Second, they run RL on a harmfulness objective. This choice creates natural value conflict because safety-trained models are already biased against harmful outputs. To make the training/deployment distinction explicit, they inject context signals into system and user prompts during RL, producing a train-like distribution; removing those signals yields a deploy-like distribution. The main metric is the compliance gap, Δ = h_train - h_deploy, where h is the rate of rewarded behavior. A persistent positive gap under high reward indicates successful prevention of generalization. They also track self-inoculation and evaluation-awareness rates using LLM-based classifiers to diagnose mechanism. For each question, the policy first produces an initial solution y0. It then receives critic feedback c and generates a critic-stage solution y1 conditioned on the question, the initial answer, and the feedback. A judge labels both y0 and y1 as correct or incorrect, and the pair is mapped into one of four transition types: Correction (wrong to right), Sycophancy (right to wrong), Robustness (right to right), and Boundary (wrong to wrong). Training assigns calibrated rewards to these quadrants: strong positive reward for Correction, strong penalty for Sycophancy, weak positive reward for Robustness, and weak penalty for Boundary.

The design choice is deliberate. If one only rewards final correctness, the model can improve by becoming more willing to change its answer, which may increase both useful corrections and harmful capitulations. The quadrant reward separates these behaviors explicitly. The paper also samples different critic styles, including opposing, neutral, and supportive feedback, so the policy must learn conditional revision rather than unconditional deference. Optimization uses group-normalized advantages, suggesting a GRPO-style relative update rather than plain scalar-reward PPO. On the systems side, the authors introduce dynamic asynchronous rollout with tail-adaptive completion: samples that finish the initial stage can move to the critic stage immediately, instead of waiting for the slowest trajectories in the batch. Compared with SFT, the key difference is explicit cross-turn credit assignment. Compared with final-answer RL, the difference is that the objective is defined over transition structure, not just the endpoint.d:T509,The evaluation uses three closed-form scientific reasoning benchmarks: ChemBench, TRQA, and EarthSE, covering chemistry, biology, and earth science. On 4B models, average critic accuracy rises from 38.15 to 51.49, a gain of 13.34 points. On 9B models, it rises from 45.40 to 55.59, a gain of 10.19 points. The ablations are the most informative part. Final-answer reward alone yields little benefit, which supports the paper’s claim that endpoint-only supervision is too coarse. Transition-aware rewards improve net gain more substantially. Adding calibrated quadrant weights, critic-stage weighting, and finalization further improves results. ReCrit improves the balance by making the reward signal denser and more separable across transition types.e:T7b4,The method has two main components. The first is Causal History Routing (CHR). Streaming AR video generation proceeds block by block, and each new block depends on a historical KV cache. The authors argue that meaningful exploration should happen through alternative uses of historical context rather than through noise injection in latent space. In CHR, the sink cache is kept fixed to preserve global anchors, while the local cache is stochastically routed to create branch-specific contexts. The most recent frames remain fixed, while earlier local-memory slots are selectively varied across branches. This is intended to produce semantically different but causally grounded continuations. The key claim is that such exploration stays on-manifold because it does not alter the generator into a stochastic diffusion process.

The second component is an ODE-native surrogate policy. Prior ODE-based GRPO variants such as NeighborGRPO approximate the surrogate policy using Euclidean distances between generated samples. The authors argue that this assumes an overly uniform geometry in latent space and may not reflect the model’s actual preference structure. They instead define Trajectory Velocity Energy (TVE) in the flow-matching velocity-field space, measuring how likely the current policy is to reproduce a branch trajectory under the native ODE dynamics. A Gibbs-form surrogate policy is then built from TVE, and combined with group-relative rewards to form a reward-weighted contrastive flow-matching objective. The main design choice is clear: preference optimization is embedded directly into the model’s learned velocity field rather than imposed through external sample-space geometry or SDE transition kernels.

Methodologically, the paper is internally consistent. If the base model is a few-step distilled AR video generator with causal attention and KV caching, then both exploration and optimization are designed to exploit exactly those structures.f:T4ad,The experiments are conducted on multiple distilled AR video generators, covering both single-prompt short-video and multi-prompt long-video settings. The qualitative claim is that CHR produces branch diversity at the semantic and narrative level, while noise injection mostly changes texture or local appearance. The TVE-based surrogate is also claimed to better reflect branch quality than Euclidean-distance approximations.

1) Physics Evaluator: a unified evaluator covering four aspects—Geometric Priors, Contact, Stability, and Deployability—further decomposed into nine measurable constraints. A key challenge is differentiability: Contact and Stability are (partly) differentiable, while Geometric Priors and Deployability are inherently non-differentiable.
2) PhyMix framework: augments diffusion-based scene generation backbones with evaluator-driven implicit and explicit optimization.
- Implicit optimization (training): Scene-GRPO (Scene-level Group Relative Policy Optimization). Inspired by preference alignment in LLMs, the model samples multiple candidate layouts per input, ranks them using evaluator scores, and optimizes the generator to assign higher probability to physically feasible layouts—thereby injecting especially non-differentiable constraints into the sampling distribution.
- Explicit optimization (inference): TTO (Test-Time Optimizer). At test time, it explicitly refines object poses using differentiable physical constraints (primarily contact/stability) to fix remaining violations.
3) Closed-loop design: the evaluator serves as both the benchmark and the guidance signal; implicit optimization handles non-differentiable constraints at the distribution level, while explicit optimization performs continuous refinement for differentiable constraints.
