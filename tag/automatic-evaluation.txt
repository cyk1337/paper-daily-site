18:T629,The paper proposes a framework, not a new metric. It starts by defining a taxonomy of behavioral correctness assumptions, then operationalizes each assumption through controlled response transformations. The assumptions are split into two groups.

1. Correctness-preserving assumptions: transformations that should not change correctness with respect to the reference, such as paraphrasing, stylistic variation, or equivalent reformulation. A reliable evaluator should assign similar scores before and after the transformation.
2. Correctness-altering assumptions: transformations that should reduce response quality, such as removing relevant information, adding factual errors, introducing logical inconsistency, or injecting irrelevant content. A reliable evaluator should lower the score accordingly.

Two design choices matter. First, the transformations are intended to isolate one property at a time, so score changes are attributable rather than confounded. Second, the framework is evaluator-agnostic: it can probe lexical, character-level, semantic, LLM-based, and hybrid evaluators under the same behavioral test suite. In that sense, the work is closer to a stress-testing methodology than to a leaderboard.

The paper also studies repeat-run variability, evaluator configuration sensitivity, and test-suite reproducibility. These are especially important for LLM judges, whose outputs can vary with prompts, decoding settings, or model versions. An evaluator that looks good on aggregate but is unstable across runs or configurations is risky as a training signal.19:T445,The empirical study covers a broad range of reference-based evaluators, including lexical overlap methods, character-level methods, semantic similarity methods, LLM-based evaluators, and hybrid systems. The main result is not that one family dominates everywhere, but that assumption-level analysis reveals distinct behavioral profiles that aggregate benchmark scores often hide. Lexical methods tend to be brittle under surface-form changes and therefore violate some correctness-preserving assumptions. Semantic and LLM-based methods are usually more robust to paraphrase, but may be less sensitive to certain correctness-altering transformations or more variable under configuration changes.

The paper also reports stability analyses across repeated runs, evaluator settings, and independently generated test suites. The key takeaway is that the proposed diagnostic framework itself appears reproducible enough to support meaningful evaluator characterization.
