18:T404,This is not a pretraining-method paper, but it matters for foundation-model research in two ways. First, it provides evidence that reliability-relevant structure is encoded in intermediate representations more robustly than in final output statistics, especially under domain shift. That is useful for anyone studying what pretraining actually organizes inside the network beyond next-token likelihood. Second, it turns internal signals into a decision-time uncertainty interface with conformal semantics, rather than stopping at post hoc probing. The contribution is best understood as an interface and measurement paper: it does not change CP theory, and it does not claim formal shift-robust validity, but it shows that better internal scores can materially improve the empirical coverage-efficiency trade-off. For pretraining researchers, the broader implication is that representation quality should also be judged by whether internal states support transferable reliability estimation, not only by final-layer calibration.19:T7e4,The conformal pipeline is standard split conformal prediction. For each calibration example, the model samples M candidate responses, which are grouped into distinct answer units. In multiple-choice QA, this is straightforward option parsing; in open-domain QA, responses are clustered into semantic answer units following prior conformal QA protocols. For each candidate answer a under input x, the method computes an answer-level reliability score F(a; x), and the corresponding nonconformity score is conformalized in the usual way to produce a prediction set.

The novelty lies entirely in how F(a; x) is defined. Instead of using final-token confidence or answer frequency, the paper introduces Layer-Wise Information (LI) scores derived from how conditioning on the input reshapes predictive entropy across model depth. The intuition is simple and plausible: if an answer is genuinely supported by the input, uncertainty about that answer should be progressively reduced as information is integrated through the layers; if the answer is mostly driven by generic language priors or spurious fluency, this entropy reshaping should be weaker or less coherent. LI aggregates this depth-wise information into an answer-level reliability signal, using hidden-state trajectories across sampled candidate answers.

Compared with prior work, the distinction is sharp. API-only conformal methods are limited to black-box output statistics. Sampling-based methods rely on agreement across generations, which can fail when the model is consistently wrong. Shift-aware conformal methods usually need explicit covariates, partitioning schemes, or importance weights to model calibration-test mismatch. This paper does none of that. It keeps the conformal machinery unchanged and swaps in a stronger score. The claim is therefore empirical rather than theoretical: internal scores rank admissible answers better, and the same conformal wrapper then yields better coverage-efficiency behavior, especially under cross-domain shift.1a:T63c,The experiments span closed-ended QA, open-domain QA, and cross-domain QA settings. The main comparison is against output-level uncertainty baselines, including probability/entropy-based scores, API-only uncertainty signals, sampling or self-consistency style methods, and selective-answering baselines. The key result is not raw accuracy but the conformal trade-off: at a fixed target risk level, LI-based conformal prediction tends to achieve smaller prediction sets at similar empirical coverage, or higher coverage at comparable set size. The gains are reported to be most pronounced when calibration and test domains differ.

A central empirical point is that the conformal wrapper itself is unchanged. Replacing only the nonconformity score improves the validity-efficiency curve, which strongly suggests that score quality is the main driver. The method also works in both multiple-choice and open-domain QA, indicating that it is not tied to a fixed label space but to answer-unit abstraction.

That said, the provided full-text excerpt does not expose all the numerical details needed for a faithful table-level summary. Specific values for model size, calibration set size, sampled answer count M, and exact coverage or set-size deltas are not fully visible here. When reading the paper, the most important numbers to extract are: how much LI improves empirical coverage under cross-domain shift, how much prediction sets shrink at matched coverage, whether final-layer-only variants lose most of the gain, and how sensitive the results are to semantic grouping noise in open-domain QA.1b:T51e,Read this as a score-design paper, not as a new conformal theory paper. Start with the end of the introduction and the setup in the method section to lock in that framing.

Then focus on the formal definition of the LI score. That is the real contribution. Check carefully what entropy is computed over, whether the method uses teacher forcing or answer-conditioned scoring, how layer-wise quantities are aligned, and how they are aggregated across depth and across sampled responses. Those details determine whether LI is measuring information gain, representation stabilization, or something closer to answer support.

Next, go straight to the main coverage-versus-set-size plots. Looking at coverage alone is misleading for conformal methods. The paper should be judged by the full validity-efficiency trade-off.

After that, inspect the cross-domain results and the ablations. The cross-domain section is where the paper either justifies its premise or collapses into a mild in-domain improvement. The most informative ablations are likely layer selection, final-layer-only variants, answer aggregation choices, and sensitivity to the number of sampled candidates.
