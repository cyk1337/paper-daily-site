18:T55f,There are three main technical moves. First, the paper formalizes a subgroup-targeted membership inference game. Targets are sampled from a designated pool, while the rest of the private dataset is sampled from its complement. This makes it possible to audit high-risk groups, random controls, or unions of groups under a common reference distribution. Second, the authors adapt normalized-evidence membership inference to synthetic text releases. The exact likelihood-ratio test over an entire released synthetic dataset is intractable, so they replace it with measurable proxy evidence extracted from the release, such as n-gram overlap and embedding-based distances. These proxies are then normalized through scenario-dependent calibration. The separation is important: evidence comes from the release itself, while calibration captures attacker knowledge. Third, attacker capability is modeled explicitly through three release-based scenarios: release-only, raw-reference, and generator-assisted release. This lets the audit distinguish what can be inferred from the released samples alone, what auxiliary reference data adds, and how much stronger attacks become if the generator is also available. The paper also goes beyond pool-level metrics by estimating per-record vulnerability scores from repeated attack episodes and relating them to intrinsic record attributes.19:T522,The study spans four datasets, three DP text generators, five privacy budgets, and 32 proxy attacks. The main empirical result is not simply that DP helps, but that it helps unevenly. For high-risk records under non-private generators, the strongest attacker reaches AUC 0.79–0.83, with 34–61% true-positive rate at under 5% false-positive rate. Even a release-only attacker can recover 39–52% of true members in that setting, which means the released synthetic text alone can carry substantial membership evidence. Under DP, average AUC drops substantially, but leakage remains above chance at every tested privacy budget: AUC stays around 0.55–0.65, with TPR up to about 18% at 5% FPR. The more important pattern is heterogeneity. High-risk pools remain more distinguishable than random controls under every DP budget, so DP does not flatten risk uniformly across groups. Within a pool, leakage is also concentrated: roughly 10% of records account for about 40% of total leakage. That concentration is the most actionable finding, because it suggests that identifying and treating vulnerable records may matter as much as tightening epsilon.
