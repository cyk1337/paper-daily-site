18:T5da,The method has three components. First, dynamic leader selection. For each token, among the Top-K selected experts, one expert is sampled as the leader according to normalized routing scores rather than deterministically taking the top-scoring expert. This prevents one expert from always dominating the unmodified update path. Second, gradient accumulation. Each expert stores a recent history of output gradients as an orthonormal basis using Gram-Schmidt, forming a compact subspace memory of past update directions. Third, gradient projection. The leader keeps its original task gradient. Each follower removes the component of its output gradient that lies in the leader’s accumulated gradient subspace, keeping only the orthogonal component. As a result, co-routed experts processing the same token are discouraged from moving in highly redundant directions.

This differs cleanly from prior work. It does not redesign the router, does not require extra forward or backward passes, and does not enforce diversity through an auxiliary loss in parameter space or representation space. It acts directly on expert output gradients that already exist in the standard training pipeline. In that sense, it is a backward-time intervention rather than a new objective. The extra cost comes from maintaining per-expert gradient bases and performing projections. For deeper reading, the interesting questions are whether the stored gradient subspace becomes stale, whether stochastic leader selection adds harmful noise, and how this interacts with load balancing and capacity constraints in large-scale language MoE pretraining.1b:T708,The paper first develops the exploration-dilemma argument. In a shared-expert MoE layer, every token always receives the shared-expert contribution, while only a subset of routed experts is selected conditionally. During pretraining, the shared branch tends to dominate the learned token representation because it captures common knowledge for all tokens. Once that happens, changing the routed experts often produces only limited variation in the final features. Alternative routes become harder to distinguish, so the router receives weaker signals for meaningful exploration.

At the same time, fine-grained expert segmentation increases both E and K, which explodes the number of possible expert subsets. The paper gives a concrete example: moving from (E, K) = (8, 1) to (64, 8) increases the routing space from 8 possibilities to about 4.4 × 10^9 combinations. So the architecture demands more strategic exploration exactly when shared experts make exploration less effective.

MirrorMoE is proposed to resolve this mismatch by adaptively shrinking the routed-expert exploration space on a per-token basis. The method is derived by mirroring routing logits through sign negation. The intuition is not to add more randomness, but to suppress routed experts adaptively so that the effective search space better matches the model’s exploration capability under shared-expert dominance. This is different from simply reducing K globally, injecting noise, or tweaking load-balancing losses. The paper frames MirrorMoE as a way to improve trainability and route discoverability, not as a way to increase theoretical expressiveness. It also claims a theoretical characterization of the adaptivity, suggesting the suppression behavior is tied to routing structure rather than being a heuristic knob.1c:T76c,The experiments are built around a clear question: when does adding shared experts help, and when does it interfere with the benefits of larger K? The authors train matched MoEs with and without shared experts at four scales, from 100M to 2.4B total parameters. At each scale, they fix the total number of experts E and vary the number of activated experts K over {6, 8, 10}. This is a good stress test because larger K simultaneously increases expressiveness and enlarges the routing search space.

Three results stand out. First, the benefit of shared experts diminishes as K increases. In some settings, shared-expert MoEs even get worse when K becomes larger. That is the empirical signature of the exploration dilemma: more combinatorial capacity does not translate into better pretraining because the model cannot explore the larger routing space effectively. Second, MirrorMoE consistently improves this regime. Third, MirrorMoE restores the expected gains from increasing K and allows shared-expert MoEs to consistently outperform their non-shared counterparts again.
