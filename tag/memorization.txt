17:T577,The contribution is an evaluation framework.
- Key reframing: attribution should be judged at the knowledge/support level, not merely by output sensitivity. Under IW/ICL overlap, sensitivity is not identifiable: removing a supporting span may not change the output because the same fact is available in weights.
- Metrics to expose this confounding:
  - BCS (Base-model Context attribution Score): evaluate attribution using a base model intended to have less IW access to the target knowledge, creating a control condition.
  - CAC (Cross-model Attribution Consistency): measure how attribution changes across models with different IW knowledge states; large, systematic drift indicates the method is reacting to IW overlap rather than contextual support.
  - APS (Attribution Preservation Score): quantify whether supporting spans keep their attribution when IW availability changes.
  - SSP (Source Separation Precision): treat the task as explicit source separation (ICL vs IW) and measure precision directly.
- Dataset: WMDP-Cyber++ adds provenance-style ground truth labels over context segments (which spans are truly supporting vs irrelevant, and which are IW-overlapping), enabling systematic evaluation.
The paper evaluates several representative methods (ContextCite, AttriBot, TracLLM, Token-Shapley) and argues the failure is structural for removal-based approaches, not a tuning issue.18:T4c5,A quantifiable Memorization Window is defined from two complementary perspectives: 1) Retention window, measured by the loss retention gap between the first and subsequent encounters of a sample, which marks the number of additional training tokens after which the model forgets the sample; 2) Generalization window, measured by downstream performance, which marks the training horizon after which continued training on the sample leads to overfitting. The effective memorization window is the minimum of these two values, representing the safe interval after which a sample can be reintroduced to the training stream. Based on this signal, Memorization-guided Data Reuse (MDR) continuously tracks per-sample loss retention dynamics during training, adaptively scheduling repeat exposures only when the interval since last exposure exceeds the memorization window, rather than using fixed epoch schedules. A core empirical finding is that when repeat intervals exceed the memorization window, the slope of validation loss decrease from repeated tokens is indistinguishable from that of fresh tokens, with no diminishing marginal returns; the only hard performance ceiling is set by the intrinsic diversity of the dataset.19:T4ba,Experiments are conducted on LLM pre-training setups ranging from 1B to 7B parameters. Key findings include: 1) Memorization window length varies with training stage and data quality: windows are shorter early in training and longer later; windows for high-quality data are 2-3x longer than for low-quality data. 2) Adaptive scheduling based on memorization windows allows safe reuse of high-quality data for 8-12 epochs (compared to the conventional 4-epoch heuristic) without overfitting, delivering 3-4 percentage point higher average downstream performance than fixed 4-epoch training. 3) When repeat intervals match the memorization window, the learning efficiency of repeated tokens is within 5% of fresh tokens, with nearly identical loss reduction per token. 4) Compared to baselines including globally shuffled multi-epoch training and fixed repetition ratios, MDR delivers 0.08-0.12 lower validation loss and 2-5 percentage point higher average downstream performance under the same total training token budget, with no observed overfitting. Ablations show memorization window length scales positively with model parameter count and total training tokens processed, and negatively with learning rate.1a:T6e4,The work has two technical pieces. First, it introduces a sample-level evaluation framework, mainly for multiple-choice benchmarks. Instead of relying only on accuracy, it compares output distributions on each sample. The intended criterion is not just whether the decontaminated model gets the same answer as a clean model, but whether its probability allocation over choices looks similar to the clean model. A contaminated model often over-concentrates mass on the gold answer; a successful decontamination method should reduce that distortion and recover a more natural distribution over alternatives.

Second, it proposes UBD, a family of uncertainty-based decontamination methods built on deep ensembles. The key idea is that loss is an unreliable proxy for contamination: both easy examples and memorized hard examples can have low loss after contamination. Ensemble-based predictive uncertainty is used to identify suspicious samples more selectively. On top of that signal, the paper develops two intervention styles: (1) debiasing, which adjusts the output distribution for likely contaminated samples to reduce contamination-induced overconfidence; and (2) unlearning, which applies a stronger forgetting-style update to move the model away from the contaminated behavior on those samples.

Compared with methods that minimize KL to a clean teacher, UBD does not require a clean reference model. Compared with paraphrasing or answer-permutation baselines, it does not try to evade memorization by changing the input surface form. It instead estimates contamination through uncertainty and then corrects the model behavior directly. That distinction is important: the former is closer to benchmark-side mitigation, while UBD aims at model-side debiasing.1b:T4a3,From the available full-text excerpt, the experiments appear to support three claims. First, aggregate accuracy can make different decontamination methods look similar even when their sample-level behavior differs substantially. Distribution-distance metrics expose those differences. Second, UBD produces per-sample output distributions that are closer to those of an uncontaminated model than paraphrasing and choice-permutation baselines. Third, uncertainty is shown to separate hard-but-memorized samples from genuinely easy ones, even when both have low loss under the contaminated model. That is the most interesting empirical point, because it argues that loss alone is not a sufficient contamination indicator.
