9:T75f,The main contribution is not a new model but a unified benchmark and protocol. System1-Arena represents each static example or environment step as a decision request r=(s,Q). The state s is a small set of named text fields, such as a message, a code diff, or a rendered webpage. Q is a set of typed questions over the same state. Questions are boolean, choice, or score. Each question includes instructions, and each option includes a description, so models do not rely on dataset-specific prompting. The output is a probability distribution over options for each question, not free-form text.

Several design choices matter. First, the interface is shared across model families. Dedicated System-1 models, autoregressive LLMs, and even diffusion LMs are all mapped into the same probability-based API. For open autoregressive and diffusion models, the authors provide a method to derive decision probabilities from answer tokens, which is important because it makes otherwise incompatible model families comparable. Second, the benchmark evaluates not only accuracy but also Brier score, since calibrated probabilities are central for thresholding, escalation, and risk-sensitive deployment. Third, it includes closed-loop environments and a real-time protocol in which the environment continues to evolve during inference according to each model’s measured latency. That turns latency from a side metric into part of the task definition.

The benchmark is broad: 92,125 typed questions across 105 tasks from 268 public sources in 16 domains, plus 138 closed-loop environments from 18 families, including web shopping, SWE-style coding control, robotics, games, and driving. This is not just a bundle of existing classification datasets. The benchmark standardizes instructions, option semantics, label sources, and environment feedback into a common probabilistic decision framework.a:T7e5,The evaluation covers 30 models: 7 closed LLMs, 13 open LLMs, and 10 System-1 models. On non-agentic tasks, Jev and Decision-2.0-Vega-27B reach 72.6% and 73.0% accuracy, slightly above GPT-6 Luna at 71.2%. They also provide better-calibrated probabilities and lower latency. The paper reports Jev at roughly $0.05 per 1,000 requests, which highlights the practical advantage of specialized decision models for high-throughput workloads.

The gains are task-dependent. The gap between strong System-1 models and the best LLMs is smallest on filtering, grouping, ranking, retrieval, and dialogue tasks. System-1 models remain weaker on knowledge and forecasting tasks. That pattern suggests that single-pass decision models are well suited to local discrimination and structured selection, but less suited to tasks that require broader knowledge integration or implicit multi-step reasoning.

Reasoning models still set the upper bound. Claude Opus 5.5 reaches 81.1% on non-agentic tasks, but at about 70 times the cost of a fast LLM. Among open models, Qwen3.8-27B reaches 75.6% when given a few hundred reasoning tokens per question, surpassing Jev in accuracy but producing less calibrated probabilities. This is an important result: explicit reasoning can improve top-1 accuracy without necessarily improving probability quality.

On agentic tasks, the paper separates single-step decision accuracy from long-horizon success. For single-step tasks, Decision-2.0-Vega-27B, Jev, and Qwen3.5-9B all reach 67.8% accuracy, showing that local action selection can be competitive across model types. On long-horizon tasks, Gemma-4-31B achieves the highest success rate at 32.9%, while the four small System-1 models reach at most 3.1%. This indicates a major weakness in long-range planning and recovery.
