18:T448,The paper asks a narrow but important question: do halting heads in looped language models actually convert low predicted iteration counts into real compute savings at inference time? The answer is often no. The mismatch comes from two places. First, the standard training objective is misaligned with the execution rule. With the common mixture loss, the halting head is trained through a mixture of hidden states from all iterations, while inference stops on one discrete iteration chosen by a thresholded cumulative halting rule. So the model can report low expected halting steps yet still run almost to the cap under the actual stopping rule. Second, batching changes the compute story again: even if some tokens halt early, the batch waits for the deepest token, so average token depth is not the same as realized batched compute. The paper is therefore not just about adaptive depth in the abstract. It is about where the training objective, stopping rule, and execution system fail to line up, how large that gap is, and how halting should be evaluated if one wants honest compute claims.19:T40a,This matters for pretraining-oriented model design because looped LMs and recurrent-depth LMs are often motivated by the promise of adaptive compute without extra parameters. The paper shows that this promise can be overstated if one reports only expected halting steps. More broadly, it is a clean case study of train-test mismatch in adaptive computation: a probabilistic training signal does not automatically translate into useful discrete decisions at inference. That lesson extends beyond looped LMs to early-exit transformers, pondering methods, and other conditional-compute schemes. It also contributes a practical reporting standard: expected steps, realized steps under a stated exit rule, and batched execution depth should all be reported. Finally, the paper proposes a pre-head diagnostic that estimates how much a halting mechanism could help before one spends effort training it. The contribution is not a new architecture. It is a careful correction to how this line of work should be trained, compared, and measured.1a:T7c9,The technical contribution is mainly analytical and experimental. First, the authors dissect the standard ACT-style mixture loss. In that setup, the language-model loss is computed from a hidden state formed by mixing representations from all iterations according to halting probabilities. This is convenient for optimization, but it gives the model access to a representation that is never available at inference, where the token exits at one concrete iteration. The paper quantifies this mismatch by replacing the mixture readout with the actual single-step state and showing a substantial degradation. Second, the authors test a PonderNet-style per-step loss, where every iteration is scored and the losses are weighted by the head’s own halting probabilities. This is much closer to the real execution path because the model is trained on all possible exit points. But the paper shows that this alone is still insufficient. Without a strong prior favoring early stopping, the learned distribution remains too conservative and realized savings stay weak. Section 6.2 is especially useful: the issue is not simply whether one supervises every step, but that the head also controls the weighting over steps, which encourages a self-consistent allocation of probability mass toward later iterations. Third, the evaluation protocol is upgraded from token-level expectations to actual execution. The paper separately measures expected halting steps, realized steps under the thresholded exit rule, and the number of iterations a batched pass really executes. This separation is central because it isolates three different objects that are often conflated. The paper also discusses a deployment detail that turns out to matter: in single-sequence generation, halted tokens have no fresh KV states for skipped iterations, yet later tokens may still attend to them there. Reusing halted tokens’ KV states is therefore treated as part of the method definition, not a minor implementation choice.1b:T8cb,The study trains looped models from 140M to 1.3B parameters, on two text corpora, mostly with three seeds, using a cap of 8 iterations. The main findings are consistent and fairly sharp. (1) With mixture-loss training, heads often predict fewer than 4 out of 8 iterations on average, but under the standard 90% cumulative-probability exit rule the realized depth remains close to the full 8. In other words, the headline expected depth reduction does not translate into actual compute reduction. (2) Forcing earlier stopping hurts quality quickly. Once the saving exceeds roughly one iteration, a same-size sampled-depth baseline—trained without a halting head, simply with randomly chosen iteration counts—outperforms the halting model at the same compute budget. That is a strong baseline result because it shows the halting mechanism can lose to a simpler training recipe on the quality-compute frontier. (3) The train-test mismatch is large in language-model terms: replacing the mixture state with the actual single-step state costs about 0.2 BPB, while the gap between the smallest and largest models in the paper is about 0.24 BPB. So the mismatch is not a minor artifact; it is comparable to a substantial scaling gain. (4) Per-step loss only works when paired with a strong early-stop prior. Without that prior, the objective is more faithful to inference but still does not produce useful realized savings. (5) Batching removes most of the remaining advantage. On whole-text evaluation, the per-step model can beat the sampled-depth baseline at matched average depth; in generation with batches of 32 sequences, the actual batch depth rises well above the average token depth and most of the advantage disappears. (6) The same phenomenon is observed for Ouro, a released looped LM with a trained halting head. (7) In single-sequence generation, some benefit survives if halted tokens’ KV states are reused across skipped iterations, but even then the model does not beat a specialist trained to run at a fixed depth of about three iterations. The experimental arc is well structured: invalidate the common metric, identify the loss-level cause, add the systems-level effect, and compare against simpler baselines under matched compute.1c:T427,For a quick but useful read, start with the Introduction and Figures 1–2. The paper only makes sense once the distinction among expected halting steps, realized steps, and batched execution depth is clear.
