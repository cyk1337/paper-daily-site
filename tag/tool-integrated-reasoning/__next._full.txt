19:T76e,The method has three stages. Stage one is MOSAIC trajectory synthesis. Starting from extended-thinking traces, the authors convert linear text reasoning into interleaved trajectories composed of deliberate thinking, action, and observation. Actions can be intermediate code snippets or final answers. The important part is not merely inserting tool-call markers. MOSAIC injects stochastic adversarial perturbations so that originally smooth trajectories become failure-prone and require recovery. In effect, the synthesized data contains replanning, self-correction, and strategic adaptation instead of only idealized successful runs.

Stage two is behavioral initialization through cold-start SFT. This stage teaches the protocol of alternating thought, action, and observation. Its role is mainly to align the model to the interaction format and basic behavior pattern before RL, reducing the search burden in a large action space.

Stage three is process-supervised RL. Built on GRPO, the paper introduces an Action-Centric Process Reward that provides dense turn-level supervision rather than only final-answer reward. From the method description, the reward is intended to score not just whether the final answer is correct, but whether intermediate deliberation is sensible, tool invocation is precise, and the model reacts appropriately to new observations. Compared with outcome-only RL, this is a better fit for multi-step tool use because many failures are caused by early bad actions, and sparse terminal rewards make credit assignment too weak.

The central claim is interleaved deliberation: effective tool use requires local planning and self-correction at every think–act–observe turn, not a one-shot plan followed by blind execution. That idea is not entirely new, but the paper packages it into a concrete recipe of trajectory synthesis, cold-start alignment, and process-level RL.1a:T612,From the available full-text excerpt, the experiments cover multiple challenging tool-reasoning benchmarks and include a cost-effectiveness analysis intended to show that interleaved thinking balances performance and token usage. The abstract claims broad gains over prior methods, but the provided content does not include the actual result tables, model sizes, training-set scale, tool inventory, or exact improvement margins. Without those details, it would be unsafe to fabricate numbers.

Still, the likely experimental structure is clear. First, there should be headline comparisons against SFT-only baselines, outcome-reward RL baselines, and probably standard ReAct-style tool-use methods. Second, there should be robustness tests showing that MOSAIC’s adversarial perturbations improve recovery from execution failures, noisy tool outputs, or misleading intermediate observations. Third, there should be efficiency analyses: whether the gains come from better decision quality at key steps or simply from spending many more reasoning tokens.

If reading the full paper, the most important ablations to verify are these: how much Action-Centric Process Reward improves over outcome-only reward; how much performance drops when adversarial perturbation is removed from MOSAIC; and whether the cold-start SFT stage contributes mostly formatting alignment or already accounts for most of the gains.
