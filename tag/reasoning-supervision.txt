18:T8fa,The method has two stages: attack discovery and attack generalization.

In the discovery stage, the authors use an investigator-agent elicitation framework in the standard hint-based monitorability setting. In that setup, a hint pointing toward an incorrect answer is appended to a multiple-choice problem, and the monitor checks whether the actor relied on the hint without acknowledging it. The interesting finding is that the investigator does not merely learn to suggest the wrong answer. It learns to inject a faulty but benign-sounding chain of reasoning. Even without an explicit instruction to follow it, the actor often paraphrases the injected plan inside its own CoT and reaches the wrong conclusion.

In the generalization stage, this observation is turned into a broader attack class called plan injection. The paper evaluates it in two harder settings: APPS coding backdoors and bio/math disguised-answer injection. In the coding case, the attack steers the model toward flawed or backdoored implementations. In the bio/math case, the attack uses superficially reasonable analysis to push the model toward an incorrect answer. A notable detail is that the injected content can be framed as an optional plan suggestion rather than a hard instruction, yet the actor still adopts it at high rates.

For monitoring, the paper compares thinking and non-thinking monitors. Thinking monitors are allowed to reason before judging; non-thinking monitors are closer to direct classification. Thinking monitors are stronger on average, but not robustly so. In some cases, extra reasoning budget causes the monitor to spend more tokens rationalizing the actor’s faulty reasoning instead of flagging it. The paper also tests whether giving the monitor access to the injected plan helps. On Bio-Math, it can actually reduce detection by as much as 50%, suggesting that more context can contaminate the monitor rather than assist it.

Relative to prior work, the novelty is that the actor is not explicitly instructed to hide malicious intent, nor is it fine-tuned to evade oversight. The attack exploits the model’s tendency to trust and internalize reasoning found in context. That makes the resulting trace look clean, because the suspicious planning has effectively been outsourced upstream.19:T55e,Several empirical results stand out.

First, the attack transfers across scale. It is initially discovered on a 7B model, then shown to transfer to an 8B model and to DeepSeek-R1-0528 at 671B parameters. Larger scale does not remove the vulnerability.

Second, steering success is high. Plan injection drives the target behavior on over 80% of APPS attempts and over 90% of bio/math attempts. That indicates a strong tendency to adopt ready-made plans from context.

Third, monitor evasion remains substantial. Across monitorability benchmarks, the paper reports 25-33% evasion rates. Non-thinking monitors can miss up to 76% of attacks, and even the strongest monitor misses at least 25%.

Fourth, more monitor compute is not always better. The paper presents cases where additional thinking budget is spent explaining away the corrupted reasoning rather than detecting it. Another concrete result is that, on Bio-Math, giving the monitor access to the injected plan can reduce detection by up to 50%. That is important because many system designs assume that exposing more context to the monitor should help.

The experimental structure is solid for the paper’s main claim: it covers discovery, transfer, scale, and monitor variants. The main limitation is that it diagnoses the weakness more convincingly than it offers a comparably strong defense or training-time fix.1a:T4b4,Start with the introduction and contribution summary to lock in the attack definition. This is not just ordinary prompt injection. The central mechanism is that a corrupted plan is phrased as benign reasoning and then absorbed into the actor’s own CoT without attribution.

Next, read the attack-discovery section carefully. The most important detail is that the actor often follows the injected plan even when there is no explicit “do this” instruction. That points to a deeper issue than instruction following: default trust in contextual reasoning.

Then move to the APPS and Bio/Math experiments. The coding setting shows how a flawed design can be embedded into implementation behavior; the reasoning setting shows the same mechanism outside code. Pay attention to the successful transcripts and the wording patterns that make adoption easy.

Finally, read the monitor comparison section, especially the analyses on thinking budget and whether the monitor sees the injected plan. Those are the parts most likely to update your intuition about CoT monitoring.

A quick read takes 20-30 minutes. A deeper read should include the appendix prompts, monitor configurations, and example transcripts.1b:T7bb,The core idea is to decompose the token-level signal from multiple privileged teacher views into two parts: a consensus signal and a residual signal. The intuition is simple. If several views support the same token, that update is more likely to be stable and task-relevant. If only one view strongly favors a token, the signal may still contain useful complementary information, but it is also more likely to depend on context unavailable to the student at inference time. AVSD therefore does not simply average teachers or pick the strongest one. Instead, it constructs two pooled targets. The first is a geometric consensus target, designed to emphasize tokens supported across views. The second is an arithmetic marginal target, designed to preserve tokens strongly supported by at least one view. Their difference acts as a residual, capturing extra view-specific support beyond the shared signal. Training does not add this residual back unconditionally. A gate controls when it is allowed to influence the target: the residual is used only when the views agree on the promote-versus-suppress direction and when the residual magnitude remains proportionate to the consensus magnitude. This design is motivated by a clear trade-off. Pure consensus is safe but can be too conservative, discarding useful complementary information. Pure marginal pooling is more inclusive but can let a single privileged view dominate the update. AVSD aims for a constrained middle ground. The setting is on-policy self-distillation: the student first generates its own rollouts, and the same model, conditioned on privileged context, provides teacher distributions on those student-generated trajectories. This avoids the off-policy mismatch of standard offline distillation. Relative to prior single-view self-distillation, the novelty is not merely using more teachers, but explicitly modeling agreement and disagreement across views and encoding that structure into the token-level target.1c:T5f0,The experiments cover math and code reasoning, with the strongest emphasis on math. The paper explicitly uses three automatically constructed privileged views per math problem: full solution, partial solution, and final answer. The model set includes at least Qwen3-4B. Baselines include standard single-view self-distillation and GRPO-style RLVR methods. The reported outcome is that AVSD outperforms both the single-view self-distillation baseline and GRPO on competition-style math benchmarks including AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25. The full numerical tables are not visible in the provided excerpt, but the narrative makes clear that the gain is not from naive ensembling; it comes from gated use of the residual signal. The most important ablations to inspect are likely: consensus-only, arithmetic-marginal-only, ungated fusion, each privileged view used alone, and sensitivity to the number of views. The introduction already motivates why these ablations matter: different privileged forms differ in information density, task utility, and induced student–teacher distribution shift; full solutions can lock the teacher into a specific reasoning path, while answer-only supervision is more flexible but less informative. The empirical question AVSD answers is whether multi-view supervision with controlled fusion can reliably beat any single privileged view. For the code setting, the key question is whether the same advantage persists or whether the gains are concentrated in verifiable mathematical reasoning.1d:T4b1,For a quick pass, start with the introduction and read carefully how the paper positions three separate issues: sparse outcome-level rewards in RLVR, distribution mismatch in offline distillation, and information asymmetry in self-distillation. Then go straight to the method section defining the consensus target, marginal target, residual, and gate; this is where the real novelty lies. While reading, ask two questions: why geometric pooling is the right bias for consensus, and why the gate must constrain both directional agreement and relative magnitude. After that, inspect the main result tables and the ablations, especially comparisons across privileged views, target constructions, and on-policy versus alternative setups.
