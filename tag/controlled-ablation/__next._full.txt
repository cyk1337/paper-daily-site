19:T7b1,The core is a five-stage retrieval-grounded synthesis pipeline split into two phases:
Phase I (Knowledge Extraction, Steps 0-2): 1) A four-level discipline taxonomy: the first three levels (62 L1 / 754 L2 / 2,730 L3 nodes) are adopted from the Chinese National Standard of Discipline Classification; L3 nodes are expanded into 12,657 fine-grained L4 subfields via LLM, followed by LLM-assisted deduplication under each L2 node to cover 15,387 total disciplines. 2) For each leaf discipline, an LLM generates 10-15 statement-form queries covering core concepts, methods, applications, and limitations; statement-form queries are chosen because they match the declarative register of pretraining text and deliver higher cluster retention rates in pilot tests. 3) Each query retrieves the top 100 passages from the pretraining corpus, passages from all queries of the same discipline are aggregated, deduplicated, and clustered into thematic units.
Phase II (Structured Generation, Steps 3-4): 4) Hierarchical tables of contents (TOCs) are planned based on thematic units with quality filtering applied. 5) Source-grounded section content is generated per TOC entry and assembled into complete books, with multi-variant generation across different audiences and writing styles to ensure distributional diversity.
Four controlled settings are designed to isolate variables: Full (retain each TOC-planned textbook as a single training document), Split (use identical generated sections and token budget as Full, but treat each section as an independent document with reset position embeddings and intra-document attention), RandomConcat (randomly concatenate Split sections across books to match Full's length distribution, separating the effect of planned adjacency from raw document length), and Rephrase (independently rewrite individual documents from the same retrieval pool under matched audience/style constraints, without clustering, TOC planning, or book assembly).1a:T60d,The paper is deliberately designed as a controlled comparison rather than a proposal of another training algorithm. Four representative methods are re-implemented under the same GRPO-style objective and the same Verl codebase: Search-R1 as a pure outcome-reward baseline with trajectory-level credit only; GiGPO with cross-trajectory step grouping based on state similarity; IGPO with heuristic per-turn rewards derived from changes in ground-truth log-probability; and Tree-GRPO with branch-based local comparisons from shared prefixes. The point is not to showcase these methods individually, but to remove implementation and infrastructure variance so that credit-assignment choices can be compared more cleanly.

The technical substance of the paper has three parts. First, it audits the retrieval environment and documents missing relevant passages in the standard Wikipedia 2018 setup, then constructs a more complete corpus and reruns training/evaluation under that corrected environment. Second, it compares outcome-based and process-based rewards not only on final QA accuracy but also on intermediate search behavior, which is important because process rewards are supposed to shape search trajectories rather than just final answers. Third, it studies training protocol variables: data diversity, degree of off-policy usage, and search-budget scaling at both training and inference time. This makes the paper closer to a recipe study than an algorithm paper: it identifies first-order versus second-order factors in search-agent learning.1b:T6ba,The experimental setup is reasonably controlled. Training uses a unified set of 9,000 examples sampled from HotpotQA, MuSiQue, and 2WikiMultihopQA. Evaluation spans HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue, Bamboogle, and PopQA, for a total of 4,125 test instances. The reward comparison is also run across three base models, which helps avoid overfitting conclusions to a single backbone.

The most important result is qualitative but strong: correcting retrieval coverage in Wikipedia 2018 produces larger gains than the differences among the compared training algorithms. That means some previously reported method advantages may have been artifacts of retrieval misses rather than better learning. On reward design, the simplest outcome-based method is competitive with, and often better than, more elaborate process-reward methods across most settings. The paper’s behavior analysis suggests why: process-level credit assignment can over-correct. It may improve one local aspect of search, such as more targeted evidence gathering, while hurting another, such as exploration diversity or robustness against premature narrowing. The protocol analysis further indicates that data diversity is a more reliable lever than sophisticated credit heuristics, that off-policy data has a useful but limited range before distribution mismatch becomes harmful, and that search budget matters in both training and inference but does not scale linearly with returns.
