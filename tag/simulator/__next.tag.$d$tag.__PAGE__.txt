9:T90d,Methodologically, this is a survey, but not a standard taxonomy survey. The authors first narrow the scope to the generative route of world models: models that map current state and action to a distribution over future observations or states. Policy-route systems are discussed as context, but not treated as simulator substitutes because their interface is fundamentally different from a state-feedback simulator. They then define an eight-capability framework: C1 asset construction, C2 physics engine, C3 interaction, C4 controllability, C5 stability, C6 state feedback, C7 diversity, and C8 evaluation metrics. This framing asks what responsibilities a model can actually assume in a simulator stack, rather than what model family it belongs to.

The main analytical innovations are threefold. First, the benchmark is external: traditional simulator capabilities, not self-referential world-model categories. That makes the gap between “looks plausible” and “simulates correctly” much easier to expose. Second, the paper uses paper-level coding to build a reproducible evidence map rather than a purely narrative review. Third, it treats state feedback as a first-class capability and quantifies it with a B1–B5 labeling scheme. This is one of the strongest parts of the paper. Many world models can emit pixels, tokens, or latent states, but they do not provide explicit object states, physical variables, queryable interfaces, or structured feedback that planners, controllers, or verifiers can reliably consume.

The paper’s overall diagnosis is also sharper than the usual “long-horizon generation is hard.” It argues that current progress is relatively stronger on interaction, controllability, and some aspects of stability, especially in short-horizon action-conditioned prediction and interactive rollouts. The structural gaps remain asset construction, physics, and state feedback. The reason is straightforward: data-driven generative models fit observation distributions well, but they do not naturally encode explicit geometry, contact dynamics, conservation laws, compositional object structure, or editable internal states. Hallucination, geometry drift, implausible collisions, and inconsistent consequences under identical actions are all treated as symptoms of missing invariants.a:T624,This is not an experimental methods paper; its empirical contribution is a corpus audit. The most important quantitative facts are these. First, the study builds a curated corpus of about 200 papers and performs systematic coding on 163 implementation papers. Second, Section IV-F conducts a paper-level audit of state-feedback interfaces and quantifies them with a B1–B5 scale, leading to the conclusion that state feedback is both essential for simulation and structurally absent in much of the literature. Third, after comparing progress across the eight capabilities, the paper concludes that controllability, interaction, and parts of stability are relative strengths, while asset construction, physics engines, and state feedback remain clear weaknesses.

The most useful “results” are not single benchmark numbers but the uneven capability profile. Many systems are already competent at visual realism, short-horizon rollouts, and action-conditioned generation. Performance degrades quickly when the requirement shifts to long-term consistency, explicit physics, object-level state, or verifiable feedback. The paper repeatedly grounds this diagnosis in recurring failure modes: objects appearing or disappearing without cause, geometric drift over long rollouts, contact and collision behavior that violates physical intuition, and inconsistent outcomes under the same action. For pretraining researchers, these failure patterns are more informative than a single leaderboard score because they point directly to weaknesses in objectives and representations.b:T4ad,For a quick read, focus on three parts. Finally, read Section IV-F carefully, including the B1–B5 labeling scheme. That is the most original analytical component.
