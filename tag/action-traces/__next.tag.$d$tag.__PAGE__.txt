5:T78e,The core contribution is a corrected protocol for measuring cross-lingual action-policy retention. Under a fixed scaffold, models operate over a shared five-tool alphabet and emit executed action traces alongside answers. The study covers 8 models, 6 parallel benchmarks, 41 languages, and 2.38 million rollouts. A naive trace-similarity metric fails for five reasons: short traces look artificially similar, empty traces score perfectly, unrelated traces overlap by chance, same-language reruns are not identical, and without a within-language baseline the quantity is not identifiable.

The estimator addresses each issue explicitly. Every language-task cell is run twice with identical decoding settings and token budgets, changing only the serving seed. Same-language agreement, I_within, compares the two replicates in one language. Cross-language agreement, I_cross, compares traces across languages, also cross-seed. The normalized retention metric is \tilde{I}=I_cross/I_within, which measures how much of a model’s own reproducible policy survives a language change. Comparisons are length-matched in both directions, pairs are dropped if either trace is empty, and uncertainty is estimated with task-level bootstraps. This design makes language the only systematic difference while keeping decoding noise symmetric.

The paper then probes mechanism. The evidence points to an English pivot. Translate is the most frequently used tool across adapted benchmarks, and reasoning text is about 99% ASCII even for non-Latin inputs such as Devanagari. Removing the translation tool lowers agreement in proportion to a model’s prior reliance on translation; forcing translation helps in a pre-registered ordering across models. The implication is that many multilingual agents do not execute the same policy directly in the target language. They often map the task into an English-centered internal workflow and plan from there.6:T697,The empirical scale is substantial: 8 models, 6 parallel benchmarks, 41 languages, and 2.38M rollouts. Three results stand out. First, under greedy decoding, all 24 main cells show a positive cross-lingual policy gap, and every bootstrap interval on the raw gap excludes zero. The effect is stable rather than anecdotal. Second, every correction increases the measured effect instead of shrinking it. That is important because it means the problem was previously hidden by bad measurement, not because it was weak. Third, after normalization, four otherwise very different models converge to nearly the same policy-retention value under greedy decoding: about 71–73%. The regularity holds at the cell level, not just in model averages, and model identity explains only about 5.7% of the variance.

The temperature ladder is also informative. As sampling temperature rises, cross-language agreement changes little, while same-language self-consistency drops sharply. Without normalization, sampling noise masks the true cross-lingual policy loss. On the mechanism side, Translate is the most-used tool in every adapted benchmark, and reasoning text is almost entirely ASCII, indicating that non-English tasks are often routed through an English internal channel. Removing translation hurts agreement roughly in proportion to how much a model already uses it; forcing translation helps in the pre-registered order predicted from those usage rates. When reading the full paper, it is worth checking how tightly the tool alphabet is standardized across benchmarks, how the bidirectional length matching is implemented, and whether retention differs systematically across language families.7:T50e,This one is worth a careful read. Start with the introduction and method sections to understand why the paper insists on measuring action traces rather than final answers, and how each of the five confounds can distort the conclusion. Then read the section defining I_within, I_cross, and \tilde{I}; that is the identification core of the paper. After that, inspect the main result figures, especially the claim that every correction makes the effect larger. Finally, read the temperature analysis and the translation-tool ablations, which explain why multilingual agents can look answer-consistent while still following different internal policies.

From a pretraining angle, two questions are especially worth carrying through the paper. First, is the English-pivot behavior driven mainly by pretraining data distribution, tokenizer effects, or English-heavy tool-use post-training data? Second, does the near-constant 71–73% retention hint at a structural bottleneck in cross-lingual policy transfer, where only part of the planner survives language change? A full read of the main text plus the key appendices will likely take 45–60 minutes.
