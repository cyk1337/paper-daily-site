17:T76f,The framework is intentionally simple. Stage one is parallel reasoning. Given a query q, a reasoning model samples K independent trajectories. The emphasis on independence is important: instead of forcing multiple branches inside one long context with special tags, the paper separates exploration from aggregation so that candidate paths do not interfere too early.

Stage two is sequential deliberation. A second model receives the original problem together with the K trajectories, and produces a final answer by comparing, reconciling, and summarizing them. This is stronger than majority voting and different from selecting the single best chain. The deliberation model is allowed to inspect disagreements, identify likely mistakes, and synthesize a new answer.

The paper also adds a memory cache. This is not long-term agent memory in the usual sense. It is a structured store for previously generated trajectories and intermediate summaries, enabling iterative deliberation and refinement. In effect, it acts as a minimal memory component extracted from larger agent harnesses.

A useful contribution of the paper is the decomposition of gains into three factors: per-trajectory reasoning quality, diversity across trajectories, and the capability of the deliberation model. The first two determine the candidate pool; the third determines how much of that latent pool can be recovered. This leads naturally to a split-optimization view where one model is tuned for broad search and another for judgment and synthesis.

The paper further adapts RLVR to this setting. The idea is to optimize both breadth and depth: the parallel stage is encouraged to produce more correct and complementary trajectories, while the deliberation stage is optimized for final answer correctness. That makes the reward signal act not only on the terminal answer but also on the structure of the reasoning population.18:T72d,The experiments span STEM, coding, and general tasks, with the clearest analysis on verifiable STEM benchmarks where Pass@k and related metrics are well defined. Four findings stand out.

First, heavy thinking consistently beats single-path reasoning. The gain appears even without a complicated tool-using harness, which supports the paper’s reductionist claim.

Second, on benchmarks with verifiable answers, the paper reports a stable ordering: Heavy-Pass@k ≥ Heavy-Mean@k ≥ Vote@k ≥ Mean@k. This is one of the most informative results in the paper. It shows that sequential deliberation is doing more than answer aggregation by vote; it is recovering signal that simple self-consistency leaves on the table.

Third, stronger base reasoners get much closer to the Pass@k upper bound under heavy thinking. That is important because Pass@k measures whether at least one correct trajectory exists in the candidate set. If deliberation approaches that ceiling, the model is effectively acting as an implicit verifier or synthesizer over candidate traces.

Fourth, the ablations show that both trajectory quality and trajectory diversity matter. If the parallel stage produces many near-duplicates, deliberation has little to work with. If the trajectories are diverse but weak, summarization cannot rescue them. The paper also finds that sequential deliberation depends strongly on the general capability of the model used in that stage, which supports using separate models for reasoning and deliberation.
