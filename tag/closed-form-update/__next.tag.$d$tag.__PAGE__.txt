5:T6a0,The method edits a single weight matrix W at a time, either the LM head or the down-projection in an MLP block. The model performs one forward pass on forget data and one on retain data, collects the input activations to W, and stacks them as key matrices. Keys are extracted only at positions where the next token should be forgotten or preserved, so the optimization is token-level rather than sample-level.

The objective is a ridge-regularized least-squares problem over an additive update ΔW. One term pushes the edited matrix toward a predefined forgetting target on forget keys. Another term constrains outputs on retain keys to stay close to the original model. Because the objective is quadratic and convex in ΔW, the optimum has a closed-form solution. This is the main departure from methods such as gradient ascent, RKLD, NPO, or preference-style unlearning, which search for a compromise through SGD.

The paper uses two target designs. Token suppression directly lowers the model’s tendency to emit the memorized answer token or related logits. Representation corruption instead perturbs intermediate representations so downstream layers cannot easily reconstruct the deleted content. The authors also introduce a logit-lens-based layer selection strategy: they inspect which intermediate layers already make the target content linearly decodable through the LM head, then edit only those high-impact layers. This avoids broad model changes. A notable extra is exact influence auditing. Because the update is analytic, the contribution of an individual deletion request to the final parameter edit can be decomposed exactly, which is much harder with iterative gradient methods.6:T66d,The paper evaluates forgetting-utility trade-offs on five benchmarks and uses gold retraining as a reference point. Three findings matter most. First, efficiency: GROM needs only forward-pass statistics for forget and retain sets plus a closed-form solve per edited layer, leading to second-level execution and up to two orders of magnitude speedup over gradient-based baselines. Second, effectiveness: on Pareto trade-offs, it is typically better than or competitive with GA, GradDiff, RMU, RKLD, NPO, and SimNPO, meaning stronger forgetting at matched utility or better utility at matched forgetting. Third, robustness: under the recent low-bit quantization recovery attack, a strong gradient-based baseline regains a substantial amount of supposedly erased content, while GROM remains close to the retrained model in forgetfulness.

The most informative ablations are not just average scores but the structure of the edit: which layers are edited, whether the LM head or MLP down-projection is modified, token suppression versus representation corruption, the ridge coefficient, the size of the retain set, and interference across multiple deletion requests. The broader experimental message is that local linear edits can handle a meaningful fraction of memory-style unlearning when layer selection is accurate and retain constraints are strong, but distributed multi-layer knowledge remains a harder case.
