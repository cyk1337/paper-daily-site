17:T48d,CAST (Constrained Ablation for Safe Steering). Setup: a steering vector v is injected into the residual stream at a chosen layer during inference (activation addition), typically built as a mean activation difference from contrastive prompt pairs.
CAST parameterizes a sanitized vector by projecting v away from a learned safety-degrading subspace S: v* = v - P_S(v). Since the true dimensionality is unknown, the main experiments use a rank-1 approximation with a learned unit direction r̂: v* = v - r̂ r̂^T v.
Learning r̂ is posed as a Lagrangian constrained optimization with primal-dual updates, balancing three requirements:
- Safety restoration: on a harmful prompt distribution D_harm (multiple jailbreak styles), the steered model with v* should refuse at least as often as the unsteered baseline.
- Effect preservation: on D_effect, outputs under v* should remain close to outputs under the original v.
- False-refusal control: on D_benign, v* should not significantly increase over-refusals relative to baseline.
The paper argues the safety-degrading component is largely separable from the effect-relevant component, enabling “surgical” ablation.18:T4e9,The study evaluates Gemma-3 4B, Qwen-3.5 9B, and Gemma-3 12B on BBH, GPQA, and MMLU. It uses four misleading cue types adapted from Meek et al.: a Stanford-professor sycophancy cue, XML metadata, a grader-hacking hint, and an unethical insider tip, each pointing to an incorrect option. Cue type is varied on GPQA, while dataset transfer is tested with the Stanford cue fixed. For each scenario, degenerate rollouts are filtered, but examples are not conditioned on whether the model follows the cue or whether the uncued answer differs. The split is roughly 67/33 train/test, with about 129–165 test items per scenario. Generation uses greedy decoding with matched limits and penalties within each model. The main findings are that reliable increases in cue acknowledgment appear mostly in Gemma-3 12B and vary by dataset; however, vectors trained on one cue perform similarly to vectors trained on the evaluated cue, and vectors trained on one dataset transfer comparably to target-dataset vectors when the cue is fixed. Synthetic and cue-agnostic optimized directions perform similarly to labeled contrastive vectors. Additional analysis shows that successful steering leaves the overall rate of cue use roughly unchanged while reducing hidden cue use.19:T442,The method builds on contrastive persona-vector extraction from prior activation-steering work. For each trait, natural-language positive and negative descriptions are used to derive a behavioral direction in activation space; interventions along that direction are then tested during generation. The main addition is the diagnostic taxonomy. A trait is labeled natural if it appears at baseline, steerable if it can be amplified by intervention, and intractable if the standard protocol cannot recover a usable direction. The authors construct a 53-trait inventory across clinician interaction, generic behavioral disposition, elementary education, and agentic task completion. They also study pairwise composition by applying two persona vectors jointly over all 171 unordered pairs from 19 generic traits, looking for clean composition, dominance, or collapse. A safety-related case study shows that an “evil” direction that cannot be extracted from a safety-tuned model can still be transferred from a fine-tuned variant, suggesting that intractability is partly protocol-dependent.1a:T691,SPARK has two parts: profiling and steering. It first estimates a behavioral boundary from accuracy as a function of task difficulty. It then measures hidden-state susceptibility by adding small noise to the input embeddings and tracking how strongly hidden states respond across layers. The key empirical finding is that raw susceptibility is not a clean reasoning signal because it is strongly coupled to prompt length. In many programmatic and algorithmic tasks, harder examples are simply longer, and the raw response attenuates with length.

To address this, the paper introduces length-controlled susceptibility. Instead of using the raw response, it compares each example to the expected response among inputs of similar length and uses the residual as a corrected activation signal. A positive residual indicates stronger-than-expected internal activation given the length; a negative residual suggests under-activation. SPARK then combines this corrected signal with cross-layer coordination to separate reasoning-active anchors from under-activated hard targets.

The steering stage extracts a reasoning direction from the active anchors, representing the latent shift from a baseline state to an active reasoning state. That direction is injected only into selected layers and only for the under-activated hard examples. This is the main methodological distinction from generic activation steering: intervention is diagnosis-guided, example-selective, and derived from active reasoning cases rather than from a global handcrafted direction. Layer selection is also informed by the susceptibility and coordination signals rather than applied uniformly across the network.1b:T5df,The main experimental asset is FRONTIER-4.5K, a 4,500-example programmatic reasoning benchmark with executable supervision and continuous difficulty annotations, covering symbolic composition, logical inference, and algorithmic reasoning. Algorithmic reasoning is the main diagnostic setting because it exhibits both a clear capability boundary and a strong length confound.

The results appear in three layers. First, the analysis result: raw hidden-state susceptibility decreases with input length and can therefore misrepresent reasoning quality. Length-controlled residual susceptibility is a better indicator of whether a hard example is genuinely under-activated. Second, the profiling result: corrected susceptibility plus cross-layer coordination can split hard failures into at least two groups, those that already show active internal reasoning signatures and those that do not. Third, the intervention result: SPARK-Steering improves performance on FRONTIER, GSM8K, and MATH-500, with larger gains on harder cases and less damage to easy cases than broad steering methods.

The provided text does not include trustworthy numerical tables, exact gains, model sizes, or full ablations, so those should be checked directly in the paper. The most important numbers to verify are the correlation changes before and after length correction, the quality of the anchor-target partition, and the gains over generic steering baselines under different layer-selection and direction-construction choices.1c:T561,Read this with one question in mind: is the paper really measuring latent reasoning activation, or just another proxy for prompt size and difficulty? Start with the introduction and method overview to understand the intended diagnosis target. Then go straight to the definition of susceptibility and the length-control procedure. That is the critical hinge of the paper. If the correction is weak, the rest of the profiling and steering story becomes much less convincing.

Next, inspect how FRONTIER-4.5K is built: how difficulty is annotated, why executable supervision matters, and why programmatic reasoning is a good setting for latent-state analysis. In the experiments, prioritize three pieces: plots showing the relationship between raw susceptibility and length, evidence that the corrected residual identifies under-activated hard cases, and the steering ablations comparing selective versus global intervention, layer choice, and direction source. A quick pass takes about 30 minutes. A careful read should also include appendix details on noise magnitude, layer aggregation, regression or normalization used for length control, and robustness checks. For pretraining-oriented readers, the main lesson is the negative one: many internal statistics that look like reasoning signals need explicit control for sequence length before they can support mechanistic claims.1d:T50f,Two complementary, weight-frozen, inference-time interventions are studied as both control mechanisms and causal interpretability probes.

(1) Neuron-level steering: Following a LAPE-style selection (Tang et al., 2024), the method computes per-dialect activation probabilities for each MLP neuron (ReLU-on frequency) and measures selectivity via the entropy of the normalized across-dialect activation distribution (low entropy = selective). Neurons are selected if they are highly active for at least one dialect (top n%) and highly selective (bottom m% LAPE). During decoding, activations of target-dialect neurons are multiplicatively amplified (α), while MSA-associated neurons are suppressed (γ), optionally also suppressing competitor dialect neurons (γ_comp).

(2) Distributed direction steering: A dialect direction is extracted from contrastive dialect–MSA pairs in activation/residual space, then injected during inference to bias generation. This targets a global, distributed representation rather than a small neuron subset.

Key claim: dialect information is both sparse and distributed. Sparse neurons exist (especially in late layers) but capture only a partial projection of the broader dialect direction, explaining why vector steering is more robust than neuron steering.1e:T4d8,Instead of relying on binary "behavior present / behavior absent" contrastive pairs, the method uses an iterative generation process to produce a sequence of samples with graded behavioral intensity: starting from a neutral seed response, the model is iteratively steered to produce responses that move incrementally towards and away from the target behavior, generating a continuous spectrum of samples from fully neutral to extreme behavioral expression. SAE features are extracted across this sample sequence, and only features that show monotonic linear activation scaling with behavioral intensity are retained as cascading linear features. This monotonicity criterion filters out spurious, incidentally correlated features and retains features with causal relevance to the behavior, a logic loosely aligned with slow feature analysis which prioritizes smoothly varying, stable signals over transient noise. Behavioral intensity is quantified as a linear sum of retained feature activations, producing a continuous, deterministic metric. Steering is performed by adjusting only these targeted feature activations, rather than manipulating the full dense residual stream, substantially reducing entanglement with unrelated capabilities.1f:T6bd,The method has three pieces. First, the paper constructs behavior directions in two ways: data-driven difference-in-means directions from activations, and hand-picked directions from lm_head rows associated with behavior-specific tokens. Detection is built both ways; intervention, especially refusal, is mainly derived from lm_head token directions. Second, the authors do causal testing rather than stopping at probing. They inject directions into the residual stream with forward hooks and measure behavioral changes layer by layer at inference time. Third, they analyze the geometry and the mechanism behind the mismatch. For formatting, detection and control collapse onto essentially the same axis. For hallucination, the cosine between detection and intervention is only about 0.12, roughly an 83-degree separation. They also rule out a construction artifact: even a detector built purely from activations, without hand-chosen tokens, remains misaligned with control, with cosine around -0.06. A key conceptual point appears later in the paper: cosine is not a steerability oracle. Detection is not a single privileged vector but a high-dimensional equivalence class, while steerability is functional. What matters is whether the control direction also serves as a detector, not whether one static angle is large. Mechanistically, the paper traces the hallucination mismatch to the output mapping, where an entity-copying pathway dominates the epistemic signal. As a result, pushing along the detection direction can strengthen name completion and make fabrication worse instead of triggering refusal.
