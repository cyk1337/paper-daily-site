18:T8ac,The method has two complementary intervention paths: lesioning and steering.

In the lesion path, the authors first train an aphasia subtype classifier on clinical data from AphasiaBank. The classifier labels generated text as resembling different aphasia profiles, such as Broca-like or Wernicke-like behavior. They then systematically ablate attention heads in frozen LLMs and use the classifier to identify which ablations induce which subtype-like deficits. The key point is that the intervention is not evaluated only by generic quality drop, but by the type of linguistic impairment it produces. This lets the authors associate some language functions, such as grammatical fluency or semantic coherence, with relatively discrete structural circuits.

In the steering path, they compute subtype-specific intervention vectors from residual-stream activation differences between processing human aphasic transcripts and healthy-control transcripts. Adding these vectors at selected layers during generation pushes the intact model toward the corresponding aphasia profile. They also test whether subtracting such directions can partially reverse lesion-induced deficits. Conceptually, lesioning targets structural hardware, while steering targets representational state.

The most important result is not a simplistic localization story. The paper finds a dual picture: some language functions can indeed be induced or disrupted through relatively specific circuits and directions, but those functions are also strongly entangled. Different aphasia subtypes share substantial overlap in their lesion-sensitive components, and their steering vectors have high cosine similarity. So the internal organization looks neither cleanly modular nor uniformly diffuse. It looks like a densely distributed system with vulnerable critical substructures.

A notable design choice is the use of a clinically informed classifier as the main diagnostic interface instead of standard LM metrics. The benefit is a structured taxonomy of degradation types. The cost is dependence on the classifier’s training distribution, which the authors acknowledge and partly offset by showing stronger generalization for steering interventions.19:T6e4,The experiments use AphasiaBank and focus on two elicitation tasks: Cinderella story retelling and sandwich-making instructions. These provide narrative and procedural speech settings with different linguistic demands. The model suite includes 11 LLMs from four families: GPT-2 XL, Llama-2-7B/13B-chat, Pythia 1B/1.4B/2.8B/6.9B, and Qwen3.5 0.8B/2B/4B/9B. Generation uses greedy decoding with a repetition penalty of 1.25 to avoid looping in base models.

The reported findings are mainly qualitative-structural rather than centered on one benchmark score. First, classifier-guided lesioning induces target aphasia-like subtypes more reliably than unguided or weaker intervention strategies, suggesting that some attention heads are more tightly linked to specific language failure modes. Second, steering can robustly induce corresponding aphasic behavior in intact models and appears to generalize better across tasks and prompts than structural lesioning. Third, there is substantial overlap across subtypes, both in the structural components implicated by lesioning and in the similarity of steering directions, supporting the paper’s dense-entanglement claim.

For a pretraining-oriented reader, the useful takeaway is that the authors turn language degradation into a structured, classifiable object and validate the pattern across multiple model families and scales. That said, the scope is limited. The study focuses mainly on attention-head ablations and residual-stream steering, not MLP circuits, multi-component pathways, or training-stage evolution. The clinical classifier may also conflate some stylistic distortions with aphasia-like deficits. So the results are better read as mechanistic probes than as strong evidence of cognitive equivalence.1a:T514,Start with the introduction and method. The result is a standard checkpoint with no custom hooks or extra forward computation at inference time.

Relative to static steering, WAQS can represent curved concept boundaries rather than a single hyperplane. Relative to dynamic nonlinear steering, it avoids per-token recomputation. The theoretical contribution is the strongest part: the paper argues that a smooth probe yields an affine gradient intervention if and only if the probe is a polynomial of degree at most two. In that sense, quadratic probes are not just one convenient absorbable family; they are the maximal absorbable class under this formulation.

The paper also gives a statistical interpretation under a Gaussian representation model. In that setting, the optimal quadratic probe has a closed form, and the induced steering decomposes into a difference of class-conditional Mahalanobis pulls. The analysis has three parts. First is representation detection: the paper uses linear probes, mean activation differences, and directly optimized candidate vectors to test whether the model contains linearly accessible signals of reasoning correctness. Here correctness is not just local token correctness; it is whether the current search state still permits a path to a correct final solution, which is especially nontrivial in the presence of backtracking. Second is localization: the authors do not restrict analysis to the final layer, because a final-layer signal may be readable but still be a poor intervention site once most computation has already happened. They search across positions, layers, and components. Third is causal testing: they inject the recovered directions into activations, or directly optimize vectors for improved final-answer likelihood, and then test whether those interventions actually improve reasoning.

The main innovation is not a new steering algorithm but a clean experimental design. Many steering papers work in large, noisy LLM settings where negative results are hard to interpret. Here the task is synthetic but nontrivial, the trajectories are structured, backtracking is explicit, and the model is small enough for exhaustive analysis. First, at the probing level, the model does contain statistically significant correctness information that is linearly decodable. Given activations at certain layers and positions, one can predict whether the current reasoning state is still on a path that can reach a correct solution. Second, at the intervention level, none of the candidate directions reliably improve reasoning. This holds for directions obtained from probes, from mean activation differences, and even from vectors directly optimized to increase the probability of a correct final answer. That is the central negative result: readable does not imply steerable.

The experimental setup uses a roughly 3M-parameter transformer trained from scratch on ChainSearch trajectories. Because the task has explicit search structure, evaluation can look not only at final success but also at local state properties along the trajectory. The paper systematically scans token positions, layers, and components to identify where correctness signals are strongest and where interventions might plausibly matter. One useful insight is that final-layer signals may be easiest to decode but are often poor intervention sites, since the upstream attention and MLP computations have already been completed. Another important result is that even directly optimizing a vector for better final-answer outcomes does not yield a stable steering direction, which weakens the objection that the failure is merely due to weak probe construction.

The scale is small, but the experimental logic is strong.
