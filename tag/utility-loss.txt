The paper is best read as an evaluation and problem-formulation piece that sharpens a failure mode many practitioners have seen but rarely isolated cleanly.19:T679,The key technical idea is controlled comparison. The paper defines safety alignment narrowly as post-training interventions such as safety-focused SFT, RLHF, or DPO, and excludes earlier-stage mechanisms like harmful-data filtering. For each aligned model, the authors use an unaligned counterpart as the reference for unconstrained utility. They then evaluate both on over-refusal datasets and compare responses pairwise to determine whether alignment introduced utility degradation.

This setup is stronger than the usual decoupled protocol where safety is measured on harmful prompts and utility on unrelated benign tasks. Standard benign tasks often do not activate safety filters at all, so they mostly capture broad side effects of alignment rather than the cost of active safety mechanisms. By contrast, over-refusal prompts are designed to engage safety behavior while remaining safe enough that helpfulness is still a meaningful target.

Another important design choice is that the paper does not reduce utility loss to explicit refusals. It also tracks implicit degradation: cases where both aligned and unaligned models comply, but the aligned answer is less informative, less specific, or less factually precise. The analysis further distinguishes genuinely low safety cost from superficially low cost caused by inactive safety filters in some languages. That distinction is important because a language can appear cheap to align simply because the safety system is barely operating there. Third, apparently low utility loss in some languages can be misleading, because it may simply reflect that safety filters are not meaningfully activated there.
