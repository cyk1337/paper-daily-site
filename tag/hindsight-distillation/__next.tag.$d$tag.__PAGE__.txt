9:T6f7,The method, Replay-Selected Hindsight Distillation (RSHD), has three main stages. First, after standard GRPO samples a group of completions for a problem and the verifier scores them, the same policy writes a short note for each failed attempt, describing what likely went wrong and what to try next. The note writer never sees the reference answer. These notes are not treated as supervision; they are prompts for targeted re-exploration. Second, the model re-solves the same problem with the note in context, generating fresh replay trajectories that are scored by the same final-answer verifier. A note is accepted only if its replayed attempts outperform the original group. This replay test is the key safeguard. It filters out plausible but misleading self-diagnoses. The paper reports that about one in nine notes explicitly states the answer, and roughly three in five fail the replay test, which makes the filtering step essential rather than optional. Third, successful replay trajectories from accepted notes are distilled into the plain-prompt policy, so the deployed model does not need notes at inference time. Importantly, this distillation branch is kept separate from GRPO’s own reward normalization. That separation avoids mixing two different signals: relative reward-based policy improvement and imitation of verified better behavior. Compared with higher-temperature retries, RSHD conditions exploration on a diagnosis of a specific failure. Compared with hindsight methods that reveal the answer, it avoids train-test leakage. Compared with direct self-reflection supervision, it requires external verification. Compared with re-scoring failed rollouts, it transfers only newly verified behavior rather than increasing the probability of the original mistake.a:T56f,The experiments cover competition math and free-form science QA, across multiple model families and sizes, comparing GRPO against GRPO plus RSHD. The most important result is not a single headline score but where the gains appear: improvements are strongest on the hardest problems and carry to out-of-domain benchmarks. The motivation is quantitatively grounded. If per-sample success probability is 0.1, an 8-sample group is all-fail more than 40% of the time. In the authors’ math training run, roughly half of the groups at the hardest MATH level carry no gradient. That explains why a method targeting all-fail groups can matter disproportionately. On cost, the extra overhead is training-time generation only, roughly doubling sampled tokens per step, with no inference-time change. The analysis also shows that a meaningful fraction of accepted notes come from all-fail groups, meaning the method recovers supervision exactly where vanilla GRPO has none. Another useful observation is that trained models solve problems in fewer tokens and hit the budget limit less often, suggesting that the distilled signal improves search efficiency, not just final accuracy.
