Each instance provides a problem, a reference solution, a candidate solution, and an expert human score. The authors draw two disjoint 200-example samples: an exploratory sample with seed 42 and a held-out validation sample with seed 7; they also report results on the full 1,000-example benchmark. All judges receive the same prompt and are asked to emit one of four coarse scores, {0, 1, 6, 7}, while the parser accepts any integer from 0 to 7. The primary metric is pass-agreement at the ≥6 threshold, with precision, recall, F1, and Spearman correlation as secondary measures. The cheap judges are GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and Gemma-4-31B; the frontier baselines are Claude Opus 4.7 and Gemini 3.1 Pro. The pre-specified ensemble is majority vote over the three cheap models. The models were selected not only for cost and accuracy but also for offsetting calibration biases, with Gemma tending to over-credit and DeepSeek-V4-Flash tending to under-credit. On the full benchmark, the authors also explore stricter consensus variants such as all-three-pass.1a:T44c,On the 200-example held-out validation set, the three low-cost judges achieve pass/fail agreement with human graders that is statistically indistinguishable from Claude Opus 4.7 and Gemini 3.1 Pro, while costing one to two orders of magnitude less, up to roughly 100 times cheaper in the reported setup. Majority vote performs well and matches the frontier baselines, but it does not improve over the strongest individual cheap judge. On the full 1,000-example benchmark, a post-hoc all-three-pass rule outperforms both cheap and frontier single judges on pass-agreement, precision, and stability, though this rule needs independent replication because it was introduced after the validation analysis. A practical detail matters: the cheap open-weight models are served through OpenRouter without pinning a provider, so quantization and backend changes can move a model’s pass-agreement by a few points across runs. First-party frontier baselines are more stable.
