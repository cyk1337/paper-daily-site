18:T73f,The method has three layers. The first is operator-level theory. The paper separates Transformer components into linear and nonlinear operators, and studies what happens when the input activation of an operator is compressed. The key result is that if the compressor is unbiased, then for a linear operator the induced gradient error is itself unbiased and does not propagate further upstream to earlier operators. For nonlinear operators, neither property holds. This distinction is the central theoretical contribution because it turns a vague engineering intuition into a concrete rule for module selection. The second layer is model-level convergence analysis. The authors study mini-batch SGD when all linear operators use activation compression, derive an upper bound on gradient variance, and decompose it into two additive terms: standard mini-batch sampling variance and compression-induced variance. The compression term is a sum over operators, controlled by the second-order magnitude of activation errors, so the total increase remains manageable rather than exploding layer by layer. Under the standard L-smoothness assumption, they further show that after the same number of updates, the stationarity metric degrades by at most a factor tied to the fourth root of the cumulative compression error. The third layer is the algorithm. The key observation is that once a linear operator's activation is low-rank, its weight gradient is naturally low-rank as well. The proposed method therefore jointly compresses activations and gradients by reusing the low-rank factors of compressed activations to maintain factorized weight gradients throughout training. This reduces both activation and gradient memory without introducing additional gradient error beyond what activation compression already causes, and with little to no extra computation.19:T51f,The experiments cover both Qwen and LLaMA families, and include pretraining datasets as well as fine-tuning datasets, which makes the evaluation broader than work limited to LoRA or SFT. The empirical study is organized around three questions. First, does the theory match practice: is compression on linear operators much safer than compression on nonlinear ones? Second, does joint activation-plus-gradient compression provide better memory savings than activation compression alone? Third, how does the method compare with other memory-efficient training approaches in the accuracy–compression trade-off? The reported conclusion is that the empirical results align well with the theory: compressing linear operators causes mild degradation, compressing nonlinear operators is much riskier, and the joint method achieves substantial memory savings while remaining competitive in model quality. The provided excerpt does not include the final numeric tables, so it would be unsafe to state exact compression ratios, throughput changes, or accuracy deltas. The most informative results are likely the ablations by operator type, the relation between compression rank or error magnitude and optimization degradation, and the comparisons against checkpointing, offloading, and prior low-rank activation methods.1a:T475,Read this one in the order of theory first, experiments second. Start with the problem setup and the operator-level analysis. Focus on the two criteria that drive the whole paper: whether the induced gradient is unbiased, and whether the error propagates upstream. Once those are clear, the rest of the design becomes easy to follow. Then move to the model-level variance bound and convergence guarantee. You do not need every proof detail on a first pass, but you should understand which terms depend on compression error and why the effect is additive rather than multiplicative across layers. 1) Key observation (linear-layer gradients lie in activation span): for a linear layer, the weight gradient is G_W = X^T G_out, hence it lies in the span/column space of input activations X. Therefore, if activations are approximated in a rank-r subspace, gradients admit an optimal rank-r representation in the same subspace.
2) Compress activations while keeping forward exact: OASIS does not modify forward-pass computation. It only projects and stores intermediate activations for backward:
- Store \tilde X_t = X_t U_t (N×r) instead of X_t (N×d).
- Perform gradient/optimizer computations in the low-rank space and project updates back via U_t.
3) Joint low-rank gradients and optimizer states: since gradients are kept as (r×m), Adam’s first/second moments can be stored and updated in the same low-rank space, reducing memory from O(dm) to O(rm) per layer.
4) Online subspace learning (core novelty): U_t cannot be fixed because activation distributions drift as parameters change. The paper uses Oja’s rule (streaming PCA) to update U_t incrementally without repeated exact eigendecomposition.
5) Projection-aware state updates: when U_t changes, low-rank momentum/variance states must be transformed to remain consistent with the new basis; otherwise optimization becomes unstable. Following LDAdam, they apply projection-aware alignment of optimizer states after subspace updates.
6) Cost/benefit: per layer, activation storage drops Nd→Nr; gradients and optimizer states drop dm→rm. Extra overhead comes from maintaining U_t and projections, aiming for large peak-memory savings with acceptable throughput/accuracy.1c:T4e9,(must_read: deep reading)
