18:T8e7,APEx maintains two levels of experience. Instance-level memory stores summarized execution traces for concrete queries, preserving operational details that can later be retrieved. Category-level skills aggregate many experiences within a category-modality bucket and distill them into reusable procedural guidance. At inference time, a query q is routed to a bucket, relevant memories R_M(q) and the corresponding skill R_S(q) are retrieved, and both are packed into a structured context E(q). The Planner uses E(q) to generate a research plan. The Executor follows that plan through multi-turn tool interaction and produces a trajectory τ and a final answer. After execution, the trajectory is summarized into a new memory entry, and once enough fresh experience accumulates, the Distiller updates the category-level skill.

The key idea is not just hierarchical memory, but the Executor-Distiller-Planner loop. Planner converts retrieved experience into a task-specific plan, Executor interacts with tools, and Distiller abstracts new trajectories into procedural skills. These modules are trained with a three-stage alternating GRPO schedule rather than a single joint objective. The motivation is cross-module credit assignment: if everything is optimized end-to-end at once, it is difficult for final task reward to shape the quality of distilled skills in a stable way. Alternating optimization gives each module a cleaner relative-reward signal while the others are partially fixed. In that sense, skill distillation is treated as reward-guided policy learning, not prompt-based summarization.

Another notable component is skill-guided TTRL. During deployment, the Planner performs test-time reinforcement learning on unlabeled queries, while retrieved skills and memories act as procedural priors that regularize online adaptation. The model is therefore not exploring from scratch at test time; it adapts locally around previously distilled high-quality procedures. The resulting improved trajectories are then written back into memory and can later trigger skill updates. This is the main conceptual difference from standard memory-augmented retrieval: retrieval helps the current sample, but APEx is designed so that the current sample also improves future behavior.19:T64b,The headline result reported in the paper is an average score of 64.4% across deep research benchmarks. This is 14.7 points above GPT-5.4 and 3.0 points above the strongest memory-augmented baseline. For judging the method, the second comparison matters more, because it isolates the gain from experience organization and training rather than from a stronger base model.

The paper also highlights a context-efficiency result: a skill-only variant remains competitive while injecting fewer memory tokens. That supports the claim that category-level procedural skills are a more compact carrier of useful experience than flat trajectory retrieval. This matters for long-context agents, where the bottleneck is often not retrieval quality but context budget and retrieval noise.

From the full text, the experimental story appears to rely on three main ablation axes. First, replacing hierarchical experience with flat memory retrieval hurts performance, suggesting that instance memories and category skills play different roles. Second, removing reward-guided distillation or the alternating GRPO schedule degrades both skill quality and downstream task performance, supporting the claim that useful skills must be shaped by task reward. Third, removing skill-guided TTRL weakens online adaptation, indicating that test-time updates without procedural priors become high-variance exploration. The abstract does not provide all ablation numbers, so the main tables and appendix are the right place to verify effect sizes, benchmark composition, tool setup, and the exact definition of the no-ground-truth reward.1a:T454,This is a skim paper rather than a line-by-line read. Then jump to the method overview figure and trace the data flow: query routing, two-level retrieval, the Planner-Executor-Distiller loop, and TTRL. After that, read the alternating GRPO section carefully enough to understand which module is optimized in each stage, where the reward comes from, and why this is expected to be more stable than joint training. Finish with the main results and ablations, checking three questions: does hierarchical experience beat flat retrieval, does the skill-only variant really save context, and is the TTRL gain robust.
