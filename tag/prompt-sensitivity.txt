18:T581,BenchDrift constructs meaning-preserving benchmark variations and attributes every correctness flip to the specific transformation that caused it. The pipeline has four roles: a generator proposes rephrasings, a validator checks that the original ground-truth answer still holds, the target model answers the original and validated variants, and a judge scores the outputs. This separation is important because it isolates wording effects from genuine changes in problem semantics.

Variations are organized along four axes: linguistic, referential, pragmatic, and structural. The paper defines positive drift as a wrong-to-right flip and negative drift as a right-to-wrong flip. Both are measured over the same denominator, the original problem set size, which makes the two directions directly comparable and allows exact decomposition into reported accuracy, best-case accuracy, and worst-case accuracy. That is a stronger framing than standard robustness reporting because it captures both hidden capability and hidden brittleness.

Methodologically, the paper also includes two important controls: one to test whether rephrasings merely simplify the problems, and another to test whether drift only affects already uncertain examples. In addition, the generator, validator, and judge are swapped across different language models to check that the observed drift is not an artifact of the instrumentation.19:T52c,The experiments cover 8 models and 3 benchmarks: GSM8K, MMLU, and MATH-Hard. The main empirical result is that drift is substantial in both directions across models and tasks. A single reported benchmark score is therefore only one point inside a wider interval defined by best-case and worst-case performance under meaning-preserving rephrasings.

A second key result is that phrasing sensitivity does not track baseline accuracy in a simple way. Stronger models are not uniformly more stable. Some high-performing models still lose many correct answers under specific transformation types. The paper also studies whether different models fail on similar transformations, which helps distinguish systematic sensitivity from random noise. The evidence suggests that at least part of the drift is structured and transformation-dependent rather than purely stochastic.

The reliability checks with swapped generator, validator, and judge models support the claim that the effect is not a pipeline artifact. The transformation-level attribution tables are especially useful because they show which kinds of rephrasing tend to reveal hidden capability and which tend to expose fragility. The abstract does not provide many headline percentages, so the full paper is best read through its drift-rate tables and breakdown figures.1a:T588,The method is a black-box search procedure for discovering spurious system prompts. A spurious prompt is defined by exclusion: it must not name or describe the target task, invoke domain-specific vocabulary, or use common reasoning cues such as asking the model to think carefully, verify answers, or eliminate options. At the same time, it must still instruct the assistant to answer the user’s query directly.

The search loop has three components: a generator produces initial candidate prompts, a validator filters them using manually specified lexical constraints, and a mutator iteratively rewrites top-performing candidates. Candidates are evaluated on disjoint training subsets, selected by top-K performance, and refined over multiple rounds. No gradients, logits, hidden states, or parameter updates are used; the procedure is fully black-box.

The important design choice is not search sophistication but the strict semantic exclusion. By aggressively removing task-related content from the prompt space, the paper tries to isolate non-semantic control factors such as tone, persona, ritual, protocol, or formatting. The paper then extends the analysis beyond accuracy gains to behavioral steering, showing that such prompts can bias the model toward first-option selection, wrong answers, or even/prime-number outputs in math settings. That makes the phenomenon more than a curiosity about odd prompts.1b:T4e8,The experiments cover reasoning and QA benchmarks, models from 0.8B to 27B, and three model families. The main result is that spurious prompts can match or outperform standard prompting baselines and PromptWizard, despite being intentionally stripped of task-specific semantics. The paper also argues that these prompts are semantically indistinguishable from unrelated text and are often shorter than task-aware optimized prompts, which weakens two simple explanations: that they are secretly task descriptions, or that they work merely because they are longer.

Transfer experiments are especially informative. The paper reports that effective spurious prompts often do not transfer cleanly across models or benchmarks, suggesting that they exploit highly specific interactions between a given model, a benchmark, and the prompt’s latent control structure. If they were simply activating a general reasoning mode, stronger transfer would be expected. The steering experiments are another key result because they show directional control, not just accidental performance variation.

This decomposition is useful because it localizes where failures may arise: graph encoding, projection/alignment, instruction understanding, or their interaction.

On top of that, the authors propose GTEval, an evaluation pipeline designed to probe graph-token understanding through instruction transformations rather than through new downstream tasks. The key idea is to vary instructions along two axes. Format-level transformations test whether the model is overly sensitive to superficial prompt rephrasing, template changes, or instruction organization. Content-level transformations test whether the model can actually adjust its use of graph tokens when the semantic content of the instruction changes, rather than relying on a fixed prompt-task association. The framework is applied to six representative GTokenLLMs.

The paper also includes internal analyses of token usage. It examines whether graph tokens are attended to across layers and how their utilization patterns change across models and instruction variants. The conclusion is nuanced: graph tokens are not ignored, and they do preserve task-relevant textual and structural information, but the way LLMs use them is unstable and often fails to generalize properly under instruction variation.

The authors further study extra instruction tuning and compare against Graph-Textualizing LLMs (GTextLLMs). Additional instruction tuning improves performance on original and seen instructions, but it does not solve the underlying understanding problem. GTextLLMs remain weaker on graph tasks despite stronger native language grounding, which suggests that the issue is not just language understanding capacity, but the fragility of how graph representations are aligned into the LLM space.1e:T680,The experiments cover six representative GTokenLLMs on text-attributed graph settings. The main findings are fourfold.

First, current GTokenLLMs do not fully understand graph tokens. Under format-level instruction changes, some models show large performance swings, indicating oversensitivity to prompt form. Under content-level changes, they can also be insufficiently responsive, indicating that they are not truly combining instruction semantics with graph-token semantics.

Second, these models rely heavily on textual attributes for reasoning, while their use of structural information is less robust. In other words, graph tokens may encode both text and topology, but the text component often dominates actual decision-making.

Third, attention and representation analyses show that graph tokens are not simply ignored. They are attended to across layers and retain task-relevant information. The problem is that their utilization patterns vary substantially across models and instruction variants, which points to weak and unstable semantic alignment.

Fourth, extra instruction tuning only partially helps. It improves performance on the original prompt and on seen instruction variants, but generalization to unseen transformations remains limited. That suggests the models are learning prompt adaptation more than genuine graph-token understanding.
