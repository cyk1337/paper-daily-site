18:T49b,The main experiments use Qwen3 models on math reasoning, with nine teacher-student configurations covering large-teacher/small-student, same-scale teacher-student, and small-teacher/large-student settings. Across these regimes, supervising a single randomly selected token per trajectory consistently improves reasoning. Selecting only one or two tokens with extreme teacher-student probability gaps often matches or outperforms dense OPD. The paper reports a non-monotonic sparsity curve: performance first degrades as supervision is reduced, but can recover under extreme sparsity and exceed dense supervision. Ablations show that the sign of the token signal matters. More expressive students benefit more from positive-reward tokens, while less expressive students in large-teacher/small-student settings benefit more from negative-reward tokens. Another important result is the mismatch between reasoning gains and reverse KL reduction: strong sparse OPD variants may increase the teacher-student reverse KL. Additional experiments on coding reasoning, Llama models, and other algorithmic settings are used to check that the phenomenon is not limited to Qwen3 math training.19:T526,An end-to-end pipeline:
- Web-graph construction: represent a web corpus as a directed hyperlink graph; improve out-link recall by merging a semantic mirror (RDF triples) with rendered markup.
- Task synthesis: sample an answer-anchored seed page, expand to a local subgraph, distill noisy pages into a connected entity graph, then generate a multi-hop question over that graph.
- Anchor removal / entity rewriting: abstract away directly searchable anchors so the task requires evidence composition across hops rather than single-query lookup.
- Dual-criteria verification: keep only tasks that are both sufficiently difficult and objectively checkable.
- Trajectory collection + filtering: roll out a strong teacher, then apply multi-stage filtering at both trajectory and turn levels to remove low-signal steps (irrelevant browsing, broken evidence chains, hallucinated citations).
- Training: start with filtered SFT; then do RL against live web search with an in-cluster judge and an observation summarizer; use iterative “SFT–RL climbing” where successful RL rollouts are fed back into the next SFT round to stabilize learning and improve sample efficiency.
- Evaluation: hold tool interface, context budget, and judging fixed; compare two inference regimes (with vs without CM) to quantify harness impact.1a:T665,The authors fix a standard OPD setup and then vary the data. OPD supervises the student on trajectories sampled from the current student policy, matching the student distribution to the teacher distribution along student-generated prefixes. The paper starts from the sequence-level reverse KL and uses its exact token-level decomposition. In practice, the main implementation is student top-k OPD: at each step, compute the KL only over the k tokens with highest student probability, after renormalizing both student and teacher on that subset. This choice is practical: much cheaper than full-vocabulary KL, but denser than sampled-token supervision.

On top of this setup, the paper runs three layers of analysis. First is 1-shot OPD: sample a single training example and ask whether training on only that example can still improve the student, and whether it overfits. Second is difficulty analysis: bucket examples by difficulty and compare the downstream gains from easy, medium, and hard subsets, including examples beyond the capability frontier of both teacher and student. Third is mechanism analysis: test whether the gains are explained by high token entropy or by long CoT trajectories. The evidence supports the latter. The paper also analyzes token categories and finds that alignment improves especially on structural reasoning tokens such as “Alternative” and “Wait,” suggesting that the student is learning reasoning organization patterns. Based on these observations, the authors propose a simple difficulty-driven data selection rule: train on hard examples rather than trying to cover a large generic dataset.1b:T4d4,The experimental story is straightforward. The default setup uses DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B as the student and JustRL-DeepSeek-1.5B as the teacher, with top-k OPD as the main training objective. First, 1-shot OPD consistently helps across sampled single examples, and training remains stable up to 2,000 steps without obvious overfitting or policy collapse. Second, hard examples outperform easy and medium ones. More strikingly, even when those hard examples are so difficult that training accuracy stays at 0% for both teacher and student, validation accuracy still keeps improving. That strongly suggests the useful signal lies in the process, not the final correctness. Third, the paper argues against a simple high-entropy-token explanation and instead shows that longer CoTs lead to better teacher-student alignment over long horizons and expose rare reasoning patterns such as self-reflection. Fourth, the data-selection result is the headline number: for a 1.5B student, training on only 8 selected hard examples reaches 53.6% validation accuracy, essentially matching the 17K full-data baseline at 53.7%. The authors also report that this extreme data efficiency generalizes across architectures and scales from 1.5B to 7B.1c:T411,Read it by following the questions rather than the sections. Start with the OPD formulation and make sure the top-k subset KL is clear; otherwise it is hard to judge how implementation-specific the conclusions are. Then go straight to the 1-shot OPD experiments and training curves, focusing on why a single example does not immediately cause overfitting. Next read the difficulty-bucket results and the plots where training accuracy remains 0% while validation still improves; that is the most informative part of the paper. If you want to dig deeper, the right follow-up questions are whether hardness is confounded with CoT length, whether the conclusions transfer beyond top-k OPD to full-vocab or sampled-token variants, and whether the same “few long high-value trajectories” phenomenon could inform pretraining curricula or synthetic-data filtering.1d:T563,XMerge has two components. The first is cross-axis selection. Each block is scored along two axes: relative magnitude, which measures the size of the residual displacement, and Block-Influence, which measures directional change between input and output hidden states. The two scores are standardized across eligible layers and combined with a maximum. A block is considered quiet only when neither axis is large. This max rule is intentional: an average could hide a large angular change with a small magnitude score, or the reverse. The second component is reconstructive layer merging. Instead of dropping a layer, analytically folding weights, or adding a learned replacement module, XMerge distills the original adjacent two-block mapping into one existing standard Transformer block. For calibration inputs, it caches the pair input h_l and the original pair output h_{l+2}, then optimizes all parameters of the surviving block so that the single block maps h_l directly to h_{l+2}. After k merges, the result is an ordinary L-k-layer Transformer with no extra inference-time parameters, no task labels, and no end-to-end fine-tuning. The closest contrast is with methods such as ReplaceMe, LLM-Streamline, and analytic merge/fold approaches; XMerge is distinctive because it reconstructs with a full existing nonlinear standard block while preserving the serving interface.1e:T7a7,The pipeline has four components. First is data curation. The authors collect 22,000 problems from 16 regional and international competition families plus online platforms, package them into executable evaluation environments, and explicitly exclude and deduplicate evaluation problems. They then use DeepSeek-V4-Flash to generate large synthetic reasoning-trace corpora: about 1.2M traces for the Nano model and 477,642 for the larger model. The key point is not just scale, but the organization of problems, reasoning, code, and feedback into long-context supervised examples.

Second is supervised fine-tuning. They start from Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B and Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B-A55B and perform long-context SFT to obtain competition-specialized models. Nano is a 30B-parameter MoE with 3B active parameters; Ultra is 550B with 55B active. The supervision appears to target not only final code generation but also intermediate problem-solving traces.

Third is reinforcement learning. RL is applied to Nano-CC, with rewards derived from executable evaluation, namely whether the generated program passes tests and how much score it obtains. This reward is attractive because it is automatic and closely aligned with the task objective. The downside is that it may favor search-friendly behaviors rather than robust one-shot generalization. The paper places RL after SFT, which is sensible: the action space in competitive programming is too large for pure RL to be stable without a strong initial policy.

Fourth is GenCorrect, the test-time closed-loop strategy. It is more than self-consistency. The system generates diverse candidate solutions, evaluates them, uses the feedback to refine later attempts, and operates under a limited submission budget. This is essentially verifier-guided search plus iterative program repair. It is especially well matched to IOI-style settings, where multiple submissions and partial credit are part of the environment.1f:T704,The main results are straightforward. It is a strong post-training and inference-system paper whose value for pretraining lies in what it reveals about executable supervision and reward quality.21:T524,The method is Adaptive Multi-Reward Projection, or AMRP. Instead of using a fixed weighted sum of reward dimensions throughout training, AMRP updates the projection weights online based on training dynamics. It uses three signals.

The first is relative shortfall, which measures whether a reward dimension is lagging behind the others. If a dimension remains under-optimized, its weight is increased. The second is reward volatility, which tracks instability in a dimension. High volatility suggests that the policy has not stabilized there, so AMRP keeps attention on it. The third is recent progress, which measures whether a dimension is improving, stagnating, or regressing. Saturated and stable dimensions get down-weighted; stagnant or regressing dimensions get up-weighted.

This design addresses the two identified failure modes in a practical way. It does not eliminate the information loss of scalarization entirely, but it reduces persistent aliasing by making the projection time-varying rather than static. More importantly, it counters shortcut lock-in by reallocating learning pressure toward neglected dimensions. Compared with manual reward tuning or simple normalization, the key difference is that AMRP is driven by observed reward dynamics during training rather than by fixed prior assumptions.22:T4a0,The evaluation covers three multi-reward settings: rule-based mathematical reasoning, citation-grounded generation with automatic evaluators, and open-ended alignment using learned reward-model scores. The main empirical claim is that AMRP consistently improves downstream performance across all three, with especially strong gains in math reasoning and citation-grounded generation, and smaller but still consistent gains in the more correlated open-ended alignment setting.

The paper also provides mechanism-level evidence. It shows that under static aggregation, training is often dominated early by dense rewards such as format or length, while harder dimensions such as correctness remain under-optimized. AMRP changes the reward-profile dynamics by shifting weight toward lagging or unstable dimensions, which leads to more balanced profiles over time. Another useful result is compatibility: the method composes with PPO, GRPO, and GDPO, suggesting that it is best viewed as a reward-aggregation module rather than a replacement for the RL optimizer itself. The joint objective is to maximize verifier reward over trajectories induced by both model parameters θ and harness h. Directly updating both at once creates a credit-assignment problem: harness search is a slow proposal-evaluate-accept loop, while online finetuning alternates rollout collection and gradient updates at a much faster cadence. WHALE avoids this mismatch by decomposing the problem into two conditional subproblems:
1) update weights with the harness fixed;
2) search for a better harness with the updated model fixed.

For the weight phase, the paper uses online RSFT. Under the current harness, the model samples multiple trajectories per prompt, keeps only verifier-accepted rollouts, and trains with supervised learning on those accepted traces. This choice is more stable than direct policy-gradient style updates and fits existing SFT pipelines well.

For the harness phase, the paper uses Meta-Harness to search over executable harness programs while keeping the model fixed. The search space includes system instructions, tool schemas, context management, parser/execution logic, and termination policy, which is much broader than prompt-only optimization.

The paper also introduces adaptive WHALE. A fixed schedule must decide how long each phase should run, but that is hard to tune: if a phase is too short, it lacks evidence; if too long, it over-optimizes against a frozen counterpart. Adaptive WHALE enforces a minimum phase length, then switches when the current phase’s training signal stops improving. This is a sensible design because the bottleneck can shift during training.

Compared with prior joint-adaptation work, the main differences are clear. Earlier methods typically co-optimize weights and textual prompts. WHALE expands the optimization target to full executable harnesses and uses conditional alternation rather than concurrent co-adaptation, prioritizing stable attribution over raw update frequency.26:T7b3,The evaluation covers three domains: SearchQA, mathematical reasoning with code execution, and chess puzzles. Baselines include weight-only optimization, harness-only optimization, and Fast-Slow Training (FST). Importantly, their FST implementation uses the same update methods and schedule as WHALE, but restricts the search space to system/user prompts. This isolates the effect of searching over a full harness.

Main results: WHALE improves over single-component baselines by 7.67 to 24.38 percentage points, and over FST by 4.15 to 13.00 points. That suggests the gain is not just from extra tuning, but from the alternating scheme plus the richer harness search space.

A particularly useful finding is the existence of different bottleneck regimes:
- In SearchQA, harness-only search can match the peak accuracy of weight-only training with far fewer rollouts. This indicates a harness-limited regime, where retrieval and context/control logic dominate.
- In Math, harness-only search barely helps until a small weight update is applied first. After that, the same harness search becomes effective. This is a weight-limited regime, where the model’s reasoning or code-use ability must cross a threshold before harness improvements matter.

This observation explains why stagewise optimization is weak: it assumes the bottleneck stays on one side for a long stretch, while the experiments show that the bottleneck can move.

The paper also compares alternating updates against stagewise optimization directly. Under matched budgets, small alternating updates outperform stagewise by 5.32 points on SearchQA and 9.16 points on Math, and surpass the final stagewise result after using only 29% and 49% as many rollouts, respectively.
