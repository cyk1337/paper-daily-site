9:T5d8,There are two main components. First, FakeContext-bench. Each instance contains demonstrations consistent with a contextual rule P, a query, and two reference answers: y_R under the real rule and y_P under the misleading contextual rule. Outputs are categorized into Reality, Context, or Other using a deterministic rule-based judge, with human validation of the scoring protocol. The benchmark spans seven domains: physics, mathematics, chemistry, biology, statistics, history, and computer science. The point is not domain difficulty, but controlled construction of cases where the context is coherent yet lacks real authority.

Second, Jurisdiction In-Context Learning (J-ICL). The method keeps the standard few-shot interface and uses only query-only LM loss. In J+ episodes, the demonstrated rule genuinely applies to the query, so the correct answer should follow the context. In J- episodes, the demonstrations still support a coherent rule, but that rule is private, conflicting, or undeployed, while the query requires the reality-grounded or formally valid answer. No explicit authority labels, auxiliary classifier, or rationale supervision is added. The model is expected to infer applicability from natural-language cues and episode structure. This is the key distinction from methods such as MetaICL or Symbol Tuning, which mainly improve rule extraction from demonstrations. J-ICL tries to train both rule induction and rule applicability judgment within the same prompting format.a:T63d,The empirical story has three important parts. The authors distinguish two safety settings. In environment-risk tasks, the user goal is benign but the interface contains hazards; the correct behavior is safe continuation, not refusal. In explicit-misuse tasks, the goal itself is harmful and should be refused. This distinction is central, because collapsing both into a single refuse/not-refuse label would push the model toward conservative shutdown behavior.

To support this, they introduce SCOPE-Gen, an automated pipeline that starts from normal GUI tasks and injects detectable forbidden events or risky interface elements while preserving the original user goal and completion evaluator. This produces paired tasks and three complementary trajectory types: capability trajectories for ordinary execution, risk-handling trajectories for safe continuation under environmental hazards, and refusal trajectories for harmful goals or no-safe-path cases. The reward is defined as the product of completion reward and safety reward, so full credit requires both successful completion and hazard avoidance.

Training proceeds in two stages. First, joint SFT teaches the model all three behaviors together, establishing a conditional execution policy. Second, capability-oriented online RL further improves task completion while monitoring whether safety is retained. The design choice is deliberate: use supervised data to shape the decision boundary first, then use RL to push capability, rather than expecting RL to discover the right safety-capability tradeoff from scratch.
