18:T545,There are two central ideas. The first is a within-item contrast between original benchmark items and semantically equivalent paraphrases. Since the intended skill is held fixed, a systematic advantage on the original item can be interpreted as memorization of surface form rather than genuine capability. This is framed as differential item functioning in psychometric terms. The second is calibration: instead of treating the paraphrase gap as an ungrounded heuristic, the authors fine-tune 74 models with known doses of contamination and use them to map observed original-versus-paraphrase gaps to estimated contamination levels.

The paper then shifts from per-model detection to leaderboard-level analysis. It distinguishes uniform contamination, where many models benefit similarly and rankings stay stable, from differential contamination, where some models benefit more and rankings can change. Within an IRT-style anchor-linking view, contaminated items are non-invariant anchors. The analysis therefore asks whether removing or accounting for those items materially changes model ordering. This is the main departure from string overlap methods, membership inference statistics such as Min-K%, or canary-based memorization tests. Those methods can indicate exposure, but they do not directly answer whether the leaderboard itself is wrong.19:T55b,The empirical setup has two stages. First comes calibration. The authors build 74 fine-tuned models with known contamination doses and test whether the paraphrase-gap signal is sensitive, monotonic, and interpretable. This is an important part of the paper because many contamination studies lack calibration, making null results hard to interpret. Second comes measurement on real models. They collect per-instance responses from 47 publicly released models and evaluate them on ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, and MMLU using both original items and semantically equivalent paraphrases.

The main result, consistent with the title and the body of the paper, is that contamination does inflate absolute scores but rarely reorders large-model leaderboards. In other words, the dominant pattern is shared inflation rather than rank-changing differential advantage. The key evidence is not just the presence of contamination signals, but the relative stability of leaderboard order after accounting for suspect items or invariance violations. The excerpt provided does not include the full result tables, so I will not invent exact point gains, correlation values, or Kendall tau numbers.
