18:T811,SpecGuard assumes the serving stack already uses speculative decoding. A small draft model proposes tokens, and the target model verifies them. The accept/reject sequence, normally used only for acceleration, is treated as an online measure of draft–target agreement. Under benign inputs, disagreement mostly reflects ordinary modeling mismatch. Under an activated backdoor, the target shifts toward the attacker-controlled response while a clean draft continues along the benign trajectory, so rejection increases and the per-query acceptance rate drops.

The base detector is simple: compute a query-level acceptance-rate score and compare it against a baseline estimated from clean traffic. The paper then extends this raw signal into a more deployable recipe. If the draft is also poisoned, low acceptance alone is no longer sufficient, so the detector uses two-sided abnormality scoring. If the trigger only affects a sparse segment of the response, whole-query averaging dilutes the signal, so the method adds multi-scale alarms over different windows. If traffic distribution shifts over time, fixed thresholds drift, so the paper recommends rolling calibration. If an adaptive attacker appends benign continuation after the malicious response to dilute the mean, early-token statistics become more informative than full-response averages.

The theory links acceptance behavior to distributional discrepancy between draft and target. Intuitively, speculative acceptance depends on how much probability the target assigns to draft-proposed tokens, so an acceptance-rate gap reflects increased divergence, discussed through total variation style arguments. The paper further derives a potency–stealth tradeoff: to hide from the detector while keeping the attack active, the attacker must reduce probability mass on tokens that the clean draft would not favor, which directly weakens the malicious behavioral shift. The theory is not trying to cover every adaptive strategy, but it gives a principled explanation for why this nearly free signal should work.19:T509,The empirical section is broader than the abstract suggests. The evaluation spans four backdoor types, three model families, multiple scales up to 32B, and several decoding settings rather than a single speculative-decoding configuration. The headline result is that SpecGuard achieves strong per-query detection of activated backdoors while remaining competitive with runtime monitors that require an extra generation pass, but with almost no additional inference-time cost.

The most informative results are the stress tests. In poisoned-draft or self-drafting settings, the signal does not simply disappear; instead, the anomaly can become two-sided because draft and target may agree on the malicious path, motivating two-sided scoring. In sparse trigger-timing settings, whole-response averages understate the effect, and multi-scale statistics recover sensitivity. Under input-distribution shift, rolling calibration is more reliable than a static threshold learned offline. Under adaptive padding attacks, where benign text is appended after the malicious answer, early-token statistics recover part of the lost signal.
