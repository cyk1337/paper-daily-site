9:T806,The central method is controlled fixed-trace replay. In standard end-to-end evaluation, compression affects both the generation of the reasoning trace and the later use of that trace, so it is hard to tell whether failure comes from not producing the right reasoning or from not being able to use already-produced reasoning. The authors decouple these factors. First, a Full-KV model generates a complete reasoning trace without compression. Then each compression method is evaluated by prefilling the same question and the same textual trace, protecting the question cache, compressing only the KV states corresponding to the reasoning trace, and resuming decoding immediately after the closing reasoning tag. This means every method sees identical reasoning content in text form; differences reflect whether the compressed KV representation still lets the model use that content effectively.

On top of this protocol, the paper evaluates three metrics. The first is standard final-answer accuracy. The second is answer–chain consistency, which measures whether the final answer remains aligned with the visible reasoning chain. The third is perturbation faithfulness, which probes whether the compressed model responds appropriately when intermediate reasoning content is perturbed in controlled ways. The authors are careful not to claim that KV states physically decompose into separate “answer” and “evidence” components; the distinction is behavioral rather than mechanistic.

The experimental suite covers ten token-eviction compression methods and one quantization method, across three models, multiple tasks, and multiple compression budgets. Tasks include mathematical reasoning, scientific QA, clinical calculation, and long-context retrieval. The paper also reports end-to-end results to show that the observed pattern is not an artifact of the replay protocol. Relative to prior KV-compression work, the novelty is not a new compression algorithm but a stronger evaluation lens that exposes a blind spot in accuracy-only reporting.a:T778,The main result is not that one compression method dominates, but that different evaluation axes separate in meaningful ways. On reasoning-heavy tasks such as AIME, GPQA-Diamond, and MedCalc, some methods remain competitive if judged only by final-answer accuracy under certain compression budgets. However, answer–chain consistency and perturbation faithfulness degrade earlier and more sharply. This indicates that compressed models may still recover the right answer from residual answer-like cues, local summaries, or conclusion-adjacent signals, while no longer preserving the reasoning structure needed to justify or verify that answer. In that sense, accuracy is an asymmetric diagnostic: when it collapses, compression damage is obvious; when it holds up, evidence preservation is still uncertain.

A second important finding is task dependence. On retrieval-oriented tasks such as RULER QA, where correct answers depend more directly on retaining exact supporting evidence, compression damage is more visible through accuracy collapse itself, so the answer–evidence gap is less hidden. By contrast, on math, science QA, and clinical calculation, the model can often preserve the final answer while losing auditability of the chain.

The study also compares multiple token-eviction methods and one quantization baseline across different model families. The answer–evidence gap appears across methods and models rather than as a quirk of a single algorithm. The abstract does not expose all numeric tables, but the paper structure indicates that Table 2 reports end-to-end results, while the main result tables and figures show how accuracy, consistency, and faithfulness diverge across budgets, tasks, and methods. When reading, the most informative pattern to look for is ranking reversals: methods that look similar under accuracy but separate clearly once evidence-oriented metrics are included.b:T51e,Treat this as an evaluation-protocol paper, not as a new compression-method paper.
