18:T7d8,There are three important design choices. First, evaluation is suite-level rather than item-level. Each episode is a six-problem contest under one total budget, spanning mathematics, competitive programming, and abstract reasoning, with 50 contests per domain. Problems are drawn from sources such as Omni-MATH, MathNet, and LiveCodeBench Pro, and mixed to induce heterogeneous resource demands. Second, the benchmark has both tool-free and agentic settings. In the tool-free setting, the budget is output tokens; in the agentic setting, the budget is actions in an interactive shell environment. This lets the authors compare pure generation-time allocation with tool-using allocation. Third, and most importantly, the paper uses matched single-problem calibration. For each problem, the same model is run in isolation under varying budgets to obtain empirical response curves. From these runs, the authors build two controls: an equal-allocation replay and a same-model empirical oracle. Equal allocation simply divides the total budget evenly across the six problems. The oracle is an offline diagnostic that selects a feasible set of observed single-problem successes under the contest budget, representing what the model has already shown it can do if allocation were ideal. Because the oracle is built from demonstrated successes of the same model, the gap between oracle and contest score is evidence of allocation failure rather than missing capability. The paper also studies lightweight online schedulers. Some improve mean performance in certain model-domain cells, but no fixed scheduler transfers uniformly across domains, suggesting that allocation policy is strongly domain-dependent. The trajectory analysis is also useful: under strong pressure, failures are often caused by exhausting the shared budget on other problems; under moderate pressure, failures more often come from stopping after partial progress. Both are allocation errors on problems the model can already solve in isolation.19:T537,The main empirical pattern is unusually clean. So shared-budget competence is not well captured by standard capability rankings.
