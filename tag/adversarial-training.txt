18:T6bd,The method has three main ingredients. The first is an agentic red-teaming harness. Instead of asking the attacker for a single-shot jailbreak prompt, the system gives it a stateful defender_model tool so it can probe, observe, revise, and retry. This matters because realistic prompt injection often depends on multi-turn interaction, tool outputs, file edits, and context-specific weaknesses that a one-pass generator will miss. The second ingredient is environment construction. Existing capability tasks are converted into adversarial RL environments under a realistic threat model: during the defender’s rollout, the attacker may be allowed to edit a designated tool response, a local file region, or another controlled insertion point. This keeps the attack surface close to real agent deployments while making the environment family extensible as production tasks expand. The third ingredient is large-scale self-play RL. The base game rewards the attacker for inducing valid failures and rewards the defender for resisting the attack while still completing its original task. Crucially, training is not limited to a single attacker-defender pair. The attacker is trained against a diverse, simultaneously trained population of strong defenders. That design is meant to prevent overfitting to one defender’s quirks and instead teach the attacker to identify model-specific weaknesses. The paper also frames scaling along three axes: inference-time compute through iterative search in the harness, environment diversity through many red-teaming scenarios and goals, and training compute through a very large RL run. The resulting GPT-Red is therefore a search-based adaptive attacker, not a prompt-template library.19:T51b,The main empirical message is that GPT-Red finds more working attacks, transfers better, and keeps improving with additional test-time compute. The paper reports that it can reliably break prior GPT-5-series models up to GPT-5.5, outperform human red-teamers and a GPT-5.5 baseline on the 2025 IPI Challenge style setting, and transfer to real systems such as an AI-powered vending machine. Another important result is longitudinal rather than benchmark-specific: progressively stronger internal red-team models were used over roughly six months in the training of each production model since GPT-5.2, culminating in GPT-5.6 achieving the strongest robustness profile in their internal evaluation suites. The public text does not fully disclose all run details such as parameter counts, RL steps, or complete absolute metrics, but several experimental signals are especially informative. Test-time scaling is strong, which suggests the attacker is using search and adaptation rather than memorized attack strings. Training against a defender population improves generalization, indicating that the attacker is learning broader attack principles rather than exploiting one model’s idiosyncrasies. 1) CAT formalization in theory: they introduce an explicit trainable embedding matrix and generate adversarial examples by perturbing the resulting input embeddings (rather than discrete tokens), mirroring PGD-style CAT.

2) Robust generalization bound: within this “ICL embedding adversarial training” framework, they prove an upper bound on robust generalization. 3) Spectral mechanism: the bound depends on the embedding matrix singular values; overly large or overly small singular values worsen the bound. This yields a concrete design rule: keep the embedding spectrum well-conditioned (neither exploding nor collapsing).

1) Motivation: FM learns the marginal velocity field of a probability flow by minimizing an L2/MSE loss between predicted and target conditional velocities. While infinite-capacity models can recover the true flow, finite-capacity models trained under a fixed Euclidean metric may generalize off the data manifold, yielding OOD samples. Using a fixed perceptual network as the metric can be exploited (“generator hacking”), motivating joint training of the criterion.
2) Method: Continuous Adversarial Flow Models (CAFMs) introduce adversarial training into continuous-time flow modeling. Unlike discrete-time Adversarial Flow Models (AFMs), CAFMs operate in continuous time (ODE/CNF) and replace the fixed MSE criterion with a learned discriminator to guide learning. This changes the effective generalized distribution induced under finite capacity and empirically yields samples closer to the target distribution.
3) Training usage: Primarily proposed as post-training (finetuning) of existing FM models for efficiency, though it can also train from scratch. The paper emphasizes FM and CAFM target the same ground-truth flow; differences arise mainly from finite-capacity generalization under different objectives.
4) Intuition: Discriminators are more sensitive to perceptual details (texture, sharpness, contours) than pointwise L2 losses and may better capture manifold structure; jointly learned criteria mitigate fixed-metric exploitation.1d:T434,(must_read: deep reading)
