9:T4c7,The main contribution is the dataset and evaluation protocol. The authors start from protocol versions modified by scientists on Benchling and compare them against published source protocols. The observed differences are converted into first-person scientist queries and weighted rubrics. A correct answer is expected not only to propose the modification itself, but also to address downstream implications. The benchmark contains 149 tasks derived from 96 published protocols across nine wet-lab biology domains. Each task is reviewed by at least two independent scientists with a relevant PhD and at least three years of bench experience. Review criteria include question relevance, scientific soundness, rubric reasoning depth, rubric scientific accuracy, and overall quality; only high-quality tasks are retained. During evaluation, models receive the query, the source protocol, and internet access, and produce a free-form answer. Scoring is done with an LLM judge that evaluates each rubric criterion separately on a 0/1/2 scale, then aggregates with criterion weights into a normalized score. The paper also reports grader-consistency checks and intentionally uses a coarse scoring scale to improve judge stability.a:T442,Nine models are evaluated, including Claude Opus 5, the GPT-5.6 family, Gemini 3.6 Flash, Grok 4.5, Kimi K3, GLM 5.2, and Inkling. Each task is attempted ten times. Invalid outputs, such as formatting failures or refusals, are assigned zero and tracked separately. Models are given the query, the published protocol, and web access; proprietary models use their native search tools, while open models use Exa. Performance is reported as a normalized weighted-rubric score between 0 and 1. From the available text, the paper emphasizes benchmark quality and evaluation protocol more than headline model gaps. The key empirical points are: (1) tasks come from real protocol modifications rather than synthetic expert-authored prompts, (2) scoring is criterion-wise and weighted, which makes open-ended evaluation more structured, and (3) grader agreement was checked across representative judge models. For exact model rankings and breakdowns by intent or biology domain, the tables and appendices would need to be consulted, since the provided text does not include the full numeric results.b:T6e9,The method has two central ideas. First, a round-trip editing primitive. Starting from a seed document s, the benchmark defines a forward instruction x→ and an inverse instruction x←. The model applies the forward edit to obtain t, then applies the inverse edit to reconstruct ŝ. If the model both follows instructions and avoids unintended changes, ŝ should match s. Because exact string match is too crude across heterogeneous document types, the benchmark uses domain-specific similarity functions tailored to formats such as code, configuration files, subtitles, crystallography files, and music notation.

Second, the paper composes these reversible edits into a relay. Since each round-trip should ideally return to the original seed, multiple round-trips can be chained to simulate long delegated workflows. This turns long-horizon reliability into a measurable degradation curve: reconstruction quality after 2, 4, ..., 20 interactions. That is the key move that makes cumulative corruption visible.

The benchmark covers 52 professional domains and 310 work environments across five broad categories. Each environment contains a real seed document, 5–10 complex edit tasks, and distractor context to mimic realistic working conditions. Each edit is executed in an independent single-turn session. This choice isolates the model’s native document-editing reliability rather than mixing in persistent agent state or external verification. The paper also validates that the backtranslation-style setup does not reward trivial copying: models must actually perform the forward edit, otherwise they fail the task. The appendices discuss assumptions behind reversibility and the limits of similarity metrics, which are important for trusting the benchmark.c:T61e,The large-scale study evaluates 19 LLMs on DELEGATE-52. The headline result is that current models steadily corrupt documents under long delegated workflows. Even frontier systems such as Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude 4.6 Opus, and GPT 5.4 lose about 25% of document content on average after 20 interactions. Averaged across all tested models, degradation is around 50%.

The paper defines a model as ready for delegated work in a domain if it retains at least a 98% reconstruction score after 20 interactions. Under that criterion, Python is the only domain, among 52, where most models are close to being usable. This domain pattern is informative: strongly structured and easily verifiable formats like Python and databases are much more robust, while natural-language-heavy or niche symbolic formats such as earning statements and music notation are much more fragile.

Several targeted analyses are especially useful. Longer documents, longer workflows, and distractor context all worsen degradation, and the effects compound over time rather than staying local to a single step. Performance after only two interactions is not predictive of performance after twenty, which shows why short-horizon benchmarks are misleading for delegated work. The paper also tests a basic agentic harness and finds no meaningful improvement, suggesting that the bottleneck is not simply prompting or orchestration but the model’s underlying editing fidelity.
