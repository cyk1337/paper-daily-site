9:T7b3,The method has two main components. First, multi-noise training. Standard TARFlow trains by maximum likelihood on noisy inputs and derives a score for denoising from the gradient of the learned log-density with respect to the input. iTARFlow no longer commits to one fixed noise level. Instead, it trains the same Transformer autoregressive flow across a range of noise scales, so the model explicitly learns the noisy data distribution at multiple corruption levels. The motivation is straightforward: high noise supports coarse global synthesis, while lower-noise scores are better suited for recovering fine detail. A single noise level forces the model to compromise between the two.

Second, iterative inference. Sampling starts from a high-noise autoregressive generation step, preserving the sequential generation structure of the flow. Then, instead of applying a single Tweedie-style cleanup, the model performs iterative denoising across the trained noise levels. The refinement updates are driven by scores obtained implicitly from the likelihood parameterization. This is the key distinction from diffusion. Diffusion models are trained to predict noise or score directly and sample by integrating a reverse stochastic or deterministic process from pure noise. iTARFlow keeps end-to-end exact likelihood training throughout; score-based denoising appears only as an inference-time refinement mechanism derived from the density model.

Architecturally, the paper is effectively splitting generation into two roles: autoregressive flow for globally coherent coarse synthesis, and iterative parallel denoising for local cleanup. Compared with discrete autoregressive image models, it avoids tokenizer-induced information loss. Compared with diffusion, it preserves a likelihood-based training objective. Compared with classical one-shot flow sampling, it accepts that a single inverse pass is not enough for visual quality and adds a controlled refinement stage.a:T674,The experimental story appears to center on three points. First, the paper systematically demonstrates the 'noise dilemma'. Different training noise levels produce qualitatively distinct failure modes: low-noise training leads to over-detailed, texture-heavy samples, while high-noise training improves global structure but becomes blurry after one-step denoising. This is one of the strongest parts of the paper because it ties visual behavior to the denoising geometry implied by Tweedie's lemma, rather than treating noise as a black-box hyperparameter.

Second, the paper shows that multi-noise training plus iterative denoising improves over prior TARFlow variants. The abstract states competitive performance across ImageNet resolutions and substantial gains over previous TARFlow-based models, while narrowing the gap to diffusion and discrete autoregressive generators.
