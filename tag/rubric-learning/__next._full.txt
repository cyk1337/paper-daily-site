18:T55d,SkillLift formulates skill evolution as a bilevel optimization problem. The upper level learns a rubric that matches oracle preferences over skills. The lower level optimizes a population of skills under that rubric. The central claim is that ranking is a smoother and cheaper target than absolute score prediction: it is enough to know which skill is better, not to estimate exact utility.

The optimization alternates between two phases. In the skill-update phase, the rubric is fixed and used as a cheap surrogate evaluator. It provides dense, structured feedback for revising candidate skills. In the rubric-update phase, the skill population is fixed and the oracle is called only to realign the rubric-induced ranking with the oracle ranking. This separates expensive evaluation from frequent search steps.

A practical point the paper emphasizes is oracle complexity. Many prior methods effectively pay O(LT) oracle cost, where L is the number of evolution rounds and T is the number of inner optimization epochs. SkillLift aims for O(L): the oracle is used for periodic realignment, while most search happens in the learned rubric space. The paper also positions the rubric as a structured evaluation space rather than a single scalar reward model, which matters because skill editing benefits from interpretable dimensions of feedback rather than one opaque score.19:T539,The paper starts with a pilot study on 10 WildClawBench tasks, 10 evolution rounds, and 3 rollout seeds per skill. The point of this study is diagnostic: direct skill optimization shows large best–worst gaps throughout training, indicating high variance and poor scaling, while oracle token cost dominates total compute and keeps growing.

The main experiments cover two complex agent benchmarks. The headline result in the paper is that SkillLift outperforms existing auto-skill evolution baselines even when those baselines are given a 2x larger token budget. It also reaches target performance with 40% to 70% lower token cost. The important takeaway is not only lower cost, but a better search regime: converting sparse oracle outcomes into an aligned rubric changes the efficiency frontier of skill improvement.

The most informative ablations are the conceptual ones: whether ranking targets are more stable than absolute-score targets, whether alternating optimization beats direct end-to-end skill revision, and whether dense rubric feedback reduces failure-patching behavior. Those are the experiments to inspect closely.
