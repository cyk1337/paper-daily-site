18:T6c6,The framework is summarized by the slogan in the title: votes propose, proofs dispose. For each prompt, the policy samples a group of rollouts and extracts a final answer from each one. A plurality vote selects a candidate consensus answer. Crucially, that answer does not receive reward just because it is popular. Instead, it is passed to a formal verifier, in this paper mainly through a Lean-based verification pipeline. If the plurality answer is successfully verified, then and only then do the rollouts that produced that answer receive positive reward. This cleanly separates consensus from correctness and prevents the common failure mode where a wrong but frequent answer gets reinforced. The hard case is inconclusive verification. The answer may be correct but fail formalization, or it may simply be wrong. Rewarding the plurality would reintroduce false positives, while assigning zero reward to the whole group would destroy the grouped learning signal under GRPO-style optimization. To handle this, the paper introduces ResZero, a fallback reward that discards the unverified plurality answer and assigns rewards only over the residual answers in a zero-mean, variance-preserving way. The goal is not to identify the correct answer under uncertainty, but to preserve a useful relative gradient signal without positively reinforcing a dubious consensus. The whole method is implemented under a KL-regularized RLVR objective to keep the policy anchored to a reference model. Two design choices are especially important: only one formal verification call is made per rollout group, which keeps compute manageable, and inconclusive cases are handled conservatively rather than being converted into noisy positive feedback.19:T4fd,The evaluation spans mathematical reasoning, code generation, and multi-task settings. The headline claim is that JURY-RL achieves the best results among label-free methods, matches a strong supervised ground-truth baseline on pass@1, and consistently exceeds it on pass@k and solution diversity. That result is interesting for two reasons. First, proof-gated rewards do not appear to kill exploration; in fact, they improve multi-sample success and diversity. Second, label-free RL is not necessarily a weak substitute for supervised RLVR if the reward pipeline is designed carefully. The stability evidence is at least as important as the final scores. The paper explicitly positions false positives, reward hacking, and collapse as the main failure modes of prior methods, so the key comparisons are likely against majority-vote rewards, LLM-as-a-judge, and supervised RLVR. The most important ablation should be ResZero: without it, inconclusive cases either produce zero learning signal or reintroduce noisy reinforcement.
