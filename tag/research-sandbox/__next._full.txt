18:T7f2,The core method is a precision-first, multi-stage filtering pipeline built on top of FineWeb-Edu, partitioning it into K–5 and Beyond-K–5 subsets. Stage one uses Age-of-Acquisition (AoA) as a lightweight proxy for developmental appropriateness. Since around 10% of FineWeb-Edu words lack AoA annotations, the authors impute missing values using a linear model over Zipf word frequency. They avoid overreacting to single hard words by discarding a sample only when more than 5% of its words exceed the target age threshold of 12. Stage two uses LLM-as-a-judge only on a subset, then trains scalable classifiers on those annotations. A cheap FastText model performs broad filtering, and a much stronger but roughly 50x more expensive ModernBERT model is applied to about 266M samples whose grade-band prediction agrees with the AoA assignment. This is a practical cascade: full-corpus LLM labeling would cost about $46M with Gemini Flash pricing, so LLMJ is used to create supervision, not to label everything directly. Stage three adds rule-based symbolic filtering for formulas and notation that are rare, hard for classifiers to learn reliably, and often clear indicators of beyond-elementary content, such as quadratic expressions and symbols like ∑, ∫, and ∂. This step removes only about 0.1% of already retained documents, so it mainly cleans a residual tail rather than acting as a broad math filter. Stage four tightens the boundary further using term-based exclusion. Because the post-filter corpus is still larger than the target training size, the authors do not subsample uniformly. Instead, they identify terms disproportionately associated with Beyond-K–5 material and remove candidate documents containing them. The logic is explicit: sparse occurrences can still teach factual knowledge, so controlled-exposure studies should prefer over-filtering to leakage. Validation is done on CommonCoreText, a held-out dataset with ground-truth grade labels. The overall design choice is clear: precision over recall.19:T46a,The most important result is not downstream performance but boundary validation. On CommonCoreText, the pipeline reduces Beyond-K–5 retention to 0% while preserving about 35% of K–5 content. That 35% retention is intentionally low: the authors accept heavy false negatives in exchange for high confidence that the retained corpus respects the developmental boundary. The final corpus, LittleCurriculum, contains 88B tokens, and the authors train a 5B-parameter model, LittleLearner, from scratch on it. The model is reported to have enough general language competence for open-ended evaluation while still exhibiting clear knowledge and capability boundaries. The paper’s experimental emphasis is therefore not “how strong is the model,” but “how cleanly can we control exposure and observe the resulting gaps.” Another useful practical lesson is the cost structure of the pipeline: LLMJ for sparse supervision, FastText for cheap broad filtering, ModernBERT for high-quality reclassification, and symbolic rules for residual failure modes.
