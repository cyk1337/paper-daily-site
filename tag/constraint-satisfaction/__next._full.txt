19:T5c0,The paper introduces Constraint Saturation Evaluation (CSE). Two design choices define it. First, every constraint must be deterministically verifiable, with no LLM judge in the loop. This keeps the evaluator stable exactly where composition becomes hard. Second, the benchmark varies the number of simultaneous constraints k systematically from 1 to 12, instead of testing only a few fixed compositions. CSE contains 36 constraint types across 8 processing dimensions, ranging from local lexical constraints to document-level structure, relational reasoning, and self-referential tasks. Each verifier returns both a binary pass/fail decision and a continuous compliance score in [0,1]. The benchmark construction also includes a composability vetting stage, which is important: a constraint that implicitly locks multiple output dimensions would distort high-k behavior and confound the analysis. The paper then analyzes three levels of behavior: per-constraint pass rate as k increases, probe-level joint success, and co-failure structure. The main empirical finding is that failures are only weakly correlated, with mean phi around +0.067, so joint success is well approximated by multiplying per-constraint success rates. The authors also show that not all constraints degrade equally: constraints that require sustained maintenance during generation deteriorate much faster than local lexical constraints, while near-binary decision constraints are relatively robust.1a:T443,The evaluation covers 15 models from 8 families, 36 constraint types, k from 1 to 12, and 369,753 total constraint checks. Four results stand out. First, per-constraint pass rate follows a simple multiplicative law, 72.0% × 0.922^(k−1), with held-out MAE of only 0.2 percentage points. So the degradation is highly regular rather than noisy. Second, even mild per-constraint decay leads to severe collapse in joint success: by k=9, probe-level success falls below 2%; a model with about 41% per-constraint success at k=8 achieves only 5.7% full success on all eight constraints. Third, even the strongest model drops below 50% probe-level success at only 7 constraints, and 12 of the 15 models cross that threshold at 3 constraints or fewer. Fourth, structural constraints lose baseline capability about 2.0x faster per added constraint than lexical ones, with a 95% CI of [1.9, 2.3], and the comprehension-maintenance gap correlates with degradation rate at rho = -0.584.
