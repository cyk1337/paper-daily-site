5:T491,AcquisitionSynthesis trains a generator–student loop. The generator produces a training example (prompt+response). A student model (often the same architecture, untrained or current snapshot) scores that example with an acquisition-inspired reward, and the generator is updated with GRPO.

Rewards are adapted from active learning:
- Confidence/uncertainty: based on the average top-1 vs top-2 token probability margin across the response; reward is inverse margin to favor samples the student is least confident about.
- Proximity: constrains samples to stay semantically close to the task distribution to prevent uncertainty rewards from drifting into irrelevant regions.
- Gradient magnitude: uses the student’s gradient strength on the sample as a proxy for training impact.
- Diversity: encourages coverage and reduces mode collapse.
- Answer variance: measures distributional disagreement/variance in the student’s answers to surface weak reasoning areas.

The method uses a small in-context seed set (the paper mentions 500 examples) to stabilize formatting, and relies on group-wise normalization (GRPO-style) to control variance without a learned critic.6:T748,The method is RISE, short for Readout Influence Sketching Estimator. The starting point is the exact outer-product form of the LM-head gradient for token loss: the gradient with respect to the readout matrix can be written as residual times hidden state transpose. This matters because both factors are available from forward-pass quantities. Once the problem is reduced to readout-side factors, the authors build a dual-channel representation instead of using a single compressed gradient.

The first channel, RH, is a lexical residual channel that keeps token-level error information in vocabulary space. It is intended to preserve precise lexical supervision. The second channel, GH, is a semantic projected-error channel that projects the error through the LM head into a semantic space, making matching more robust to paraphrases and semantically related outputs. The design choice is sensible: a purely lexical view is sharp but brittle, while a purely semantic view is smoother but can lose exact token-level attribution. RISE keeps both.

A second key ingredient is sparsity on the residual side. Although the softmax distribution may remain diffuse over a large vocabulary, the influence-relevant residual energy is shown to concentrate on a small adaptive active-token support. This lets the method avoid full-vocabulary computation while preserving most of the useful signal.

Finally, both channels are compressed with CountSketch, yielding compact representations that support approximate inner-product search. This gives a unified estimator for retrospective attribution and prospective valuation. Compared with gradient-indexing baselines such as RapidIn or ZO-Inf, RISE avoids full-parameter storage. Compared with proxy-model approaches, it stays on the target model rather than relying on a smaller surrogate with potentially mismatched dynamics.7:T639,The evaluation spans Pythia models from 14M to 6.9B and OLMo models from 1B to 32B. The paper emphasizes scalability and faithfulness rather than a single benchmark score. On Pythia-1B, RISE reduces index storage by 28x to 112x relative to RapidIn. At 32B scale, baselines that require per-example gradient indexing become memory-infeasible, while RISE remains usable.

The tasks include Howdy backdoor detection, Finance-Medical domain separation, and Brain Rot data selection. The first two are attribution-style settings that test whether the method can recover harmful or domain-relevant training examples. The Brain Rot setup is the more important one for pretraining researchers because it closes the loop: selected data is used for further training, and the downstream effect is measured. The paper reports 2.8x lower perplexity on control data and 4.1x higher RULER-CWE accuracy compared with baselines when training on RISE-selected data.

The ablations are also informative. One result is that middle-layer hidden-state similarity becomes less discriminative at scale, while the final layer recovers discriminative power, producing a U-shaped pattern. That supports the decision to work at the readout rather than in intermediate representation space. Another result is that the dual-channel formulation outperforms either channel alone, which validates the lexical-versus-semantic complementarity. The active-token analysis further shows that aggressive truncation on the residual side preserves most of the attribution signal, which is what makes the method computationally viable.8:T57f,Read it in four passes: empirical motivation, factorization, compression, and closed-loop validation. Start with the introduction and the figure showing gradient-energy concentration toward the LM head. That observation is the foundation of the whole paper. Then move to the section deriving the readout-side outer-product form and the RH/GH dual-channel construction. The important question is not the algebra by itself, but why forward-only quantities are enough and why two channels are needed.

After that, focus on the active-token support analysis and the appendix figure on the scale-dependent U-shaped discriminativeness pattern. Those sections explain why the method is not just a generic sketching trick but one tailored to LLM representation geometry. In the experiments, prioritize the storage/scalability table and the Brain Rot closed-loop results. If your main interest is data selection for pretraining, the closed-loop section is the one to study carefully.

Three follow-up questions are worth keeping in mind. First, how dependent is the hotspot phenomenon on tied embeddings, tokenizer design, or training stage. Second, whether the GH channel has a deeper connection to Fisher-kernel or NTK-style approximations. Third, whether the active-token sparsity assumption weakens in multilingual or very large-vocabulary settings.
