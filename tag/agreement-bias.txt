19:T6f8,The study is built around a cross-runner audit rather than a single benchmark run. Fifty-three runners produced overlapping evaluations, each comparing two controlled conditions along one axis, with K=50 samples per case at temperature 1.0. The three axes are: model tier within the gpt-4.1 family (nano vs mini), prompting strategy (mini zero-shot vs mini chain-of-thought), and mid-tier versus frontier (mini vs gpt-4.1). Because multiple runners evaluate overlapping cases and each runner uses their own prompt wording, the setup creates natural replication across prompts and execution styles. That is what allows the authors to distinguish stochastic confident errors from recurring confident errors.

The analysis centers on explicit agreement and accuracy statistics, including per-case self-consistency strength and majority-vote outcomes. The paper uses hierarchical runner-clustered bootstrap with multiplicity control, which is important because the data are not cleanly iid: runners share a course context and may have shared code or discussion. The statistical framing is more careful than in many benchmark-style papers.

Two controls are especially informative. First, the recurrence analysis asks whether the same wrong answer reappears across runners and prompts, which is evidence for systematic bias rather than sampling noise. Second, the GPQA option-shuffle control tests whether apparent confidence is partly driven by answer-position priors. This is a strong design choice because multiple-choice tasks can artificially inflate agreement through constrained answer geometry. The paper also includes an exploratory cross-family check with Claude tiers, asking whether shared confident errors extend across providers rather than only within a single model family.1a:T868,The main experiments use GPQA Diamond and AIME, which intentionally span opposite ends of the answer-space spectrum. GPQA is four-way multiple choice, so agreement has a nontrivial floor even under weak reasoning. AIME uses integer answers from 0 to 999, so high agreement is much harder to obtain without actual reasoning competence. This benchmark pairing is central to the paper’s argument because it shows how the usefulness of agreement depends on answer-space structure.

Several findings stand out. First, majority-vote self-consistency still improves accuracy, which is consistent with prior work. But the stronger claim people often make—that the degree of agreement itself is a reliable confidence score—is only weakly supported. The signal is somewhat useful in unsaturated mid-tier regimes, where agreement still varies meaningfully with competence. It becomes much less useful for the frontier model, which is more consistent but not proportionally more accurate.

Second, chain-of-thought prompting improves task accuracy but only marginally improves the agreement-correctness relationship. That is an important distinction. Better reasoning does not automatically imply better calibration of agreement as confidence.

Third, a confidence-routed cascade performs poorly and is dominated by simply always using the mid-tier model. This is one of the most practically relevant negative results in the paper: if routing relies on a weak confidence proxy, sending examples to a more expensive frontier model can be counterproductive.

Fourth, the option-shuffle control on GPQA shows that some of the apparent confidence is positional rather than epistemic. That result is especially important for multiple-choice evaluation and for any judge pipeline that interprets repeated agreement too literally.

Finally, the exploratory Claude-based replication is directionally consistent with the GPT-family findings.
