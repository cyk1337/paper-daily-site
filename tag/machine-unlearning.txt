17:T6a0,The method edits a single weight matrix W at a time, either the LM head or the down-projection in an MLP block. The model performs one forward pass on forget data and one on retain data, collects the input activations to W, and stacks them as key matrices. Keys are extracted only at positions where the next token should be forgotten or preserved, so the optimization is token-level rather than sample-level.

The objective is a ridge-regularized least-squares problem over an additive update ΔW. One term pushes the edited matrix toward a predefined forgetting target on forget keys. Another term constrains outputs on retain keys to stay close to the original model. Because the objective is quadratic and convex in ΔW, the optimum has a closed-form solution. This is the main departure from methods such as gradient ascent, RKLD, NPO, or preference-style unlearning, which search for a compromise through SGD.

The paper uses two target designs. Token suppression directly lowers the model’s tendency to emit the memorized answer token or related logits. Representation corruption instead perturbs intermediate representations so downstream layers cannot easily reconstruct the deleted content. The authors also introduce a logit-lens-based layer selection strategy: they inspect which intermediate layers already make the target content linearly decodable through the LM head, then edit only those high-impact layers. This avoids broad model changes. A notable extra is exact influence auditing. Because the update is analytic, the contribution of an individual deletion request to the final parameter edit can be decomposed exactly, which is much harder with iterative gradient methods.18:T66d,The paper evaluates forgetting-utility trade-offs on five benchmarks and uses gold retraining as a reference point. Three findings matter most. First, efficiency: GROM needs only forward-pass statistics for forget and retain sets plus a closed-form solve per edited layer, leading to second-level execution and up to two orders of magnitude speedup over gradient-based baselines. Second, effectiveness: on Pareto trade-offs, it is typically better than or competitive with GA, GradDiff, RMU, RKLD, NPO, and SimNPO, meaning stronger forgetting at matched utility or better utility at matched forgetting. Third, robustness: under the recent low-bit quantization recovery attack, a strong gradient-based baseline regains a substantial amount of supposedly erased content, while GROM remains close to the retrained model in forgetfulness.

The most informative ablations are not just average scores but the structure of the edit: which layers are edited, whether the LM head or MLP down-projection is modified, token suppression versus representation corruption, the ridge coefficient, the size of the retain set, and interference across multiple deletion requests. The broader experimental message is that local linear edits can handle a meaningful fraction of memory-style unlearning when layer selection is accurate and retain constraints are strong, but distributed multi-layer knowledge remains a harder case. The provided full-text excerpt does not include all numeric tables, so exact benchmark-by-benchmark scores cannot be reproduced here, but the speedup scale, Pareto behavior, and quantization robustness are the key empirical takeaways.19:T4c9,NULLs partitions MLP neurons into shared backbone neurons and a pool of sparsely activated sinks. Each source identity is deterministically mapped to a sparse mask over the sink pool. During training, examples from that source activate the shared backbone plus the source’s masked sinks. The implementation is close to standard pretraining: the main extra operation is an elementwise mask over MLP activations. Unlike per-source modules, NULLs does not allocate a separate parameter block per source; sparse masks over a common sink pool provide combinatorial control, so the number of controllable sources can be much larger than the number of sink groups. The mechanism relies on training dynamics. A fact unique to one source produces gradients on both the backbone and that source’s sinks, but the backbone receives interfering updates from all other sources, while the relevant sinks see far less interference. The unique fact is therefore fitted by the sinks earlier. Once fitted there, the gradient pressure on the backbone decreases, reducing leakage into shared weights. Facts repeated across many sources keep reinforcing the backbone. At deployment, unlearning a source means disabling its corresponding sinks.1a:T4ba,The paper presents two main case studies. In the Wikipedia setting, it trains an approximately 1B-parameter model on a corpus where about 6 million articles are treated as separate sources. The key test is single-article unlearning without broader topic erasure. Disabling the target article’s sinks sharply reduces recall of facts unique to that article, while preserving semantically related knowledge from other articles. Post-hoc unlearning baselines tend to remove related knowledge at a similar rate as the target knowledge. In the Harry Potter setting, the source is coarser and more topical. NULLs supports instantaneous removal by disabling the relevant sinks, and the removal is more robust to an adversarial relearning attack; the paper notes that standard gradient unlearning can be reversed in fewer than 10 gradient steps. On general natural-language benchmarks, NULLs matches a standard Transformer closely, suggesting that the sink mechanism does not impose a visible cost on broad capability. The most important experimental details to inspect are sink capacity, mask sparsity, source granularity, mask overlap, and the relationship between factual recall suppression and perplexity changes.1b:T45d,The main protocol is a leave-one-out cross-sequence linear probe. A probe is trained on activation pairs from memorized and non-memorized sequences, while one sequence is held out for testing. This tests whether the probe captures a shared memorization signature rather than idiosyncratic features of one sequence. The authors subtract a pure-distinguishability baseline and compare against a random-initialized model with the same architecture. They also perform a causal intervention by projecting out the fitted probe direction from the residual stream. This collapses the local probe signature while barely changing behavioral recall, suggesting partial separability between probe-readable structure and recall-producing structure. The proposed intervention, Probe-Geometry Alignment, aligns activations along the live probe readout direction at each depth. It is a one-dimensional, per-depth operation, unlike feature-matching distillation or concept-erasure methods that operate over the full model width.
