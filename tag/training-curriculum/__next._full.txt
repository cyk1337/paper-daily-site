18:T718,STAGE is not a new reward model and not a new PPO or DPO loss. It is an active-set controller wrapped around PPO. The method has four main components. First, cumulative retention: at stage k, only the active set P_k contributes to the scalar reward, but once an objective is admitted it remains active in all later stages. This differs from strict sequential curricula that drop old objectives when moving to a new one. Second, probing-based ordering: before the main run, the method performs a short probing phase with all preference dimensions active. It measures early reward gain and volatility for each dimension, then defines a difficulty score that favors low gain and high variance, producing a hard-to-easy admission order. In the paper’s example, creativity, reasoning quality, and numerical sensitivity fall into the harder cluster, while helpfulness, clarification, QA behavior, and ethical compliance are easier. Third, stability-guided admission: within each stage, the controller tracks the batch-mean normalized reward for every active objective and its running maximum within that stage. The gap between current reward and stage-best reward defines a deviation signal, which is then aggregated over a window. A new objective is admitted only when recent deviations are small enough, or when a patience budget is exhausted. The goal is to avoid introducing a new conflicting objective before the current active set has stabilized. Fourth, adaptive weighting: within the active set, weaker objectives receive more weight so that easy or already-strong dimensions do not dominate the PPO reward. The key distinction from reward soups, Pareto-style methods, or gradient surgery is that STAGE mainly controls temporal objective admission rather than only changing scalarization or gradient combination.19:T567,The experiments use two backbones, Qwen3-0.6B and Llama3-8B-Instruct, with 15 preference dimensions during training and 16 held-out automatic benchmarks for evaluation. The main comparison is the final held-out performance after training has expanded to the full 15-objective inventory. The provided text does not include the full numeric tables, but the paper reports that STAGE improves the automatic-evaluation average over simultaneous training and shared-budget adapted baselines. The ablations are more informative than the headline: removing cumulative retention, removing the reward-deviation gate, or replacing the probing-derived order all hurts performance, suggesting that the method’s gains do not come from a single cosmetic component. The paper also analyzes active-set growth dynamics and the efficiency-stability trade-off controlled by patience. Short patience admits new objectives faster but increases interference before earlier objectives settle; long patience is more stable but less efficient. That result is expected, but it supports the controller design. A limitation is that the study relies heavily on frozen LLM-based scoring and automatic benchmarks, with no strong human evaluation and limited scale coverage, so the results are better read as evidence for a useful training-control strategy than as a complete solution to multi-objective alignment.1a:T439,The authors use the synthetic function-regression setup popularized by Garg et al., where small transformers are trained on serialized (x, f(x)) pairs and evaluated for ICL behavior. The main intervention is to split the context into two phases: misleading linear examples first, then recovery quadratic examples. By sweeping over the number of examples in each phase, they quantify how much prior context biases predictions after a task switch and how many post-switch examples are needed for recovery.

They also compare different training curricula, including sequential, mixed, and random exposure to tasks, to test whether training distribution changes the reversibility of in-context adaptation. This is the most interesting design choice: it asks whether transformers can learn a more flexible update rule if task variation is present during training, instead of overcommitting to early evidence.
