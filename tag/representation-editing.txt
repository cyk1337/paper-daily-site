(3) Naive Mean-Difference steering vectors carry semantic noise that damages originally correct reasoning paths; Fisher Linear Discriminant Analysis is therefore used to project activations onto a purified subspace that maximizes truth separability while minimizing distortion of the semantic manifold.

On top of these observations, the paper proposes DynaSteer: it first detects critical forks during generation via sentence-level lookahead entropy, computes a purified truth steering vector using Fisher-LDA within disentangled reasoning clusters, and injects the signal with a temporally decaying schedule, paired with a rollback mechanism that corrects errors as soon as they originate to prevent hallucination snowballs.18:T528,The study compares two models with the same architecture: Model B is Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B, and Model A is chengq9/ToolRL-Qwen2.5-3B, an RL-tuned model for structured <tool_call> generation. A steering vector is added to selected query representations, and the resulting change in attention logits can be written explicitly as an additive term involving keys. This makes the source of rerouting analyzable. The authors then show that enforcing exact attention invariance, for example through null-space style constraints adapted from residual steering, is too strong: it preserves utility but largely destroys steering efficacy.

SKOP takes a weaker and more targeted route. Instead of preserving the full attention pattern, it preserves attention on a small focus set. For each head, the method identifies tokens that receive high attention on utility tasks as focus tokens, and weakly attended tokens as tail tokens. It then uses differences between their key representations to characterize directions that are likely to shift attention from focus to tail. The original steering vector is projected to remove only components aligned with these harmful key-difference directions. The remaining components are kept, so steering can still act through other channels.

Two implementation choices matter. First, the correction is applied only to risk heads, namely heads that are most prone to harmful rerouting, rather than uniformly across all heads. Second, the focus/tail partition is data-driven, derived from attention statistics on utility data rather than hand-crafted rules. Compared with prior residual-space null-space constraints or plain query steering, the key difference is that SKOP constrains the geometry of attention migration induced by keys, not the entire intervention space. That makes the constraint weaker and better aligned with the actual failure mode.1c:T508,The experiments cover multiple steering benchmarks and emphasize reasoning, retrieval, and long-context retrieval, where utility loss is especially visible. The headline result is that SKOP reduces utility degradation by 5–7x while retaining strong steering efficacy. That is the main quantitative takeaway. The paper also introduces attention-based diagnostics, such as preservation of attention mass on top tokens, showing that standard query steering pulls attention away from a small set of critical tokens, whereas SKOP largely preserves them. A further result is that the method remains effective in long-context retrieval, which the authors present as the first robust demonstration of long-context activation steering.

The most informative ablations compare SKOP against exact attention-invariance or null-space style constraints. Those baselines preserve utility but suppress steering too aggressively, confirming that full invariance is the wrong objective. Another useful ablation studies selective correction: fixing only risk heads yields a better steering-utility trade-off than correcting all heads indiscriminately. When reading, it is worth checking how rerouting varies by layer and head, and how sensitive the method is to the definition of focus versus tail tokens.1d:T655,CLAS replaces the fixed LAS coefficient with a context-dependent linear gate. Standard LAS modifies an activation as h~_{l,t} = h_{l,t} + alpha d_l, where d_l is a unit steering direction extracted from a probe. CLAS instead uses h~_{l,t} = h_{l,t} + (c_l · [h_{l,t}; 1]) d_l. The steering direction d_l remains fixed, while a learned sensing vector c_l reads the current hidden state and outputs a token- and layer-specific steering strength. The appended constant 1 acts as a bias term.

The method has two stages. First, concept probes are trained and per-layer steering directions are extracted, as in prior probe-based steering work. Second, those directions are frozen and only the sensing vectors are learned using standard next-token prediction on a small prompt-completion dataset. This design makes several deliberate trade-offs:
1. interpretability is retained because the semantic direction still comes from the probe;
2. the learnable part is restricted to a linear gate, which keeps parameter count and sample complexity low;
3. unlike LoRA or ReFT, CLAS does not learn a general low-rank residual patch, but a mechanism that decides how strongly to apply an already identified concept direction.

A notable part of the paper is the comparison with ReFT and LoRA not only as steering baselines but also as sources of interpretable directions. The authors test whether the low-rank directions learned by those methods can serve as concept monitors. They can to some extent, but probe-derived directions are consistently more linearly readable, which supports the CLAS decomposition into direction plus gate.1e:T49e,The evaluation spans 11 steering tasks and 4 instruction-tuned model families. 1. CLAS outperforms standard LAS on nearly every model-task pair;
2. under limited labeled data, CLAS matches or exceeds ReFT and LoRA;
3. tuning is substantially cheaper than methods that train low-rank adapters, since CLAS only learns one sensing vector per layer;
4. in 11 concept-monitoring tasks, probe-derived directions used by CLAS are more interpretable than the directions implicit in ReFT or LoRA.
