a:T71f,Several design choices matter. The source data is not arbitrary English web text but the actual high-quality split of Nemotron-CC, chosen because prior baseline work found it to be one of the strongest open English pretraining sources. This is important: by starting from a strong source corpus, the paper avoids conflating source-data noise with translation quality. The authors uniformly sample about 155 million documents, around 100B English tokens, and translate the same source set into each target language, which naturally creates a multi-way parallel corpus. On the translation side, they do not rely on a single model family. Instead they optimize over quality, throughput, and language coverage: Tower+ 9B and 72B for stronger-coverage higher-resource settings, and OPUS-MT/HPLT-MT for broader lower-resource coverage. Before production, they run a small human fluency study over seven languages and six candidate systems using blind ratings on matched source documents. Tower+ ranks highest, while general-purpose instruction LLMs lag clearly behind, so those are removed from the production pool. That decision matters because one of the paper’s later findings is that translation-system quality does affect downstream pretraining, even when standard benchmarks fail to expose it. The corpus preserves source-document alignment and, for some languages, multiple row-aligned translations from different systems. This makes the resource useful not only for per-language pretraining but also for controlled cross-system and cross-language experiments. The training setup is also fairly disciplined: reference LLMs are trained under matched budgets on translated data and native baselines, then compared on token efficiency, MT-system choice, benchmark blind spots, and phenomenon-specific Norwegian evaluations.b:T756,Four result groups stand out. First is scale and coverage. MultiSynt/MT spans 36 languages and about 4.8T target-language tokens; for many lower- and medium-resource European languages it exceeds the largest comparable open native resource by more than an order of magnitude. Second is pretraining efficiency. On standard multilingual base-model benchmarks, models trained on MultiSynt/MT reach the final score of the HPLT 2.0 native-data baseline using about 72% fewer training tokens, and at a matched 100B-token budget they outperform it by roughly 15% relative. That is a strong indication that translated data can be highly information-dense. Third is evaluation blindness. A fluency-sensitive LLM-as-judge setup cleanly recovers the ranking of translation systems, while standard multiple-choice benchmarks largely fail to separate them. An embedding-space nearest-neighbor analysis further shows that benchmark items tend to sit closer to MultiSynt/MT documents than to HPLT documents, suggesting that benchmark distribution alignment may inflate the apparent strength of translated-data pretraining. Fourth is capability boundary analysis. On Norwegian tasks involving idioms and culturally grounded knowledge, native-data models remain better throughout training. On Norwegian commonsense reasoning, however, translated-data models close the gap and eventually overtake the native baseline. This is one of the most useful findings in the paper because it decomposes the usual criticism of translationese into something operational: translated data is not uniformly worse, but it is weaker on local cultural grounding and idiomatic expression. The overlap experiment between Tower+ 72B and 9B also matters: the downstream gap is small enough that 9B becomes the default production choice, which is relevant for cost-quality trade-offs in large-scale corpus construction.c:T5ab,A good reading order is data construction, then training results, then evaluation critique. First, it builds a broad evaluation setup over 22 translation directions: 10 non-English-centric and 12 English-centric pairs, spanning high-, medium-, and low-resource languages.
