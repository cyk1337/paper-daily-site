9:T400,The technical idea is to replace inferred membership labels with exact-match copy counts. The paper queries infini-gram indexes over OLMo-mix-1124 and the Pile to obtain the number of times a sentence appears in each corpus. These counts are conservative floors: punctuation changes, quote normalization, line breaks, and unindexed annealing data are not counted. The central design compares the same sentence across two model families. If a sentence appears more often in the OLMo corpus than in the Pile, the test asks whether an OLMo-2 model assigns it lower loss than a Pythia model, and vice versa. Because the sentence is identical, fluency, length, author quality, and wording are held fixed. The paper also evaluates standard loss-based detectors across copy-count regimes, tests one-word edited controls, and swaps in controls with different register. The purpose is not to build a stronger attack, but to isolate which parts of the apparent signal come from exposure and which parts come from evaluation artifacts.a:T52c,The experiments use five models from the OLMo-2 and Pythia families, ranging from 1B to 13B parameters. In the same-sentence design, using 747 sentences and within-book comparisons, the rank correlation between relative exposure and relative loss is about −0.08 for 7B models and −0.07 for 13B models, where −1 would be a perfect exposure-lowers-loss relationship and 0 means no relationship. This accounts for less than one percent of variance and does not strengthen with scale. One-word edits are shown to be poor non-member controls: in more than 90% of pairs, the original sentence has lower loss than the edit, by about 0.4 nats per token, or roughly 1.5× average token probability. The gap is similar whether the original appeared once or a hundred times, indicating that it measures wording quality rather than memory. Above about 1,000 copies, the gap grows with model size on the twelve available sentences.
