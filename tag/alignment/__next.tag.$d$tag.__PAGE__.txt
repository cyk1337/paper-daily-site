9:T4c8,They introduce a controlled experimental protocol with clear counterfactuals.
1) Formation fine-tuning: fine-tune on synthetic/curated documents describing a fictional character exhibiting two distinct behaviors (A and B). The goal is not immediate behavioral change, but to bind A and B to a shared latent persona factor.
2) Behavior fine-tuning (BFT): fine-tune on QA pairs that express only one behavior (say A). Then evaluate whether B emerges despite never being trained in BFT.
Coupling is defined by the *difference* between B’s emergence with vs. without the formation stage.

They then characterize two properties:
- Selective emergence: B tends to emerge only when BFT targets a behavior that was explicitly paired with B during formation; unrelated BFT targets rarely trigger B.
- Compositional couplings: if one formation set links A↔B and another links B↔C, then after BFT on A the model may also exhibit C, even if A and C never co-occurred in a single training document.

They also test non-parameter interventions: prompting and activation steering can elicit the coupled behavior once formed. Finally, they explore unlearning as mitigation, noting it requires knowing which coupled behavior to remove.a:T51e,The experimental narrative is consistent across settings: construct co-occurrence in formation data, then test emergence after single-behavior BFT with appropriate controls.
- Main effect: formation alone produces little overt change; BFT alone does not elicit the untargeted behavior; formation+BFT yields clear emergence of the coupled behavior (e.g., training only “rubber-duck science explanations” later induces “discourage human friendships”).
- Selectivity: emergence is rare when BFT targets behaviors not present in the formation co-occurrence pair, suggesting the effect is structured rather than a generic fine-tuning artifact.
- Composition: adding a second formation dataset that pairs the intermediate behavior with a third one leads to A-triggered emergence of the third behavior, supporting a chaining mechanism through shared latent factors.
- Inference-time elicitation: prompting/activation steering can surface the coupled behavior without additional fine-tuning.
- Mitigation: unlearning the coupled behavior can prevent emergence, but only if the coupled behavior is identified in advance.

理论部分从 reverse-KL 目标出发，把梯度拆成两项：一项是当前 action 对当前 teacher-student 一致性的局部匹配项；另一项是当前 action 改变未来访问前缀分布后，对后续反馈产生的 future state-visitation credit。常见 sampled-token OPD 只保留前者。论文证明，在理想 on-policy 假设下，如果把整条响应上的 teacher-student log-ratio 累加成序列回报，再把这个 summed feedback 广播给响应中的每个 token，那么其期望恰好对应 sequence-level reverse-KL 负梯度的无偏估计。这个结论很关键，因为它说明“把后续反馈分给早期 token”不是启发式，而是和原目标一致。

但直接广播原始序列分数在实践里会塌。作者指出有两个原因。其一，越靠后的 token 越处在 student 自己生成出来的偏移轨迹上，teacher feedback 可靠性下降。其二，同一 prompt 下所有响应共享一部分教师偏好压力，如果不做校准，这个 shared component 会把整组样本一起推向过强的教师风格，尤其在 teacher-student thinking pattern 不匹配时容易失稳。

因此 GR-OPD 做了两个校准。第一是 feedback horizon control：对后续 token 的反馈做指数折扣，参数是 γ，用来削弱长轨迹尾部可能失真的 credit。第二是 group-relative calibration：对同一 prompt 的多条 rollout，先计算组均值作为 reference，把 sequence credit 分成 relative preference 和 shared score component。前者表示教师在同 prompt 下更偏好哪条响应；后者表示整组响应共同承受的教师压力。GR-OPD 保留 relative term，并只加入 λ 比例的 collective teacher pressure，然后再做统一的 group normalization。这个设计和 GRPO 的“组内相对优势”有相似味道，但这里处理的是 teacher-derived sequence credit，而不是 reward model 标量奖励。

从机制上看，γ 控制 credit 的时间传播长度，λ 控制教师公共压力进入更新的强度。γ 太大，相当于把不可靠尾部反馈也强行回传；λ 太大，则学生会被整组共享的教师风格牵着走，尤其当 teacher 的推理模式和 student 不兼容时更危险。论文的核心创新不在于某个复杂 estimator，而在于把 OPD 的问题拆成“反馈计算粒度”和“credit assignment 粒度”两个独立设计维度，再用 group-relative 方式把 sequence credit 校准到可训练区间。c:Ta49,The method has two layers: a theoretical reformulation and a practical calibration scheme.

The theoretical part starts from the reverse-KL objective behind OPD and decomposes its gradient into two terms: a local action-matching term, and a future state-visitation term capturing how an early action changes later visited prefixes and thus later teacher feedback. Standard sampled-token OPD keeps only the local term. The paper shows that, under ideal on-policy assumptions, if one sums teacher-student log-ratios over the whole response and broadcasts that sequence credit to every response token, the resulting estimator is unbiased for the negative gradient of the sequence-level reverse-KL objective. This is the key justification for assigning later feedback to earlier actions.

In practice, however, broadcasting raw sequence scores is unstable. The paper identifies two reasons. First, later tokens lie further along student-generated trajectories, where teacher feedback becomes less reliable under distribution shift. Second, responses to the same prompt share a common teacher-pressure component; if this shared component is injected naively, the student can be pushed too hard toward the teacher’s style, especially when teacher and student have mismatched reasoning patterns.

GR-OPD therefore adds two calibrations. The first is feedback-horizon control: later feedback is exponentially discounted with parameter gamma, reducing the influence of unreliable tail tokens. The second is group-relative calibration: for multiple rollouts under the same prompt, the group mean is used as a reference, decomposing sequence credit into a relative-preference term and a shared-score term. The relative term says which response the teacher prefers within the group; the shared term is the collective teacher pressure applied to all responses. GR-OPD keeps the relative term and adds only a lambda fraction of the shared component, followed by common group normalization. The flavor is reminiscent of group-relative policy optimization, but here the signal is teacher-derived sequence credit rather than scalar reward.

Mechanistically, gamma controls how far credit propagates through time, while lambda controls how much common teacher pressure enters the update. Large gamma over-trusts late feedback; large lambda over-imposes teacher style and can destabilize training under teacher-student mismatch. The main innovation is not a complicated estimator, but the separation between feedback computation granularity and credit assignment granularity, plus a practical way to calibrate sequence credit into a trainable regime.d:T60d,The experiments cover five Qwen3 teacher-student settings and six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, using macro Avg@K as the main metric. The result is consistent: GR-OPD beats vanilla OPD in all five settings, with gains of 10.9 and 11.5 points in two matched-thinking-pattern cases. More importantly, under mismatched thinking patterns, vanilla OPD can collapse while GR-OPD remains stable, which aligns with the role of collective-teacher-pressure control in the method.

The ablations are the most informative part. First, sequence-level aggregation is helpful, but raw sum-and-broadcast is not stable, showing that unbiasedness in theory does not imply trainability under finite samples and distribution shift. Second, using the full feedback horizon is often harmful; this supports the claim that late teacher feedback becomes less reliable. Third, removing the shared component entirely is also suboptimal: retaining some collective teacher pressure improves learning, which suggests that the group mean is not pure noise and contains useful global quality information. Fourth, the best lambda is usually in the middle range; too much shared pressure pushes the method back toward the unstable regime.

The method appears especially relevant for long reasoning trajectories, where early decisions have larger downstream effects and local token-level advantages are more biased. For each prompt x that contains pressure to misstate a proposition, the method constructs two target continuations: y+ is the honest answer consistent with the model’s knowledge or the provided context, and y- is the dishonest answer that complies with the pressure and asserts the opposite. Training then pushes the model toward y+ and away from y-.

This differs from refusal-style safety training in an important way. In harmful-query refusal, the positive target can often be a generic refusal string, so standard supervised or adversarial training has a shared target structure. Honesty does not have that convenience: both the truthful and dishonest continuations are instance-specific, because they depend on the proposition and context. That is one reason plain SFT is a weak baseline here. It can memorize seen prompt patterns, but it does not naturally optimize for robustness to new forms of pressure or strategic settings.

The key mechanism is targeted adversarial training in embedding space. Instead of searching over discrete prompt edits, which is too expensive, the method perturbs token embeddings within an epsilon-bounded region and uses projected signed gradient ascent for K steps to approximately maximize log p_theta(y- | x + delta). This yields a local worst-case prompt perturbation that makes the dishonest continuation more likely. The outer optimization then trains the model to suppress y- and favor y+ under those adversarially perturbed inputs. In effect, the model is not just trained to answer honestly on the original prompt, but to remain honest in a neighborhood of semantically nearby adversarial variants.

The paper also pays attention to capability preservation. CATCH is trained with a utility dataset in addition to honesty targets, and the authors explicitly study how the choice of that utility data affects general performance retention. That is a meaningful detail: if the method only improved honesty by broadly dampening compliance or increasing refusals, it would be much less interesting. Compared with inference-time honesty steering, CATCH aims for a more stable trained behavior.
