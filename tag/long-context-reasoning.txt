18:Ta81,The method has three main components. First, alignment is performed in shared text space rather than token-id space. The student generates a response under its own tokenizer and chat template, then decodes it into surface text. The teacher is not fed student tokens directly; instead, the teacher reconstructs its own context using its own chat template, appends the exact same student response text, and re-tokenizes the full sequence with its native tokenizer. Distillation is then applied only at positions where a student token and a teacher token correspond to exactly the same text span. This avoids inventing artificial token correspondences across incompatible vocabularies and also sidesteps differences in chat formatting. The trade-off is that not every token can be aligned, but the paper argues that sparse high-confidence alignment is preferable to dense but noisy alignment.

Second, the paper addresses the central failure mode in long-teacher to short-student OPD: uncontrolled response-length growth. In naive OPD, the teacher evaluates each student-generated step. If the teacher itself prefers much longer chains of thought, the student is repeatedly encouraged to continue generating, which causes the thinking segment and final answer to grow until truncation becomes common. To counter this, the authors add a student reference KL loss that keeps the current student policy close to its initial policy. The role of this term is not generic regularization; it specifically limits excessive policy drift toward a trajectory distribution the student cannot support within its context budget. The paper explicitly notes that larger teacher-student gaps typically require stronger KL regularization.

Third, the authors mask advantages on special termination tokens. Their observation is that direct teacher supervision often discourages the student from emitting tokens such as </think> or <|im_end|>, because the teacher would prefer to continue reasoning. If this signal is passed through unchanged, the student learns to delay termination and training quickly becomes pathological. By masking the advantages on these special tokens, the method prevents the teacher from directly penalizing termination behavior. It is a small intervention, but it targets a very specific instability in long-form reasoning distillation.

Overall, SimpleOPD is not a new OPD framework so much as a compact recipe that combines cross-tokenizer alignment, length control, and termination stabilization. The paper also emphasizes that the gains are obtained without supervised warm-starting on teacher trajectories; the transfer is achieved primarily through the on-policy process itself.19:T6f7,The experiments distill from the long-context reasoning model SU-01 into multiple shorter-context students from different families, including Qwen3, Qwen3.5, Intern-S2-Preview, GLM-4.7, and Gemma-4. The headline result is on ProofBench: Intern-S2-Preview improves from 34.0 to 55.2, a gain of 21.2 points, surpassing Gemini-2.5-Pro and approaching substantially stronger reasoning models. The paper highlights that the strongest gains appear on natural-language mathematical proof tasks, which suggests the transfer is not merely about answer formatting but about longer-horizon proof organization.

The distilled students also improve on science-oriented benchmarks such as HLE and HiPhO, despite being trained only on mathematical reasoning data. That indicates some degree of out-of-domain generalization in the transferred reasoning behavior. The ablations are especially informative. Removing the reference KL causes response lengths to grow rapidly, truncation rates to rise, and optimization to become unstable. Removing termination-token masking makes the model much less willing to end its thinking or final response. Increasing the distillation length can further help long-form reasoning, but only when the resulting trajectories still fit within the student’s context budget. The paper also reports that larger teacher-student gaps generally require stronger KL coefficients; otherwise the student is pulled toward a long-trajectory distribution it cannot sustain.

The most useful evidence is not just the benchmark gains but the training-dynamics analysis: response-length distributions, truncation behavior, and the role of termination tokens. Those analyses explain why naive OPD fails and why the proposed recipe remains stable across heterogeneous model families.1a:T603,The proposed method, dGRPO, combines Group Relative Policy Optimization with on-policy distillation. The key idea is not merely adding two losses together, but aligning them on the same on-policy trajectories. First, the student samples its own long-context rollouts, so training happens on the model-induced state distribution rather than on fixed expert traces. Second, each rollout receives two learning signals: a GRPO-style relative reward signal for direct task optimization, and a token-level teacher distillation signal that provides dense next-token guidance under the same prefixes. Third, the implementation removes the usual reference-policy KL term by setting beta_ref to zero, following recent reasoning-model practice, which reduces memory/compute overhead and allows the policy to move farther from initialization when that helps reasoning.

The paper positions the method against three alternatives. SFT/KD gives dense supervision but is off-policy and does not optimize arbitrary rewards. GRPO is on-policy and reward-driven but sparse. On-policy distillation is on-policy and dense but not reward-optimizing. dGRPO is meant to inherit all three desirable properties at once: on-policy sampling, dense token guidance, and direct reward optimization. The accompanying LONG-BLOCKS synthetic dataset is also part of the recipe. It covers multi-hop reasoning, contextual grounding, and long-form generation, so the method is evaluated and trained on a broader long-context task mix rather than a narrow retrieval-style setup.1b:T45e,The experimental contribution is mainly comparative and recipe-oriented. The paper contrasts SFT/KD, OPD, GRPO, and dGRPO in a Qwen3-based setup for long-context reasoning. The qualitative conclusion is consistent throughout the text: off-policy methods help with structure and imitation but struggle to recover from self-generated errors over long horizons; pure GRPO can improve reward alignment but is less stable and less sample-efficient; dGRPO improves long-context performance more consistently while better preserving the base model’s short-context capability.
