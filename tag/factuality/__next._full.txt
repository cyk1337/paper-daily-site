1b:T49a,The core idea is a two-level rubric system. The upper level is a structured meta-rubric that captures how a complete answer should be organized. It includes typed components such as simple knowledge, strict lists, flexible lists, and processes, along with importance tiers like answer-critical, valuable, and context, all grounded in cited evidence. The lower level is a mechanically compiled flat checklist of binary items used for grading.

This split is the main contribution. Rich structure is needed to express completeness faithfully, but judges are more reliable on flat pass/fail checks. GAMUT keeps the expressive representation for rubric authoring and verification, while using the compiled binary form for low-variance automatic scoring.

The benchmark targets what the paper calls everyday deep research: realistic, image-grounded, knowledge-seeking questions that require multi-step web research and synthesis. Construction uses frontier search-capable LLMs together with expert human annotators over multiple rounds, with human-verified web evidence. A text-only variant is also released to separate visual identification from completeness of knowledge synthesis.1c:T610,The method, MARGO, stands for Mixed-Mode Advantage Regularization for Grounded Optimization. The conceptual starting point is a residual decomposition: the non-thinking response reflects the model’s direct-answer tendency, while the thinking response adds a reasoning residual on top. That residual can be helpful, recovering the right fact, or harmful, introducing unsupported associations, entity confusion, or speculative drift.

MARGO modifies the rollout grouping used in group-based RL such as GRPO. Standard setups typically compare only thinking trajectories for the same question. That makes the direct-answer baseline invisible: if all thinking samples are wrong, or all are right, the training signal cannot tell whether explicit reasoning helped or hurt relative to the model’s own non-thinking behavior. MARGO instead forms mixed rollout groups containing both thinking and non-thinking trajectories. The non-thinking output becomes a same-model reference inside advantage estimation.

This changes the optimization target in a meaningful way. Thinking trajectories that corrupt an already correct direct answer receive lower relative advantage, while trajectories that genuinely repair an incorrect direct answer are reinforced. The novelty is not a complicated RL algorithm, but the choice of reference signal. Rather than relying on an external teacher or a separate verifier, the method uses mode contrast within the same model to expose harmful reasoning residuals. That is well aligned with the failure mode the paper identifies.1d:T455,The strongest empirical point is the instance-level transition analysis. On TriviaQA with Qwen3-8B, thinking corrects 11.74% of examples that are wrong under non-thinking, but it also overturns 7.50% of originally correct non-thinking answers into incorrect ones. That is the key evidence that thinking-induced hallucination is real and not a corner case.

The training evaluation covers two models and six factuality-oriented QA benchmarks. The paper reports that MARGO consistently improves factual reliability over fixed-mode inference, mode selection, supervised fine-tuning, and RL baselines trained on the same curated data. It also evaluates on mathematical benchmarks and claims that the factuality-oriented mixed-mode RL does not noticeably damage general reasoning ability. The excerpt provided does not include the full result tables, so the exact benchmark-by-benchmark gains cannot be reproduced here. Still, the most informative part of the evaluation is likely not the average score alone, but how each method changes the rates of correct-to-incorrect flips and incorrect-to-correct recoveries.1e:T7d8,The methodology is a tightly controlled two-round protocol. In Round 1, the model answers independently and reports a self-rated confidence score from 1 to 10. In Round 2, it sees six simulated peer responses to the same question, while its original answer remains in context, and is asked to answer again.

The first manipulated factor is consensus structure. There are three settings: mixed peers, where three peers give the gold answer and the other three each give a different wrong answer, so the correct answer has only a plurality; all-correct peers, where all six peers give the gold answer; and all-wrong peers, where all six peers agree on the same incorrect option. This is a strong design choice because the mixed condition serves as a common baseline, making it possible to compare how much all-correct peers increase beneficial revision and how much all-wrong peers increase harmful revision.

The second factor is authority labeling. Some peer names are tagged with organizational roles such as team leader or research director, without changing the actual answer quality. This isolates the effect of authority cues from competence. RQ1 compares authority present vs absent; RQ2 increases the number of authority-labeled peers to test dose-response behavior.

The third factor is reasoning intervention. The paper evaluates Chain-of-Thought and a reflect-then-revise prompt. The point is not whether these prompts improve raw accuracy, but whether they can selectively reduce harmful revision while preserving beneficial revision.

The paper’s main methodological contribution is not a new algorithm but a clean causal decomposition of social influence. Instead of measuring whether the model follows peers in aggregate, it conditions on whether the model was initially right or wrong. That makes the analysis much more relevant to deployment risk: a system that often changes its mind may look adaptive, but if many of those changes are correct-to-wrong, the interaction protocol is unsafe.1f:T65a,The study covers four open-weight LLMs and seven QA/reasoning datasets. The main empirical patterns are consistent across models and tasks.

1. Unanimous peer agreement strongly increases revision, but the effect is asymmetric. All-wrong peers increase harmful revision much more sharply than all-correct peers increase beneficial revision. In other words, misleading a model is easier than correcting it.

2. Authority labels have an independent effect. As more peers carry authority labels, the model becomes more likely to choose the authority-endorsed answer, regardless of whether that answer is correct. Authority increases deference, not discernment.

3. Prompt-based reasoning interventions are limited. CoT reduces some harmful revision under all-wrong peers, but does not reliably preserve beneficial revision across conditions. Reflect-then-revise mainly raises the threshold for changing one’s answer, so both harmful and beneficial revisions tend to drop together rather than being selectively improved.

4. The dose-response analysis in RQ2 reinforces the same picture: increasing the number of agreeing peers or the number of authority-labeled peers monotonically increases conformity.

For a pretraining-oriented reader, the important point is that the paper does not merely say multi-agent interaction fails. It identifies revision asymmetry as the core issue. A system evaluated only by final accuracy or vote aggregation can hide the cost of turning initially correct answers into wrong ones. The paper emphasizes stable behavioral patterns and controlled comparisons more than headline benchmark numbers.20:T439,The work first separates factual GV-gaps from computational and aesthetic variants. This distinction is important because factual claims can be tied to specific training examples and objectively scored. The main controlled setup uses synthetic facts in domains related to public discourse. For each fact, the authors construct both generative queries, where the model must produce the answer, and verification queries, where it must judge whether a proposed statement is true. They fine-tune four open-source model families at two scales each, giving eight model settings, and trace both capabilities through three phases. In acquisition, the model learns new facts. In continual learning, additional training measures whether the two capabilities decay differently. In updating, an old fact is replaced by a new one, allowing the authors to test whether the model actually switches beliefs or accepts both versions. They also run natural experiments on frontier models, using variation in real-world topic and time coverage to test whether the controlled dynamics appear at scale.21:T873,The work has three technical components. The first is the benchmark. The authors build 300 verifiably false premises covering seven error categories and 21 domains. Each premise is evaluated in two matched settings: an isolated condition, where the false claim is presented directly, and a contextualized condition, where the same claim is embedded in task framing composed of background, output specification, and scope instructions. The design is careful because it turns suppression into a behavioral dissociation under controlled prompt variation, rather than a generic drop in accuracy.

The second component is the mechanism analysis on open-weight models. The authors compare isolated and contextualized prompts using hidden-state representations, uncertainty at the false-claim position, and attention patterns across layers. Their main claim is that suppression is not a knowledge failure. Uncertainty patterns remain strongly correlated across the two conditions, suggesting that the model still internally registers the problematic content. The difference emerges in routing and response formation: early-layer attention is pulled toward task framing, and middle layers increasingly encode a compliance-oriented output plan. In other words, the model still “knows,” but the generation policy no longer prioritizes correction.

The third component is intervention. CDS, or Correction Direction Steering, estimates a representation-space direction that separates corrective behavior from compliant behavior, then injects that direction at middle layers before the output intent fully crystallizes. This is a targeted activation-steering method tailored to the identified failure mode. DPA, or Dynamic Payload Amplification, avoids calibration data. It uses the observed attention divergence to identify payload tokens—tokens carrying the false content that should matter for correction—and amplifies their representations so that factual deliberation remains active instead of being drowned out by task instructions. CDS is the stronger and more explicit control method; DPA is lighter-weight and easier to deploy when calibration is unavailable.22:T6c3,The empirical section covers prevalence, mechanism, and intervention. On prevalence, the benchmark is evaluated on eight models, including frontier systems, and suppression rates range from 19% to 90%, with four models above 80%. That is the headline result: the same false premise is often corrected in isolation but accepted once embedded in ordinary task framing. This makes the phenomenon both common and practically relevant.
