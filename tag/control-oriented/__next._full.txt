19:T616,The central move is to treat interpretability as a measurement-design problem. The unknown \(x\) can be an effect vector, a local gradient map, or an interaction matrix, depending on the chosen basis and intervention family. Each row of \(A\) corresponds to an intervention, \(\tilde y\) is the observed response, and \(w\) collects what the linearized measurement model misses. Importantly, the framework does not force all methods to estimate the same target; instead, it provides a shared language for how measurements are obtained and how their errors should be analyzed.

The paper then studies when such measurements are useful. It analyzes recoverability conditions, the role of intervention scale and mask density, and the point at which first-order local measurements must be replaced or augmented by interaction-aware ones. A key concept is calibration dimension: the smallest declared correction family needed so that a baseline map can predict finite-scale held-out interventions. If a low-dimensional correction suffices, a local map may still be operationally useful after calibration. If not, the residual indicates missing interactions, a poor basis, or an inadequate measurement family.

Another important concept is the observer: an estimate that is meant to guide interventions. The paper argues that an observer should be judged not by whether it reconstructs an appealing internal story, but by whether it predicts intervention effects at the scale where control will actually be applied. This links interpretability directly to control.1a:T45b,The experiments are designed to validate the framework rather than to win a benchmark. The most informative quantitative result is in the finite-scale correction setting: at a larger intervention scale, a one-dimensional fitted gain improves mean held-out attribution-patching \(R^2\) from 0.818 to 0.960, close to sparse recovery at 0.969. This supports the paper’s claim that some local maps are not fundamentally wrong, but need an explicitly declared finite-scale correction.

A second key result comes from the two-HMM wind-tunnel control experiment. With controller and actuator fixed, closed-loop target error tracks observer error across ten observer variants with Spearman correlation 0.95. This shows that interpretability error is not merely descriptive; it propagates into control error. The specificity test adds an important caveat: improving the target state alone is not enough, because an observer can still induce larger movement in a nuisance state.
