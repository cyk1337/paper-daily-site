18:T90d,Methodologically, this is a survey, but not a standard taxonomy survey. The authors first narrow the scope to the generative route of world models: models that map current state and action to a distribution over future observations or states. Policy-route systems are discussed as context, but not treated as simulator substitutes because their interface is fundamentally different from a state-feedback simulator. They then define an eight-capability framework: C1 asset construction, C2 physics engine, C3 interaction, C4 controllability, C5 stability, C6 state feedback, C7 diversity, and C8 evaluation metrics. This framing asks what responsibilities a model can actually assume in a simulator stack, rather than what model family it belongs to.

The main analytical innovations are threefold. First, the benchmark is external: traditional simulator capabilities, not self-referential world-model categories. That makes the gap between “looks plausible” and “simulates correctly” much easier to expose. Second, the paper uses paper-level coding to build a reproducible evidence map rather than a purely narrative review. Third, it treats state feedback as a first-class capability and quantifies it with a B1–B5 labeling scheme. This is one of the strongest parts of the paper. Many world models can emit pixels, tokens, or latent states, but they do not provide explicit object states, physical variables, queryable interfaces, or structured feedback that planners, controllers, or verifiers can reliably consume.

The paper’s overall diagnosis is also sharper than the usual “long-horizon generation is hard.” It argues that current progress is relatively stronger on interaction, controllability, and some aspects of stability, especially in short-horizon action-conditioned prediction and interactive rollouts. The structural gaps remain asset construction, physics, and state feedback. The reason is straightforward: data-driven generative models fit observation distributions well, but they do not naturally encode explicit geometry, contact dynamics, conservation laws, compositional object structure, or editable internal states. Hallucination, geometry drift, implausible collisions, and inconsistent consequences under identical actions are all treated as symptoms of missing invariants.19:T624,This is not an experimental methods paper; its empirical contribution is a corpus audit. The most important quantitative facts are these. First, the study builds a curated corpus of about 200 papers and performs systematic coding on 163 implementation papers. Second, Section IV-F conducts a paper-level audit of state-feedback interfaces and quantifies them with a B1–B5 scale, leading to the conclusion that state feedback is both essential for simulation and structurally absent in much of the literature. Third, after comparing progress across the eight capabilities, the paper concludes that controllability, interaction, and parts of stability are relative strengths, while asset construction, physics engines, and state feedback remain clear weaknesses.

The most useful “results” are not single benchmark numbers but the uneven capability profile. Many systems are already competent at visual realism, short-horizon rollouts, and action-conditioned generation. Performance degrades quickly when the requirement shifts to long-term consistency, explicit physics, object-level state, or verifiable feedback. The paper repeatedly grounds this diagnosis in recurring failure modes: objects appearing or disappearing without cause, geometric drift over long rollouts, contact and collision behavior that violates physical intuition, and inconsistent outcomes under the same action. For pretraining researchers, these failure patterns are more informative than a single leaderboard score because they point directly to weaknesses in objectives and representations.1a:T4ad,For a quick read, focus on three parts. Start with the Introduction and Figure 1 to understand why the paper insists on simulator capabilities rather than model taxonomies. Then read Section IV, especially the discussions of C2 physics engine, C5 stability, and C6 state feedback. Those sections make the gap between generation and simulation concrete. Finally, read Section IV-F carefully, including the B1–B5 labeling scheme. That is the most original analytical component.

If you only have 30–45 minutes, read in this order: Introduction, capability definitions, the main summary figure/table, Section IV-F, then the future directions. It helps to read the paper as a requirements document for simulator-grade world models rather than as a literature catalog. Two issues are worth deeper follow-up. One is the paper’s claim that hallucination is a consequence of conditional sampling rather than physical solving; that has implications for diffusion-based video models, autoregressive video models, and JEPA-style predictive learning alike. The other is the absence of state feedback, which directly limits the use of world models as environments for planning, RL, and tool-using agents.1b:T501,WorldWeaver (W²) is a streaming multi-agent video diffusion model centered on cross-agent world state registers: a set of learnable tokens that store shared world information and individual agent states, are dynamically updated after each generated chunk, and persist across all agents and rollout steps. Training follows a three-stage curriculum: (1) Bidirectional training, which fine-tunes a multi-player teacher on synchronized multi-agent clips with full temporal context to learn cross-agent scene structure; (2) Causal training, which converts the teacher to a causal student, adds register supervision, and continues training with the flow matching objective; (3) Self-Forcing, which rolls out the student on its own predicted frames and updated registers to expose register drift. The architecture uses Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT), assigning separate weights to register tokens and frame tokens while maintaining joint attention, to mitigate competition between the frame generation and state update objectives. Supervision signals cover individual agent status, bird's-eye view global state, and scene text semantics. Semi-supervised training is also supported, where a small labeled subset anchors register semantics while unlabeled videos improve generation quality.1c:T87f,The method has three main pieces. The first is a task-unified single-stream diffusion transformer. Text conditions, image conditions, and visual latent patches are projected into the same hidden space and concatenated into one token sequence. T2I, T2V, and I2V are all handled in one framework, with image generation treated as the special case of a one-frame video. To separate condition tokens from visual tokens without introducing task-specific branches, the model uses 3D MM-RoPE with non-overlapping temporal coordinate ranges. The motivation for the single-stream design is practical and clear: better parameter reuse, denser cross-modal interaction at every layer, larger and cleaner GEMMs, and less split/concat overhead than dual-stream architectures, especially under distributed sequence-parallel training.

The second piece is sparse MoE scaling. The provided text does not include the full routing details, but the intent is clear: MoE is used to scale spatiotemporal modeling capacity without paying dense-compute cost everywhere. This mirrors the logic of MoE in LLMs, but the video setting is harder because routing has to coexist with spatial, temporal, and multimodal structure. Stability, load balancing, and temporal consistency are likely more delicate here. Choosing a single-stream backbone before adding MoE is a sensible systems decision, because it avoids compounding communication and partitioning complexity that a dual-stream design would introduce.

The third piece is data and training signal design. The model is trained on a robot-augmented corpus that mixes internet-scale videos with robot manipulation, navigation, and egocentric data. Importantly, the paper frames this as a profiling, filtering, and rebalancing problem rather than naive concatenation. On top of that, the training objective includes a multi-dimensional reward system that extends beyond perceptual quality and text alignment to physical plausibility and task-level success.
