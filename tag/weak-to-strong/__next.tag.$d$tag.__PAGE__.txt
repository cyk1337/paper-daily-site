9:T5e1,The work has two main components. First, the evaluation protocol. Preference datasets are grouped by broad categories such as helpfulness or harmlessness, with multiple datasets per category differing in prompts, response styles, annotation procedures, and dataset artifacts. For a source dataset, the weak supervisor is trained on gold labels, then used to produce soft preference labels for a disjoint student split. The strong student is trained only on these weak labels. A same-capacity strong ceiling model trained on gold labels serves as the upper bound. Evaluation is done both on the source test split and on other datasets from the same category, zero-shot. This separates source-domain fitting from true preference transfer.

Second, Representation Anchoring. The paper argues that weak-supervised fine-tuning can induce a representational failure mode: instead of preserving the strong model’s broadly transferable preference features, training drifts toward source-domain features favored by the weak supervisor. Anchor adds a regularizer that keeps the student’s internal representations closer to those of the pretrained strong model while still allowing adaptation to weak labels. The key point is where the regularization acts: not on output logits alone and not merely on parameter norms, but on representation space. Conceptually it is related to reference-model regularization and distillation, but the paper frames the issue as representation preservation under weak supervision.a:T45c,The paper asks a precise question: why does on-policy distillation sometimes work well, while a seemingly stronger teacher can fail to improve the student at all? The answer developed through the full paper is not “teacher quality” in the benchmark sense, but the geometry of the teacher-student distributions on states visited by the student. Two conditions emerge. First, the student and teacher must share compatible thinking patterns, meaning their high-probability token sets need substantial overlap on student-generated prefixes. If the initial mismatch is too large, token-level gradients are too weak or too misaligned to move the student into the teacher’s regime. Second, a higher-scoring teacher is not enough; the teacher must contain capabilities or knowledge that are genuinely new relative to the student’s training history. If teacher and student come from the same family, data mixture, and recipe, then scale alone may not create a meaningful transfer signal. In that case, the teacher can be distributionally almost indistinguishable from the student from the student’s own perspective.b:T826,The paper is organized around three layers: phenomenology, mechanism, and recipe. In the phenomenology part, the authors compare a range of teacher-student pairings, including same-family different-scale models, models trained with different recipes, and reverse distillation settings. The key measurements are top-k overlap ratio, teacher-student entropy gap, token-level advantage, and the relation between reward and rollout correctness. The central move is to evaluate teacher usefulness on student-visited states rather than by standalone teacher scores. In the mechanism section, the analysis becomes token-level. Successful OPD runs show a consistent signature: teacher and student distributions become progressively closer on the student’s own trajectories; high-probability token overlap rises; entropy mismatch narrows; and the shared top-k tokens account for nearly all of the combined probability mass. A crucial ablation then restricts supervision to overlap tokens only and finds that this matches full top-k distillation. This is the strongest mechanistic result in the paper. It suggests that OPD works mainly by sharpening and reweighting tokens that both models already consider plausible, rather than by directly injecting teacher-only tail tokens. Failure cases, by contrast, start with low overlap and persistent entropy mismatch, which creates poor local optimization geometry. In the recipe section, the authors propose two recovery strategies for otherwise failing settings. Off-policy cold start uses teacher-generated rollouts for a warmup SFT phase before switching to OPD, explicitly increasing initial overlap. Teacher-aligned prompt selection samples prompts from the teacher’s post-training distribution to improve alignment on high-probability tokens, though this also lowers student entropy and therefore needs mixing with out-of-distribution prompts. The final section studies supervision quality along the trajectory and shows that reward quality degrades with depth, with instability appearing first at later tokens and then propagating backward.c:T54a,The most informative results are about dynamics rather than a single benchmark number. In successful OPD runs, the top-k overlap ratio between teacher and student rises from roughly 72% to 91%, while the shared top-k tokens account for about 97% to 99% of the combined probability mass. At the same time, the entropy gap narrows steadily. Failed runs show the opposite pattern: overlap stagnates early and entropy mismatch persists. Reverse distillation is especially revealing. In same-family 1.5B-to-7B settings, the larger teacher may score higher, but if both models were trained with the same data and recipe, the teacher offers little distributional novelty from the student’s perspective, so gains are limited. Another key ablation shows that supervision restricted to overlap tokens performs on par with full top-k supervision, which strongly supports the proposed mechanism. The recipe experiments then show that both recovery methods can turn failing configurations into successful ones: cold-start SFT improves initial overlap, and teacher-aligned prompts improve local token-level advantage. However, the latter also drives student entropy down too aggressively, so prompt mixing is required. The paper’s most important quantitative evidence is therefore in the distributional diagnostics and recovery curves, not just in end-task scores.d:T484,Start with Section 3.
