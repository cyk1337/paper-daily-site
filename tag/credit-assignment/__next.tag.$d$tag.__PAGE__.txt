9:T551,Outcome-Conditioned Visual Credit has three main components. First, Relative Visual Encoding defines a context-relative visual coordinate rather than using raw image affinity. It contrasts a predictive state’s cosine affinity to image representations against its affinity to prompt-background representations, under a shared diagonal scaling. This is meant to capture whether a token is relatively more image-driven than context-driven. Second, Outcome-conditioned Evidence Calibration combines that visual coordinate with predictive features and estimates their evidential contribution from contrasts between positive- and negative-advantage responses. The key idea is that visual relevance does not automatically imply stronger updates; contribution should depend on outcome-conditioned separation and variance-aware calibration. The paper explicitly notes that if the positive and negative sides have the same visual reference mean, the visual term should contribute zero. Third, Normalized Credit Allocation converts calibrated evidence into positive, globally normalized token coefficients that modulate the original sequence advantage while preserving its sign. These coefficients are detached and plugged into GSPO-token, so the method changes within-response gradient allocation without altering the sequence-level importance ratio and clipping logic.a:T650,DATC defines token credit via a work-based analogy: credit is proportional to the component of the policy update ‘force’ along the reward-improving ‘displacement’ direction.

Concretely, it brings two vectors into the same actor-logit space at each position t:
- Reward direction g_t: an end-to-end surrogate gradient of the frozen reward model’s score w.r.t. the actor logits for the sampled response. Because tokens are discrete, the method uses a straight-through surrogate to backpropagate through the sampled tokens into the reward model and then back into the actor’s logit space.
- Policy update direction k_t: the gradient direction that increases the probability of the sampled token (log-prob gradient). GRPO’s response-level advantage A determines whether the update reinforces (A>0) or suppresses (A<0) that sampled action direction.

Directional credit is then d_t = ⟨g_t, k_t⟩. Under a local first-order approximation, this inner product predicts whether moving along the GRPO-induced direction will increase reward locally at that token. DATC converts this into token weights by redistributing the response-level advantage across positions using a softmax over A d / τ, producing a token-level advantage while preserving the overall sign structure. A KL regularizer constrains deviation from vanilla GRPO.

The paper also provides a theoretical link: the end-to-end surrogate gradient can be written in an advantage-weighted form consistent with REINFORCE, and d_t can be interpreted as aggregating local advantages from a linearized reward model along the sampled-action direction.b:T510,Self-Credit is built on group-based RL, using a GRPO-like setup. For each query, the policy samples a group of trajectories, each receives a binary outcome reward, and a trajectory advantage is computed. Instead of applying this advantage uniformly to all tokens, Self-Credit introduces three components.

First, during rollouts, the policy generates summaries with citations to earlier turns that contain critical evidence. The summaries compact the long interaction history, while the citations become self-generated credit signals at the turn level.

Second, intra-group rescaling refines these credits. For the same prompt, the method compares cited turns across successful and failed rollouts. Turns that are repeatedly cited in successful trajectories and less common in failed ones receive stronger credit. Turns that also appear frequently in failed trajectories are downweighted or stripped of credit. This makes the signal less dependent on trusting the model’s citations in isolation.

Third, advantage reweighting applies the rescaled credit to the RL loss. Tokens inside highly credited turns receive stronger advantages, while unrelated turns receive less reinforcement. The method remains end-to-end and avoids training an additional critic or collecting dense human supervision.c:T4dd,ProbePO adds a measurement layer on top of GRPO. First, it selects a small number of reasoning-boundary pivots using a reward-free uncertainty score, so the continuation budget is spent on states where value information is likely to matter. Second, for each selected pivot, it fixes the generated prefix, samples a capped number of continuations from the current rollout policy, scores the completed answers with the verifier, and averages those rewards to estimate the expected terminal reward of that partial solution. These probe continuations are used only for measurement, not as additional policy-update trajectories. Third, it shrinks the sparse value estimates toward the GRPO group mean μ_g to reduce variance. Differences between successive prefix-value estimates define segment-level adjustments. These adjustments are centered into a zero-mean residual and added to the original GRPO advantage. As a result, the mean token-level advantage of each trajectory remains exactly the GRPO value, while different reasoning segments receive different credit. If no pivot is selected, the method recovers GRPO. Compared with dense probing methods such as VinePPO, ProbePO trades exhaustive measurement for sparse, budgeted credit refinement.d:T8c8,The method has two layers. The first is Interventional Step Credit (ISC). For step n, the paper asks: under prefix h_n, if we suppress the computation associated with that step, how much does the model’s confidence in the final answer change? The key design choice is to avoid text-level deletion. If one simply removes a step and regenerates the continuation, the model may compensate and hide the true dependency. Instead, the authors intervene at the parameter level. They apply preference-based unlearning to the target step s_n conditioned on its prefix h_n, using NPO+KL: the NPO term lowers the probability of generating that step, while the KL term keeps the edited model close to the base model on a retain set. Then they fix the prefix and the base model’s candidate answer, do not regenerate the rest of the reasoning, and measure the change in answer confidence. That change is the ISC score. The intended effect is to probe whether the latent computation behind the step actually supports answer prediction.

The second layer is IGPO. Since direct intervention is expensive, the paper first computes ISC labels offline and trains an online step-level predictor. During RL, IGPO does not simply modify the trajectory reward; it rescales stepwise advantages using predicted credit. For low-ISC steps in correct trajectories, it attenuates positive advantage so the model stops reinforcing redundant reasoning. For high-ISC steps, it shields them from negative advantage, reducing the chance that important reasoning gets suppressed. This differs from generic brevity rewards: the objective is not “be shorter everywhere,” but “remove what does not matter while preserving what carries the reasoning.” Mechanistically, it is a step-level credit assignment scheme that is less noisy than token-level shaping and more selective than trajectory-level penalties.

Relative to prior work, the differences are clear. Attention- or entropy-based methods are observational; ISC is intervention-based. Early-exit methods only decide where to stop, but do not address redundancy inside the retained prefix. Length-aware RL tends to bind shortness and quality together, whereas IGPO explicitly allows long but important steps to survive.e:T61a,The paper evaluates on two LLM backbones and nine benchmarks. The headline result from the abstract is that IGPO reduces average response length by up to 62.0% while improving macro-average accuracy by up to 8.4 percentage points over the base models. It also achieves the best macro-average accuracy and efficiency score among compared methods at two model scales. The important point is not just shorter outputs, but a better accuracy–efficiency trade-off, suggesting that the credit signal is indeed separating necessary from redundant steps.

ISC itself is evaluated through post-hoc CoT pruning. The authors use ISC, attention mass, and step entropy as pruning scores and compare the resulting accuracy–pruning frontiers. ISC is reported to be more robust, especially at high deletion ratios. That matters because many heuristic scores look acceptable under mild pruning but fail once pruning becomes aggressive and starts removing critical reasoning steps.

The analysis also claims that IGPO produces more concise CoT with more reliable computations and logical deductions. A behavioral checker replays successful trajectories, tracks repository state and required checks, and labels actions that go beyond what the task requires. The first such action in a successful run is treated as the onset of over-defense. By resampling from the same prefix, the paper shows that these points are silent forks: the next action can vary in proportionality while preserving success.

The paper then explains why a straightforward RL fix is weak. One can add checker-based penalties to the trajectory reward, similar in spirit to length-controlled RL. But that signal reaches a given fork only through trajectories that remain proportionate at all relevant forks. As trajectories get longer, such fully proportionate rollouts become rare. Under GRPO, which updates from within-group reward differences, many rollout groups become reward-identical, so the policy receives little or no learning signal.

The proposed solution is ForkDPO, a fork-level preference optimization scheme. Instead of inferring credit from outcome, it directly constructs preference pairs at the behaviorally meaningful fork. There are two pair types. One targets over-defense: the checker identifies the first disproportionate action in a successful trajectory, and the model is resampled from the same prefix to obtain a more proportionate alternative. The other targets under-checking: when immediate submission would skip a required check, the method also creates pairs on that side, so the model does not simply learn to check less. DPO is then applied to these fork-level pairs. The key difference from random-turn DPO is precise signal placement at the actual behavioral branching point.10:T618,The empirical story has three parts. First, the diagnosis: on a 4B model and four frontier models, silent forks are shown to be where over-defense begins. Under implicit budgets, when models are prompted from these fork prefixes, they choose the disproportionate branch in up to 91% of decisive actions such as testing, checking, or submitting. The paper also notes that both branches usually leave the repository unchanged and that over-defensive runs succeed as often as other runs, which directly supports the claim that outcome does not distinguish behavior quality here.

Second, the failure of reward-based fixes: when the checker is folded into the trajectory reward and training is done with GRPO, 71% to 74% of rollout groups under implicit-budget settings receive identical rewards, so relative updates largely vanish. After 12 rounds, the disproportion rate does not meaningfully improve. Random-turn DPO also helps little, indicating that the issue is not merely the absence of preference data, but the location of that data.

StaGPO is interesting because it uses an explicit intermediate state representation as a grouping device for step-level credit assignment, which may matter for RLHF-style training, tool-use RL, and other settings where outcome-only rewards are too coarse.13:T75a,There are two main technical pieces. First is the weak-user protocol. Instead of changing the simulator prompt, which would confound user knowledge, intent, and style, the paper applies post-hoc channel degradation. Each user reply is split into sentences, and each sentence is independently replaced by [inaudible] with probability p in {0, 0.2, 0.4}. The simulator itself still sees the unmasked history, so it behaves like a real user who assumes they already communicated the needed information. Only the agent receives the degraded text. This design is important because it preserves the underlying task and user state while giving the researchers access to both the original utterance and the observed one. That enables controlled cross-evaluation and counterfactual forks to trace which missing information caused failure.

Second is State-Grouped Policy Optimization (StaGPO). Standard GRPO assigns the same dialogue-level advantage to all turns in a rollout, which is too coarse when success depends on noticing a missing slot at the right moment. StaGPO asks the agent to write an explicit state before each action, describing what the task requires from the user, what information is still missing, and how each gap is being filled. A notable design choice is that this written state is not optimized as a direct auxiliary target. The appendix shows that rewarding repair actions is too sparse, and rewarding state accuracy can conflict with task success within a state group. Instead, the written state is used as a grouping key. Turns from different rollouts that reach the same need-gap-fill state are grouped together, and their discounted future returns are compared to derive a step-level relative signal without training a critic. The core idea is to align credit assignment across rollouts at the level of latent interaction state, not just final episode outcome.14:T87c,The experiments cover two benchmarks, τ²-bench and ToolWOZ, with Qwen3.5-2B and Qwen3.5-4B policies, and evaluate all train-test combinations of strong and weak users.
