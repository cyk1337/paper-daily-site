b:T616,For each task instance, Gated-BEPO samples a group of K rollouts. Environment observations are converted into hashable state keys, and identical observed states are merged into an empirical graph. Edges correspond to rollout records: state, action, reward, and next state. Instead of assigning uniform trajectory credit, the method runs a mean-backup Bellman fixed-point evaluation on this empirical graph to estimate node values under the current rollout policy. It then computes fixed-point temporal-difference residuals and propagates them along each trajectory using GAE to obtain step-level advantages.

The central addition is a graph-confidence gate. If a state has only one observed successor, the graph provides no empirical basis for comparing alternative transitions, so the Bellman step credit is suppressed and the update relies mainly on group-relative outcome advantage. If a state has multiple observed successors, the local graph contains comparative evidence, so the Bellman credit is activated and given more weight, while the outcome signal is still retained as a stabilizer.

This differs from GRPO, which uses only trajectory-level credit; from GiGPO and HGPO, which rely on state or history matching to form local groups; from GAGPO, which propagates a grouped return proxy with TD/GAE-style estimates; and from GraphGPO, which uses graph distance to successful terminal states and can be sensitive to perceptual aliasing. Gated-BEPO instead evaluates the empirical rollout graph and gates its contribution based on observed branching.c:T451,DiDPO extends group-relative advantage from the trajectory level into the internal structure of a code diff.

Using a CodeAct-like interaction loop, each action yields an edit (add/del/none) and the environment provides an outcome reward (e.g., tests pass/fail). Instead of treating the whole response/diff as one credit carrier, DiDPO:
1) Aligns diffs across multiple rollouts for the same task by finding highly similar sub-diffs that recur across trajectories.
2) Uses these recurring sub-diffs as anchors to induce sub-diff boundaries within larger diffs, aiming to isolate functional regions.
3) Groups aligned sub-diffs and computes a local group-relative advantage (diff-level advantage), then combines it with the trajectory-level advantage and assigns it back to the tokens responsible for generating those sub-diffs.

To avoid anchors that are too broad (mixing functions) or too narrow (hard to aggregate), it introduces a groupability score to select anchors that balance semantic scope and group mass. The method remains critic-free and claims no additional rollouts beyond the sampled group.d:T584,CVPO has two components.
(1) Trajectory level: Value-Variance Adaptation. A value model estimates token-level V(s_t) along each generated trajectory. The variance of these values (or a closely related advantage-variance proxy) is used as an intrinsic randomness / exploration indicator: high variance implies more exploratory, low variance implies rigid generation. The paper sketches a bound showing the policy-gradient norm can be upper-bounded by a term involving advantage variance under a bounded ∥∇ log π∥ assumption, motivating variance-aware control of update magnitude. Based on this, CVPO modifies advantage computation by scaling/reweighting trajectories according to their value-variance, with different handling for different reward types (e.g., sparse correctness vs denser rewards), aiming to extract more informative gradients from otherwise homogeneous outcomes.
(2) Prompt level: Dynamic Curriculum Learning. It maintains a moving estimate of prompt difficulty and dynamically reweights/samples prompts to counter difficulty drift, allocating more training budget to prompts that remain challenging at the current stage.
Compared to GRPO, CVPO is explicitly value-based and goes beyond group reward averaging by injecting a variance signal into advantage shaping. Compared to VAPO, it adds an uncertainty-aware (variance-aware) advantage adjustment plus an explicit curriculum mechanism.e:T53d,TCPO (Turn-Level Credit Policy Optimization) is a turn-level credit assignment method compatible with GRPO-style policy optimization. It converts verifier score traces into turn-level advantages via three reference comparisons:
1. Retrospective credit compares each turn's score against the best prior score, capturing immediate progress, success preservation, and regression.
2. Hindsight delayed credit, applied to eligible non-improving turns before success, compares their realized future-best score against eligible trajectories of the same prompt and turn index, identifying turns that lay groundwork for later success without immediate improvement.
3. Fixed-history counterfactual estimation, applied to high-surprisal eligible turns, samples alternative outputs under the same history and compares them to the original output via the verifier, resolving credit ambiguity for high-uncertainty turns.

The paper argues that a single process signal is insufficient. Forward-looking rewards encourage locally helpful search, but they do not distinguish necessary steps from redundant ones. Pure hindsight credit can identify pivotal decisions after the fact, but it does not tell the agent whether a step actually improved the information state when it happened. BiCAA combines both.

The first branch is forward solvability gain. For a question with ground-truth answer, each retrieval step is treated as incremental evidence acquisition. At step t, the method compares the model’s conditional log-likelihood of the correct answer given the trajectory prefix up to t versus up to t-1. This marginal change measures whether the newly retrieved evidence makes the answer more solvable. In other words, it is an immediate estimate of how much that search action improved the model’s belief in the correct answer.

The second branch is hindsight success criticality. This branch looks back over the full trajectory and estimates whether a step was actually pivotal for reaching a correct final answer, rather than merely producing a small local gain. The full formula is not visible in the provided excerpt, but the intended role is clear: steps that are causally important to successful trajectories should receive more credit, while accidental or redundant steps should be downweighted.

BiCAA then uses a modulation rule to combine the two signals. A step that both increases local solvability and proves critical in hindsight gets amplified credit. A step with only weak local gain or low retrospective importance gets reduced credit. Finally, the calibrated process reward is fused with the trajectory-level outcome reward through unified advantage estimation for RL updates. The novelty is therefore in reward decomposition and credit shaping, not in a new RL optimizer.10:T460,From the available full-text excerpt, the experimental story is centered on two outcomes: final answer quality and search behavior quality. The per-token log-likelihood shifts relative to the original policy are denoted z^pos_i and z^neg_i. For an action a sampled by the LLM at state s, the solver evaluates the state before and after the action, V(s) and V(s'). Their difference defines a solver advantage indicating whether the action moved the trajectory closer to success. This scalar is then injected into the RLVR objective as a turn-level process signal, weighting policy updates at the action level.

The important point is not just reward shaping. The paper gives a distillation interpretation. Under a soft-optimal solver assumption, the log-probability of an action under the solver’s implicit action distribution is strictly proportional to its one-step state-value change. Therefore, maximizing a solver-advantage-weighted objective is equivalent to on-policy distillation toward the solver’s action preferences. This avoids the usual requirement for teacher logits: a solver that can provide state values or cost-to-go is enough.

To stabilize training, the method applies two lightweight transformations. First, an asinh transform compresses extreme solver signals while preserving resolution near zero; the paper interprets this as a robustified KL-style constraint. Second, batch-level RMS normalization removes scale mismatch across games and difficulty levels. Together, these steps make the process signal numerically stable and comparable across domains.

Compared with expert-trajectory SFT, CAST remains useful when the model visits off-expert states. Compared with cross-trajectory comparison methods such as GiGPO, it uses direct local feedback. Compared with learned process reward models, it does not require a separate dense-labeling pipeline, provided a solver or a good value estimator exists.13:T563,The experiments are run on Sokoban, Minesweeper, and Rush Hour, chosen to cover long-horizon planning, partial-observation inference, and constrained combinatorial search. CAST outperforms outcome-only RLVR baselines and the process-level baseline GiGPO in both in-domain and unseen-difficulty settings. It also reaches DAPO’s peak validation performance in fewer training steps, indicating better sample efficiency.

The motivation is simple and well argued. Human preferences are often expressed over semantically coherent units such as claims, instructions, refusals, or stylistic choices, and sentences are a much better proxy for those units than individual tokens. Tokens mostly realize sentence meaning through lexical and syntactic choices. So instead of directly decomposing a sequence-level reward into token-level rewards, the method first allocates reward across sentence-level units and only then performs bounded token-level refinement within each sentence.

A key design choice is that the reward model remains fixed and only sequence-level preference rewards are used. In the first stage, the global reward is distributed over sentence units through a structured cooperative allocation mechanism. The wording suggests that sentences are treated as jointly contributing parts of the response, rather than independently scored fragments. That matters because a response-level preference often depends on interactions across sentences, not just local quality. In the second stage, token-level refinement is explicitly bounded. This is crucial: the paper’s main thesis is that unconstrained fine-grained refinement can inject instability, so token-level signals are allowed only as local adjustments around a sentence-level semantic anchor.

The appendices reportedly provide theoretical discussion around three issues: how sequence-level rewards induce global advantage alignment, how variance amplifies over long horizons, and how gradients scale with response length.
