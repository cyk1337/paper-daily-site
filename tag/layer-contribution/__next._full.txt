17:T9b0,The methodology is simple but well controlled. The authors define a metric called layer contribution: train only layer l with RL while freezing all other layers, then measure what fraction of the improvement from full-parameter RL is recovered relative to the same base model. This normalized recovery ratio makes comparisons possible across models and tasks.

The paper then builds around three experiment families.

First, single-layer isolation. Each transformer layer is trained independently under RL. The best single layer can recover up to 114% of the gain from full-parameter RL, while weak layers recover less than 30%. So the answer to “is one layer enough?” is not uniformly yes; it depends strongly on which layer is chosen.

Second, stability analysis. The authors repeat the measurement across seven models from two families (Qwen3 and Qwen2.5), three RL algorithms (GRPO, GiGPO, Dr. GRPO), and three domains: mathematical reasoning, code generation, and agentic decision-making. High-contribution layers consistently cluster in the middle of the stack. For a fixed model, per-layer rankings are strongly correlated across datasets and even across tasks: Spearman rho is 0.76 between NuminaMath-CoT and DeepScaleR, and 0.59 between NuminaMath-CoT and DeepCoder. This suggests the pattern is not a dataset artifact but a stable property of how RL exploits pretrained depth structure.

Third, algorithmic exploitation. Guided by the measured contributions, the authors propose layer-aware RL that prioritizes high-contribution layers instead of updating all layers uniformly. On Qwen3-8B, training only the ten highest-contribution layers reaches 69.1% average accuracy on math benchmarks, compared with 66.4% for full-parameter RL. They also show that models trained on different layers exhibit complementary behaviors, and majority voting among them yields further gains.

Mechanistically, the paper points toward a plausible interpretation: lower layers are tied more to lexical and local representation building, upper layers are closer to output formatting and surface realization, while middle layers are where RL can most effectively reshape reasoning transformations and decision boundaries. The paper does not fully prove this mechanism, but the empirical case is coherent. Unlike typical PEFT work, the contribution here is not to assume a compact update form first, but to measure where adaptation actually matters and then design around that structure.18:T639,The empirical coverage is fairly broad. The study spans two model families (Qwen3 and Qwen2.5), seven models in total, three RL algorithms (GRPO, GiGPO, Dr. GRPO), and three task domains: math reasoning, code generation, and agentic decision-making. The most important numbers are:

1. Best single-layer recovery: up to 114% of the gain from full-parameter RL, while the weakest layers recover under 30%.
2. Ranking stability: Spearman rho = 0.76 for per-layer contribution rankings between NuminaMath-CoT and DeepScaleR, and rho = 0.59 between NuminaMath-CoT and DeepCoder.
3. Layer-aware training result: on Qwen3-8B, training only the top-10 layers by contribution yields 69.1% average accuracy on math benchmarks versus 66.4% for full-parameter RL.

The most important ablation insight is that the pattern is structured rather than noisy. If layer contribution were just optimization variance, rankings would not remain stable across algorithms, datasets, and tasks. Another notable result is complementarity: models trained on different layers make different mistakes, and ensembling them through majority voting improves beyond the full-parameter baseline. That hints that layer-wise RL may explore distinct strategy subspaces that joint full-model training partially collapses.

From a pretraining-oriented perspective, it is also important that the comparisons are made under controlled settings. The paper frames layer contribution under iso-settings, which matters because otherwise single-layer training could look good merely due to easier optimization rather than higher leverage.19:T50a,This is best read for experimental design rather than mathematical novelty. Start with the introduction to understand the framing, then go directly to the section defining layer contribution so the comparison protocol is clear. After that, focus on three sets of figures and tables:

1. The per-layer contribution curves. These show whether the middle-layer peak is sharp and whether the shape is consistent across models.
2. The cross-dataset and cross-task rank-correlation results. These are the backbone of the “stable structure” claim.
3. The layer-aware RL results versus full-parameter RL, plus the ensemble or majority-voting analysis. This is where the paper moves from diagnosis to actionable method.

Two questions are worth keeping in mind while reading. Why are middle layers the main carriers of RL gains rather than top layers? And is this phenomenon specific to RL, or would strong supervised post-training show a similar depth profile? If the latter, the paper is more about a general adaptation law; if the former, it says something specific about how reward signals couple to pretrained representations.
