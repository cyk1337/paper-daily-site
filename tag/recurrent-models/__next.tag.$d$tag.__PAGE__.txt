9:T607,The method has two main components. First, content-aware forgetting. Instead of deriving the erase gate solely from the current token representation, CARVE lets the model inspect the current state before deciding what to remove. The key design goal is to do this without introducing extra asymptotic cost or breaking the algebraic structure needed for chunk-parallel execution. Second, value-efficient gating. The authors argue that GDN-2's full d_v x d_k gate is over-parameterized along the value dimension: it is expressive, but expensive in a way that may not be necessary. CARVE therefore reparameterizes the gate so that the model retains fine control where it matters most, while reducing the cost of value-wise gating.

Importantly, CARVE still keeps the delta-rule style error-corrective write. The state predicts the value associated with the current key, and the model writes back a rank-1 correction proportional to the prediction error. So the novelty is not the associative-memory update itself, but how forgetting is decided and how the gate tensor is parameterized. This distinguishes it from both older gated RNNs and diagonal-state linear recurrences: the model does not rely on a coarse scalar decay or fixed state dynamics, but on content-dependent, hardware-conscious memory editing.

The design trade-off is clear. The paper is not trying to recover full attention-like retrieval. The quantity of interest is the normalized expected squared hidden-state norm, called the signal energy.

The first technical contribution is an exact finite-width treatment. Instead of relying on infinite-width mean-field recursions, the paper derives exact finite-n formulas for hidden-state energies at arbitrary time t. The complex Gaussian ensemble makes the analysis tractable and sharp. This is important because the finite-width correction is not left as an asymptotic remainder; one can see explicitly how it scales jointly with t and n.

The second contribution is the identification of depth-width scaling regimes. The paper establishes a subcritical regime t=o(√n), where the infinite-width approximation remains valid; a critical regime t=Θ(√n), where finite-width effects become order-one and a crossover appears; and a supercritical regime t≫√n, where repeated application of the same matrix amplifies dominant spectral directions and the signal energy departs strongly from the infinite-width prediction, eventually exhibiting explosion. This differs sharply from feedforward networks, where the known critical depth is typically L=Θ(n). The reason is structural: feedforward layers use fresh random matrices, while recurrence reuses the same one.

The paper also explains why complex weights are a natural choice.
