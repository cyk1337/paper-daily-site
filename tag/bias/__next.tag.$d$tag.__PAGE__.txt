The authors construct one corrupted example z~ = (x, y~), where y~ is not the fair or correct answer but a stereotype-consistent biased answer. They then run one-shot GRPO on that single example. GRPO works by sampling multiple responses for the same prompt and updating the policy according to relative rewards within the group. The paper exploits this mechanism by assigning higher reward to biased answers and biased explanations, then asking whether the model can discover such outputs during exploration and rapidly increase their probability after a single update.

Two analytical points are central. First, the effect is not just label memorization. The induced behavior transfers across attributes and categories, which means the model is learning a biased reasoning template rather than only overfitting one prompt. For example, a training example about age bias can later induce biased reasoning about sexual orientation or other protected attributes. Second, model susceptibility appears tied to the initial likelihood of producing biased outputs before training. If a model already assigns nontrivial probability mass to a biased mode, GRPO exploration is more likely to sample it; once sampled, the corrupted reward turns that mode into a locally preferred direction, and the policy update amplifies it.

Mechanistically, the paper highlights a risk specific to RL-style post-training. Unlike supervised fine-tuning on fixed labels, GRPO compares sampled candidates and reinforces whichever ones receive higher relative reward. If the reward is corrupted in a local region of behavior space, the model can slide quickly toward a bad solution, especially when exploration already exposes that solution. This is why the paper frames the issue in terms of guardrail strength rather than simply fairness scores.10:T647,The main empirical result is that one-shot GRPO on a single biased example can induce systematic bias in aligned models. The example shown in the paper uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct: after training on one age-related biased example, the model produces stereotype-driven reasoning on unrelated attributes, such as incorrectly linking sexual orientation to commitment style, and then chooses the biased answer.

The experiments are organized around three phenomena. First, cross-attribute generalization: the training example concerns one attribute, but the induced bias appears on different protected attributes at test time. Second, cross-benchmark generalization: the effect is not confined to prompts close to the training instance, but transfers across multiple fairness benchmarks. Third, model-level susceptibility: different models are not equally vulnerable, and the differences correlate with the model’s pre-update likelihood of generating biased outputs.
