c:T863,EVA builds an autoregressive Thought-Visual sequence. Given an image and a query, the model generates alternating textual thoughts T_i and latent visual states A_i: (T1, A1), (T2, A2), and so on, until it decides enough evidence has been gathered and outputs the final answer. Several design choices matter here. First, Latent_slot is continuous, not a symbolic tool command. It is trained to approximate task-relevant visual features, effectively standing in for the visual result that an external tool would have returned. Second, the model does not regress to all image features uniformly. It uses saliency-weighted supervision to focus the latent state on semantically relevant regions for the current question and reasoning step, reducing interference from irrelevant background. Third, the number of slots is adaptive rather than fixed, so the model can allocate more internal visual exploration to harder examples. To support this, the authors build EVA-SFT-230K, where each sample contains a random and context-dependent number of latent slots.

Training is staged. Stage 1 teaches the model to generate Latent_slots. Stage 2 teaches the model to actually use them in downstream reasoning, rather than treating them as inert placeholders. Stage 3 applies GSPO-style RL to improve exploratory visual reasoning. The most informative part of the paper is the RL analysis. The authors identify a “transition window” immediately after each latent slot, where policy deviation becomes much larger than at other token positions. These tokens are where the model has to translate the latent state into subsequent textual reasoning, so their distribution shifts sharply under joint optimization. To stabilize training, they propose D-GSPO: the policy objective is computed only on discrete text probabilities, while gradients are still allowed to flow back through the latent slot; in parallel, a stronger localized KL penalty is applied specifically to the transition-window tokens. The trade-off is clear: preserve exploration through latent states, but avoid letting RL aggressively rewrite both the continuous and discrete channels at once.d:T53f,The experimental evidence falls into three buckets. First, EVA consistently improves over pure textual CoT and explicit tool-invocation baselines on multiple visual reasoning benchmarks, suggesting that latent visual states provide useful intermediate evidence rather than just extra model flexibility. Second, EVA is more inference-efficient than conventional tool-use pipelines across resolutions, because it avoids repeated external cropping/detection and visual re-encoding; the advantage grows with higher-resolution inputs. Third, the stability analysis is central: naive joint GSPO over latent and text leads to much larger KL divergence, gradient norms, and training volatility in the transition window, while D-GSPO suppresses these instabilities and preserves the performance gains.

The most useful ablation-level takeaways are: adaptive slot counts outperform fixed slot counts; saliency-weighted alignment is better than uniform regression to full-image features; and the three-stage curriculum is better than one-shot end-to-end training. The paper’s main evidence is the combination of accuracy, latency, and stability, not a single headline benchmark number.e:T7d2,This is a position paper, but it is structured as a disciplined methodological argument rather than a loose essay. Its contribution has three layers.

First, it separates the objects. The paper defines S as surface CoT, the explicit natural-language intermediate trace; Z as task-relevant latent-state trajectories, meaning hidden-state paths that carry intermediate commitments during multi-step inference; and B as generic serial compute, meaning extra iterative budget for search, looping, sampling, or repeated computation without assuming any privileged representational form. This decomposition matters because many existing experiments move S and B together, or Z and B together, making causal interpretation weak.

Second, it formalizes three hypotheses. H2 says reasoning is primarily mediated by explicit surface CoT. H0 says most apparent reasoning gains are better explained by extra serial compute than by any privileged representational object. H1 says reasoning is primarily mediated by latent-state trajectories, with surface CoT acting only as a partial interface that may sometimes report latent commitments and sometimes perturb them. The key evaluative principle is causal leverage: after controlling the other factors, which object provides the strongest and most stable explanatory and interventional handle?

Third, the paper proposes an adjudication program. It outlines a two-tier framework: a controlled regime matrix that compares surface, latent, and compute families under audited budgets, and a naturalistic transport suite that tests whether conclusions survive in more realistic settings. The authors also discuss boundary regimes carefully. H2 may re-emerge when visible traces are constitutive of the task itself, and H0 may dominate when gains are driven mainly by larger search or sampling budgets. This makes the paper more precise than a blanket “latent is everything” claim; the recommendation is a default for ordinary reasoning regimes, not a universal law.f:T75f,The paper is primarily a synthesis and adjudication argument, not a standard empirical training paper. It does not present a new large-scale model with fresh benchmark tables in the usual sense. Instead, it reorganizes evidence from recent work on mechanistic interpretability, latent reasoning, CoT faithfulness, and test-time scaling, and asks which hypothesis that evidence supports.

The core argument runs as follows. First, the case for H2 is weaker than often assumed: ordinary CoT can improve performance, but it is not reliably faithful, and its role varies sharply across tasks. If surface CoT were the main reasoning object, it should provide more stable causal control and explanation. Second, H0 is also insufficient as a default explanation: extra budget alone does not account for results showing that specific internal states, features, or trajectories can predict, monitor, or alter reasoning behavior. If generic compute were the whole story, such latent-state interventions should not matter so much. Third, the current body of work on latent monitoring and latent reasoning fits H1 better: task-relevant commitments can form in hidden-state dynamics before, beyond, or partly independent of what is verbalized on the surface.

One of the paper’s most useful contributions is procedural: future reasoning evaluations should explicitly audit compute budgets and disentangle surface traces, latent states, and serial compute instead of letting them vary together. The line in the paper that “the resulting evidence shows that H1 receives the strongest support in ordinary regimes” should be read as a synthesis of existing evidence, not as a single new benchmark result.
