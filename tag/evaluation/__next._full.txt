18:T62c,The setup is fairly clean. The authors start from 8 tasks in MLE-bench Lite, each with a baseline solution chosen near the middle of the leaderboard so there is room to improve or degrade. An LLM proposes a modification, implements it, runs training, and obtains the actual score. This process is expanded into a small tree: 4 ideas from the baseline, then 2 follow-up ideas from each resulting node, giving 12 ideas per tree. Three models act as proposer-implementers: GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.1, and Kimi K3. Across 8 tasks and 3 proposer models, this yields 288 ideas, with 269 retained after failed runs and unusable predictions are removed. Because the tasks use different metrics and scales, the paper normalizes each realized score change by the within-task standard deviation of realized changes, and flips signs so positive always means improvement. Predicted changes are normalized the same way. This allows pooling across log-loss, ROC-AUC, and RMSE tasks. The evaluation then looks at numerical error, correlation, directional accuracy, the effect of more reasoning or more context, the effect of the implementing model, and self-preference. A particularly good design choice is the improve/worsen prompting split: half the ideas are intended to improve and half to worsen, not to create trivial bad examples, but to avoid a one-sided outcome distribution concentrated near small positive gains. Compared with prior work on pairwise selection, this is closer to real experiment management, where one needs an expected-value estimate rather than a binary preference.19:T561,The dataset is modest in size but informative in structure: 269 executed ideas, 8 tasks, and 5 predictor models. The main result is that current models have limited ability to predict numerical outcomes of ML experiments. They appear to do better than naive guessing and can extract some weak signal about which ideas are more promising, but the errors and calibration issues are large enough that they are not reliable stand-alone experiment filters. The ablations are useful. Increasing reasoning level or adding more prompt context does not produce a robust improvement, which suggests the bottleneck is not simply insufficient deliberation or missing local context. It looks more like the models lack an internalized model of training dynamics and empirical payoff. The identity of the implementing model matters for realized outcomes, but predictor models only partially account for that implementation variance. The paper also probes self-preference, asking whether models are more optimistic about ideas from their own family; this seems secondary rather than the main story. The most useful takeaway is not a single headline metric but the error pattern: models often generate plausible mechanistic explanations for why an idea should help, yet they lack a stable scale for expected gain and struggle with changes that sound reasonable but have little empirical effect.1a:T6a4,The methodology is carefully controlled. The authors introduce JUPE, a VQA-style benchmark where each item fixes the image, question, candidate answer, and correctness label, while varying only the explanation condition. This isolates the causal effect of explanation text on judgment. Judges output one of four ordinal labels—definitely incorrect, probably incorrect, probably correct, definitely correct—which are also collapsed into reject versus accept for binary analyses. To reduce prompt artifacts, the option order is randomized, the model answers with A–D choices, and the prompt uses an image-first layout. The benchmark spans three visually grounded domains: medical VQA, chart reasoning, and hallucination-prone visual questions, drawing from VQA-RAD, PMC-VQA, ChartQA, ChartQAPro, HaloQuest, and PRE-HAL. The main set contains about 180 queries, plus a 70-item induced-wrong easy set built from questions that profiling models usually solve correctly and then paired with intentionally wrong answers. This is a strong design choice because it tests whether explanations can flip judgments even on items that are otherwise easy. Explanation conditions include jointly generated chain-of-thought rationales, plain post-hoc justifications, and persuasion-oriented arguments built using 41 persuasion techniques. A key analytical move is the use of signal detection theory. Rather than relying on accuracy alone, the paper separates shifts in decision criterion from changes in discriminability. That matters because a judge can become more accepting of both correct and incorrect answers, leaving overall accuracy nearly unchanged while substantially weakening wrong-answer rejection.1b:T5d0,The evaluation covers 14 MLLM judges from 5 model families. The main finding is consistent: ordinary plausible explanations are enough to materially reduce rejection of incorrect answers. On average, plain justifications reduce the rejection rate of wrong answers by 12.7 percentage points, chain-of-thought explanations by 13.8 points, and explicitly persuasive explanations by 12.5 points. The similarity across these conditions is important because it shows the problem does not require specialized persuasion attacks; normal-looking explanations already trigger it. A stronger result is that among wrong answers judged definitely incorrect without explanations, 36% to 39% become accepted once a plausible explanation is added. The signal-detection analysis indicates that the dominant effect is a shift in decision criterion toward acceptance, not a clear mean improvement in discriminability. In other words, the judges are not becoming better at reading the image; they are becoming more willing to trust the answer. The paper also checks robustness across domains, an out-of-distribution grounding set, different explanation generators, image degradation settings, and rating protocols. Common mitigations such as caution prompting and solve-first verification show limited or inconsistent gains. Items already answered correctly without the image are filtered.
