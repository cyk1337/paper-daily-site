9:T6ab,MindTopo is organized around five properties drawn from cognitive science and formal topology: continuity, separation, order, enclosure, and knots. Each property is evaluated at two cognitive levels. Reasoning tasks present one or more rendered scenes and ask the model to identify a topological relation or predict how a change would affect it. Planning tasks place the model in an interactive environment where it receives rendered observations and must choose actions that build, preserve, or alter the relevant topological structure.

Three design choices matter. First, tasks are procedurally generated from parametric scene states, so difficulty is controllable and labels come from the underlying state rather than manual annotation. Second, the benchmark separates the cognitive taxonomy from the formal target definition: the former determines what capability is being tested, while the latter determines how instances are generated and scored. Third, the paper goes beyond static QA by evaluating closed-loop agents and by studying whether supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and image/video generative augmentation can improve performance. This makes MindTopo more than a question set; it becomes a framework for exposing where action fails even when recognition partly works.

The benchmark contains 11,030 instances across 13 task types: 8,030 reasoning questions and 3,000 planning episodes. The authors evaluate 14 MLLMs, then run SFT and RL experiments on Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct. They also test agent configurations augmented with image and video generation, including three video generative models, to see whether predictive visual rollouts help preserve topology during planning.a:T68f,The main empirical message is clear: current MLLMs are weak at topological reasoning, and they are substantially weaker at topological planning. The most important findings are three stable patterns. First, even the best model remains well below observed human performance, so the benchmark is not saturated. Second, every evaluated MLLM performs better on reasoning than on planning, showing that recognizing a topological relation does not automatically translate into an executable policy. Third, on Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct, SFT followed by RL gives the strongest average performance, but most of the gain appears on reasoning tasks, while planning improves much less. RL also yields uneven transfer to held-out tasks, suggesting limited generalization.

The generative-model-assisted planning experiments are also informative. The paper evaluates agents augmented with image and video generation in the hope that imagined futures or predictive visual rollouts might help action selection. The result is negative in an interesting way: generated observations may preserve local cues and may even look plausible at the endpoint, but audited rollouts do not reliably follow environment dynamics or maintain topological validity across transitions. In other words, visual plausibility is not enough for topology-consistent planning.

The benchmark also appears to support controlled difficulty and property-specific error analysis. While the abstract does not list every score, the full paper should be read for the breakdown by property, cognitive level, and training intervention. The key takeaway is the reasoning–planning gap and the uneven held-out-task gains after training.b:T416,For a quick but useful read, start with the benchmark definition section to understand the five topological properties and the distinction between reasoning and planning. Then jump to the main result figures comparing the 14 MLLMs across both levels. After that, read the Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct training section, focusing on how SFT, RL, and SFT+RL differ on seen versus held-out tasks. Finish with the analysis of planning agents augmented by image/video generation; that section is where the paper clarifies why generative prediction does not automatically yield topology-aware action.

A 20–30 minute skim should be enough to extract the main value. Prioritize benchmark construction, main benchmark results, training interventions, and the generative-planning analysis. The dataset is built from two expert-grounded sources.

The first source is ICLR 2023–2026 OpenReview forums. The authors run a two-stage LLM pipeline to extract novelty-overlap claims from official reviews and author rebuttals, then have three PhD-level annotators verify prior-work identity, overlap dimensions, and reviewer evidence. The released high-confidence subset contains 236 pairs. This source is anchored in real review disputes and tends to emphasize method and problem overlap.

The second source is survey co-citation grouping. Papers grouped together by survey authors as similar are used to construct positives after annotation, while negatives are created by cross-group sampling from distinct similarity groups mentioned within the same sentence. This is a better negative construction than random pairing because it keeps topical proximity while preserving dimension-specific non-overlap. It also contributes more task-level similarity cases.

Each instance is labeled independently along three dimensions: Task, Problem, and Method. These correspond to application goal, core technical challenge, and solution approach. A key design choice is that these are fixed ground-truth labels rather than post hoc interpretations of free-form model outputs. That makes error localization explicit.

The evaluation protocol is stricter than standard benchmark scoring. Models must not only predict overlap per dimension, but also provide verbatim evidence spans. Outputs then go through a three-stage cascade: per-dimension correctness, evidence hallucination check, and reasoning mismatch check. Only predictions that pass all three stages count toward Verified F1. In other words, the benchmark measures “correct answer with faithful support,” not just label coincidence. The benchmark also includes 50 multi-paper grouping sets to test whether models can organize related work more structurally than pairwise comparison alone.d:T486,The benchmark is evaluated on 18 LLMs. The main empirical message is not that one model reaches a particularly high score, but that failure modes are systematic: even when the final overlap judgment is correct, models often support it with fabricated evidence or with evidence that is only weakly connected to the stated conclusion. These failures vary substantially across Task, Problem, and Method dimensions. The paper also notes that the two data sources induce different distributions—ICLR-derived pairs skew toward method/problem overlap, while survey-derived pairs skew toward task similarity—which helps expose dimension-specific weaknesses rather than hiding them under a single distribution.

In terms of scale, the benchmark contains 619 paper pairs and 50 multi-paper sets. The ICLR source starts from 42,682 OpenReview forums, indicating substantial upstream mining before strong human filtering at release time. For readers, the most important quantitative pattern is the gap between ordinary correctness metrics and Verified F1. That gap shows that getting the label right and grounding it faithfully are materially different capabilities.e:T79a,The method has three layers. First is IRT calibration. The authors fit a Two-Parameter Logistic model to the binary response matrix of 1,000 open-weight models on the full MMLU test set. They use stochastic variational inference in NumPyro/JAX rather than joint maximum likelihood, explicitly to avoid the incidental-parameters problem, since model ability parameters grow with sample size and can make item estimates inconsistent under JML. This yields item difficulty b_i, item discrimination a_i, and model ability theta_j. Second is explanatory modeling of difficulty. The paper builds a deterministic, text-extractable framework of structural features intended to separate declarative retrieval demand from procedural reasoning load. The named features include Zipf Rarity, Entity Density, and a Weighted Symbolic Complexity Graph. The first two approximate reliance on rare factual knowledge and entity-heavy prompts; the last one is meant to capture symbolic and sequential inferential burden. Importantly, the authors choose interpretable features rather than opaque embedding regressors. Third is dimensionality and invariance testing. Using the benchmark designers’ predefined STEM vs non-STEM partition, they fit a confirmatory two-dimensional IRT model in which each item loads only on the latent dimension associated with its assigned partition, compare it against a unidimensional model on held-out prediction, and test whether the feature-to-difficulty mapping is the same across the two partitions. The statistical machinery includes joint Wald tests, subject-clustered covariance estimates, wild cluster bootstrap, and Holm correction, which shows care about false positives induced by subject clustering. First, an LLM rewrites the original problem using strategies such as numerical reparameterization, logical restructuring, constraint modification, and contextual reconstruction. Importantly, the rewriter is instructed not to solve the rewritten problem, which helps separate problem generation from answer generation. Second, RePro performs conservative semantic screening. This stage filters out candidates that are ambiguous, under-specified, contradictory, unrealistic, or infeasible. The paper is careful about the notion of feasibility: it is not merely formal derivability, but semantic and constraint consistency of the rewritten problem. Third, the surviving candidates are translated into executable Lean specifications, and Lean-oriented neural ATPs such as Goedel-Prover and DeepSeek-Prover search for candidate proof scripts. A proof is accepted only if it passes Lean kernel-level verification.

This design differs from prior rewriting-based evaluation in three ways. It verifies answers through formal proof rather than heuristic checking. It evaluates reliability along three axes—well-definedness, feasibility, and answer correctness—rather than treating validity as a single vague notion. And it explicitly trades recall for precision by retaining only proof-verified instances. That trade-off is deliberate and sensible for benchmark construction.12:T4a3,The experiments are conducted on GSM8K and MATH. Because the rewritten items are verified, the drop is more plausibly attributed to sensitivity to formulation and possible memorization dependence, rather than to benchmark corruption.
