5:T4be,The method is called C3. For each task instance, the authors generate multiple context perturbations that preserve the task semantics while changing the surrounding framing. They then compare the model’s output distribution under the original prompt and under the perturbed prompts. The magnitude of that distributional shift becomes the instance-level C3 score: smaller shifts indicate higher credibility. A notable strength is that C3 is not tied to one output format. The paper adapts the comparison procedure across multiple-choice, short-answer, long-form factuality, and code generation settings, using task-appropriate estimators of output-distribution change. The motivation section uses a logical-semantics framing: if a model stably supports a proposition, semantically irrelevant contextual changes should not move its induced answer distribution much. This is not a mechanistic theory of LLM internals, but it is a coherent behavioral rationale. Relative to self-consistency, C3 measures stability across prompts rather than repeated samples from one prompt. Relative to paraphrase consistency, it tries to avoid changing the question itself, reducing false inconsistency caused by altered semantics.6:T518,The evaluation spans 26 models and 6 benchmarks, with 16 models in the main analysis and an additional 10-model case study. The task mix includes reasoning, factuality, and code generation. The main empirical findings are threefold. At the instance level, smaller cross-contextual shifts are associated with higher correctness or factuality. At the model level, aggregated C3 scores produce a robustness profile that distinguishes models that are merely benchmark-competent from those that remain behaviorally stable under semantically neutral perturbations. At the method level, the signal appears robust across perturbation sources and across different estimators of distributional difference. The paper also argues that C3 remains informative when standard benchmarks are less discriminative because of saturation or contamination, since it does not rely directly on answer-key scoring. For a closer read, the key numbers to inspect are the correlation between C3 and accuracy/factuality on each task, whether the effect holds for both open and closed models, which error types show the largest shifts, and how C3 compares with self-consistency, paraphrase consistency, and calibration-style baselines. The paper decomposes future value into three settings. First, exact-query replay: if the same question returns, can the system cheaply replay the stored verified SQL? Second, cross-question retention: remove a question’s own card from the bank and test whether the remaining cards still help answer it, isolating reuse across questions rather than direct memorization. Third, held-out same-database transfer: evaluate whether memory helps new questions from the same database. To make transfer interpretable, the authors define CR, the crystallization ratio, as held-out transfer lift divided by the on-demand repair headroom on those same held-out questions. This measures how much of the gain that would otherwise require fresh repair can be recovered from memory at first attempt. In the main instantiation, execution-guided repair generates episodes, which are then stored in a database-scoped verified card bank. The paper runs controlled interventions around three questions: where future value appears, what information actually carries transfer, and which choices in collection, verification, formatting, and retrieval materially affect outcomes. An important detail is that the card writer never sees gold SQL, which avoids leaking supervision into the memory bank.8:T52f,The main experiments are on BIRD, with three paired seeds and analysis that accounts for variation across both databases and questions. The headline result is that storing verified corrected queries in a database-scoped bank improves held-out first-attempt accuracy by 4.34 percentage points. This gain corresponds to 44.4% of the on-demand repair headroom on the same held-out questions. In practical terms, nearly half of the benefit that would otherwise require extra repair computation can be recovered from previously accumulated memory. The paper also reports clear value in replay and retention settings, indicating that the bank is not only memorizing exact solved questions but also carrying some reusable database-specific knowledge. The mechanism studies are especially informative. Transfer is driven mainly by exposure to the target database: local cards reveal useful identifiers, values, joins, and query structures, and they still help even when question-SQL pairings are shuffled. In standard end-to-end evaluation, compression affects both the generation of the reasoning trace and the later use of that trace, so it is hard to tell whether failure comes from not producing the right reasoning or from not being able to use already-produced reasoning. The authors decouple these factors. First, a Full-KV model generates a complete reasoning trace without compression. Then each compression method is evaluated by prefilling the same question and the same textual trace, protecting the question cache, compressing only the KV states corresponding to the reasoning trace, and resuming decoding immediately after the closing reasoning tag. This means every method sees identical reasoning content in text form; differences reflect whether the compressed KV representation still lets the model use that content effectively.

On top of this protocol, the paper evaluates three metrics. The first is standard final-answer accuracy. The second is answer–chain consistency, which measures whether the final answer remains aligned with the visible reasoning chain. The third is perturbation faithfulness, which probes whether the compressed model responds appropriately when intermediate reasoning content is perturbed in controlled ways. The authors are careful not to claim that KV states physically decompose into separate “answer” and “evidence” components; the distinction is behavioral rather than mechanistic.

The experimental suite covers ten token-eviction compression methods and one quantization method, across three models, multiple tasks, and multiple compression budgets. Tasks include mathematical reasoning, scientific QA, clinical calculation, and long-context retrieval. The paper also reports end-to-end results to show that the observed pattern is not an artifact of the replay protocol. Relative to prior KV-compression work, the novelty is not a new compression algorithm but a stronger evaluation lens that exposes a blind spot in accuracy-only reporting.d:T778,The main result is not that one compression method dominates, but that different evaluation axes separate in meaningful ways. On reasoning-heavy tasks such as AIME, GPQA-Diamond, and MedCalc, some methods remain competitive if judged only by final-answer accuracy under certain compression budgets. However, answer–chain consistency and perturbation faithfulness degrade earlier and more sharply. This indicates that compressed models may still recover the right answer from residual answer-like cues, local summaries, or conclusion-adjacent signals, while no longer preserving the reasoning structure needed to justify or verify that answer. In that sense, accuracy is an asymmetric diagnostic: when it collapses, compression damage is obvious; when it holds up, evidence preservation is still uncertain.

A second important finding is task dependence. On retrieval-oriented tasks such as RULER QA, where correct answers depend more directly on retaining exact supporting evidence, compression damage is more visible through accuracy collapse itself, so the answer–evidence gap is less hidden. By contrast, on math, science QA, and clinical calculation, the model can often preserve the final answer while losing auditability of the chain.

The study also compares multiple token-eviction methods and one quantization baseline across different model families. The answer–evidence gap appears across methods and models rather than as a quirk of a single algorithm. The abstract does not expose all numeric tables, but the paper structure indicates that Table 2 reports end-to-end results, while the main result tables and figures show how accuracy, consistency, and faithfulness diverge across budgets, tasks, and methods. When reading, the most informative pattern to look for is ranking reversals: methods that look similar under accuracy but separate clearly once evidence-oriented metrics are included.e:T51e,Treat this as an evaluation-protocol paper, not as a new compression-method paper. After that, go straight to the main result figures and tables: first check whether accuracy and faithfulness decline together on each task, then inspect whether there is a budget range where answers remain correct but evidential support is already degraded. If you work on long-context inference, reasoning-model serving, KV compression, or the use of reasoning traces as supervision, the paper is worth a 20–30 minute read focused on the method definition and result tables. The most useful follow-up questions are: whether deployment optimizations alter the trustworthiness of interpretability signals; whether training or distillation under compressed settings could feed this answer–evidence gap back into the model; and whether future compression methods should optimize for evidence preservation rather than final-token accuracy alone.f:T561,The framework separates two objectives: compression efficiency and context preservation. Compression efficiency is measured by the ratio between the original text-token count and the visual-token count after rendering. Context preservation is approximated with complete-text OCR: the evaluator must recover the whole source text from the rendered image, rather than answer a downstream question. This choice is deliberate. Downstream QA, retrieval, or summarization may require only sparse evidence, while natural-text OCR lets the model use lexical, syntactic, and semantic priors to correct visually ambiguous characters. Complete-text recovery is much more sensitive to missing characters, ordering errors, and local visual degradation.

The paper also introduces ZeroSense, a benchmark of long contexts with low semantic coherence and low predictability. The text is synthesized to remove ordinary linguistic dependencies, making it difficult for an MLLM to fill in unreadable regions from context. The same source content can be rendered with different layouts, including Fox, Omni, and Glyph-style renderings, and across different compression ratios. The evaluation uses a fixed set of MLLMs and reports the best model-level result over the complete benchmark, reducing underestimation caused by a weak evaluator while keeping the rendering function as the main variable.10:T68f,The pipeline has three stages. First, CoT trajectory generation: for each question, a teacher LLM produces a zero-shot chain-of-thought trajectory. The key premise is that questions with different wording but similar logical structure become closer once their reasoning paths are made explicit. Second, contextualized trajectory embedding: the question and its generated reasoning trace are embedded into a dense space intended to reflect the latent reasoning manifold better than plain question embeddings. Third, isomorphic clustering and selection: clustering or geometric covering is performed in that space, and representative items near cluster centers are selected under a fixed budget.

A useful design choice is the decoupling between subset selection and score estimation. CoT-Core only constructs the subset. The selected coreset can be used with simple standard aggregation in a fully training-free setup, or combined with stronger estimators such as GP-IRT when historical logs are available. This is more flexible than IRT-style methods, which typically tie item calibration and score reconstruction together.

Compared with lexical embedding plus k-center selection, the novelty is not the sampler itself but the representation space: neighborhood structure is induced by generated reasoning traces rather than surface wording. Compared with IRT-based approaches, it avoids the cold-start dependency on historical correctness matrices. The trade-off is also clear: it is training-free, but not cost-free, because CoT trajectories must be generated for the benchmark upfront. The method assumes that this one-time cost is amortized over many future evaluation runs.11:T58c,The experimental section is organized around three questions: whether CoT-based representations are better than plain text embeddings for coreset construction, whether the selected subsets remain faithful in cold-start score estimation, and when the gains disappear.

The paper compares against two baseline families: history-dependent approaches such as IRT/GP-IRT, and training-free geometric methods based on question embeddings, including k-center-style selection. The main result is that in cold-start settings, CoT-Core consistently outperforms text-only selection, especially on benchmarks with nontrivial multi-step reasoning. The selected subsets better preserve full-benchmark score estimates and model ranking. The paper also states a boundary condition: when tasks are dominated by factual recall, template matching, or shallow judgments, the benefit of reasoning-aware pruning becomes smaller because the underlying reasoning manifold is weak to begin with.

The most informative ablations vary the use of CoT, the embedding construction, the clustering/selection strategy, and the downstream estimator. The main takeaway is that the gain comes primarily from the reasoning-aware representation rather than from a particular clustering heuristic.
