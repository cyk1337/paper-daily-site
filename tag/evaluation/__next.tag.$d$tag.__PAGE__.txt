a:T5c0,The paper introduces Constraint Saturation Evaluation (CSE). Two design choices define it. First, every constraint must be deterministically verifiable, with no LLM judge in the loop. This keeps the evaluator stable exactly where composition becomes hard. Second, the benchmark varies the number of simultaneous constraints k systematically from 1 to 12, instead of testing only a few fixed compositions. CSE contains 36 constraint types across 8 processing dimensions, ranging from local lexical constraints to document-level structure, relational reasoning, and self-referential tasks. Each verifier returns both a binary pass/fail decision and a continuous compliance score in [0,1]. The benchmark construction also includes a composability vetting stage, which is important: a constraint that implicitly locks multiple output dimensions would distort high-k behavior and confound the analysis. The paper then analyzes three levels of behavior: per-constraint pass rate as k increases, probe-level joint success, and co-failure structure. The main empirical finding is that failures are only weakly correlated, with mean phi around +0.067, so joint success is well approximated by multiplying per-constraint success rates. The authors also show that not all constraints degrade equally: constraints that require sustained maintenance during generation deteriorate much faster than local lexical constraints, while near-binary decision constraints are relatively robust.b:T443,The evaluation covers 15 models from 8 families, 36 constraint types, k from 1 to 12, and 369,753 total constraint checks. Four results stand out. First, per-constraint pass rate follows a simple multiplicative law, 72.0% × 0.922^(k−1), with held-out MAE of only 0.2 percentage points. So the degradation is highly regular rather than noisy. Second, even mild per-constraint decay leads to severe collapse in joint success: by k=9, probe-level success falls below 2%; a model with about 41% per-constraint success at k=8 achieves only 5.7% full success on all eight constraints. Third, even the strongest model drops below 50% probe-level success at only 7 constraints, and 12 of the 15 models cross that threshold at 3 constraints or fewer. Fourth, structural constraints lose baseline capability about 2.0x faster per added constraint than lexical ones, with a 95% CI of [1.9, 2.3], and the comprehension-maintenance gap correlates with degradation rate at rho = -0.584. In practical terms, the more a constraint must be actively maintained throughout generation, the earlier it breaks.c:T4be,The method is called C3. For each task instance, the authors generate multiple context perturbations that preserve the task semantics while changing the surrounding framing. They then compare the model’s output distribution under the original prompt and under the perturbed prompts. The magnitude of that distributional shift becomes the instance-level C3 score: smaller shifts indicate higher credibility. A notable strength is that C3 is not tied to one output format. The paper adapts the comparison procedure across multiple-choice, short-answer, long-form factuality, and code generation settings, using task-appropriate estimators of output-distribution change. The motivation section uses a logical-semantics framing: if a model stably supports a proposition, semantically irrelevant contextual changes should not move its induced answer distribution much. This is not a mechanistic theory of LLM internals, but it is a coherent behavioral rationale. Relative to self-consistency, C3 measures stability across prompts rather than repeated samples from one prompt. Relative to paraphrase consistency, it tries to avoid changing the question itself, reducing false inconsistency caused by altered semantics.d:T518,The evaluation spans 26 models and 6 benchmarks, with 16 models in the main analysis and an additional 10-model case study. The task mix includes reasoning, factuality, and code generation. The main empirical findings are threefold. At the instance level, smaller cross-contextual shifts are associated with higher correctness or factuality. At the model level, aggregated C3 scores produce a robustness profile that distinguishes models that are merely benchmark-competent from those that remain behaviorally stable under semantically neutral perturbations. At the method level, the signal appears robust across perturbation sources and across different estimators of distributional difference. The paper also argues that C3 remains informative when standard benchmarks are less discriminative because of saturation or contamination, since it does not rely directly on answer-key scoring. For a closer read, the key numbers to inspect are the correlation between C3 and accuracy/factuality on each task, whether the effect holds for both open and closed models, which error types show the largest shifts, and how C3 compares with self-consistency, paraphrase consistency, and calibration-style baselines. The paper decomposes future value into three settings. First, exact-query replay: if the same question returns, can the system cheaply replay the stored verified SQL? Second, cross-question retention: remove a question’s own card from the bank and test whether the remaining cards still help answer it, isolating reuse across questions rather than direct memorization. Third, held-out same-database transfer: evaluate whether memory helps new questions from the same database. To make transfer interpretable, the authors define CR, the crystallization ratio, as held-out transfer lift divided by the on-demand repair headroom on those same held-out questions. This measures how much of the gain that would otherwise require fresh repair can be recovered from memory at first attempt. In the main instantiation, execution-guided repair generates episodes, which are then stored in a database-scoped verified card bank. The paper runs controlled interventions around three questions: where future value appears, what information actually carries transfer, and which choices in collection, verification, formatting, and retrieval materially affect outcomes. This gain corresponds to 44.4% of the on-demand repair headroom on the same held-out questions. In practical terms, nearly half of the benefit that would otherwise require extra repair computation can be recovered from previously accumulated memory. The paper also reports clear value in replay and retention settings, indicating that the bank is not only memorizing exact solved questions but also carrying some reusable database-specific knowledge.
