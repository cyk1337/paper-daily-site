b:T4cd,VideoGAIA is an agentic video understanding benchmark for general AI assistants. It reframes video understanding from single-turn question answering into a multi-turn, tool-augmented interaction process: models must iteratively perceive video content, invoke external tools, gather complementary information, and integrate evidence from multiple sources to solve real-world tasks. The benchmark is built via a multi-stage human-model co-design pipeline: starting from 100K public videos, frontier models (Gemini-3.1-Pro, GPT-5.5) handle frame-level captioning, evidence-grounded task generation, rubric-based quality control, cross-modal filtering, and tool-necessity assessment. Each task is then independently reviewed by at least three human experts, with only approved samples retained. The final test set contains 271 high-quality tasks across six categories (Culture, Geography, Daily Life, History, Technology, Economics) and two difficulty levels (L1, L2). All models are evaluated under a unified ReAct-style agent loop with three standardized tools: web search (for external information retrieval), page visit (converts webpages to text), and video review (reinspects relevant video segments) to ensure fair comparison.c:T40e,The paper formalizes isolation evaluation and joint evaluation, then tests an invariance condition: a verdict for rubric r should depend only on the conversation and r, not on the rest of the rubric set. It measures violations through four controlled operations: expansion from isolation to a larger set, subsetting between nested rubric sets, reordering of the same set, and noise injection with unrelated rubrics. Metrics include rubric-level agreement, Cohen’s kappa, and sample-level exact match. The mitigation method, SARA, uses on-policy self-distillation. A student generates a joint multi-rubric report, which is parsed into per-rubric segments. A slowly updated teacher evaluates these segments under isolation context. A divergence loss aligns analysis segments that are distorted by interference, while a preservation loss maintains verdict structure and output format. The key distinction from prompting or voting is that the method trains joint-mode behavior to match the model’s own more stable isolation-mode behavior.d:T53c,The core contribution is DDBench plus a two-condition evaluation protocol. The benchmark contains 60 historical bugs mined from 13 open-source distributed systems, partitioned into three difficulty tiers, with tier-1 being the hardest and containing 31 cases. Each case is independently reproduced, sanitized for leakage, and reviewed by distributed-systems researchers, so the emphasis is on fidelity and evaluability rather than raw scale. The evaluation runs each case under two conditions: symptom-only and context-augmented. In symptom-only, the agent receives the bug symptom and codebase, similar to standard SWE-style repair. In context-augmented, it also receives curated debugging context such as logs, traces, or diagnostic notes. This design cleanly separates model-internal reasoning from externally supplied evidence. The authors use mini-swe-agent as a unified harness and evaluate 10 proprietary and open-weight models, tracking pass rate, token consumption, and steps to completion, with bootstrap tests for significance. An important nuance is that the paper does not assume more context is always better. It explicitly studies cases where faithful but causally distant observations mislead strong models, which makes the benchmark relevant not only for evaluation but also for context construction and evidence selection.e:T555,The main empirical findings are sharp. First, DDBench creates much stronger model separation than conventional coding benchmarks. On tier-1 under the symptom-only condition, the 10 evaluated models span 61 percentage points in pass rate. Among six models with publicly reported SWE-bench Verified scores, the same set spans only 7 points on SWE-bench but 58 points on DDBench tier-1. Pairwise bootstrap tests on the 31 tier-1 cases distinguish 9 of 15 top-tier model pairs at p<0.05, suggesting the separation is statistically meaningful rather than noise. Second, curated debugging context improves success and consistently reduces cost, but the gains are uneven across models. Aggregate pass rate rises from 32.6% to 50.6%, a gain of 18.1 points. Weaker models benefit mostly in success rate; for example, GLM 4.7 improves from 9.7% to 48.4%. Stronger models gain less in pass rate but much more in efficiency; Claude Opus 4.6 sees 69% lower token consumption and 41% fewer steps to completion with added context. The paper also reports ranking changes: context-augmented GPT 5.4 matches Opus 4.6’s symptom-only pass rate while using 60% fewer tokens. The case studies are also useful: some faithful context can still hurt, especially when the observation is far from the actual fault and the model overfits salient symptoms instead of tracing the causal chain.f:T5c0,The paper introduces Constraint Saturation Evaluation (CSE). Two design choices define it. First, every constraint must be deterministically verifiable, with no LLM judge in the loop. This keeps the evaluator stable exactly where composition becomes hard. Second, the benchmark varies the number of simultaneous constraints k systematically from 1 to 12, instead of testing only a few fixed compositions. CSE contains 36 constraint types across 8 processing dimensions, ranging from local lexical constraints to document-level structure, relational reasoning, and self-referential tasks. Each verifier returns both a binary pass/fail decision and a continuous compliance score in [0,1]. The benchmark construction also includes a composability vetting stage, which is important: a constraint that implicitly locks multiple output dimensions would distort high-k behavior and confound the analysis. The paper then analyzes three levels of behavior: per-constraint pass rate as k increases, probe-level joint success, and co-failure structure. The main empirical finding is that failures are only weakly correlated, with mean phi around +0.067, so joint success is well approximated by multiplying per-constraint success rates. The authors also show that not all constraints degrade equally: constraints that require sustained maintenance during generation deteriorate much faster than local lexical constraints, while near-binary decision constraints are relatively robust.10:T443,The evaluation covers 15 models from 8 families, 36 constraint types, k from 1 to 12, and 369,753 total constraint checks. Four results stand out. First, per-constraint pass rate follows a simple multiplicative law, 72.0% × 0.922^(k−1), with held-out MAE of only 0.2 percentage points. So the degradation is highly regular rather than noisy. Second, even mild per-constraint decay leads to severe collapse in joint success: by k=9, probe-level success falls below 2%; a model with about 41% per-constraint success at k=8 achieves only 5.7% full success on all eight constraints. Third, even the strongest model drops below 50% probe-level success at only 7 constraints, and 12 of the 15 models cross that threshold at 3 constraints or fewer. Fourth, structural constraints lose baseline capability about 2.0x faster per added constraint than lexical ones, with a 95% CI of [1.9, 2.3], and the comprehension-maintenance gap correlates with degradation rate at rho = -0.584. In practical terms, the more a constraint must be actively maintained throughout generation, the earlier it breaks.11:T4be,The method is called C3. For each task instance, the authors generate multiple context perturbations that preserve the task semantics while changing the surrounding framing. They then compare the model’s output distribution under the original prompt and under the perturbed prompts. The magnitude of that distributional shift becomes the instance-level C3 score: smaller shifts indicate higher credibility. A notable strength is that C3 is not tied to one output format. The paper adapts the comparison procedure across multiple-choice, short-answer, long-form factuality, and code generation settings, using task-appropriate estimators of output-distribution change. The motivation section uses a logical-semantics framing: if a model stably supports a proposition, semantically irrelevant contextual changes should not move its induced answer distribution much. This is not a mechanistic theory of LLM internals, but it is a coherent behavioral rationale. Relative to self-consistency, C3 measures stability across prompts rather than repeated samples from one prompt. Relative to paraphrase consistency, it tries to avoid changing the question itself, reducing false inconsistency caused by altered semantics.12:T518,The evaluation spans 26 models and 6 benchmarks, with 16 models in the main analysis and an additional 10-model case study. At the instance level, smaller cross-contextual shifts are associated with higher correctness or factuality. At the model level, aggregated C3 scores produce a robustness profile that distinguishes models that are merely benchmark-competent from those that remain behaviorally stable under semantically neutral perturbations. At the method level, the signal appears robust across perturbation sources and across different estimators of distributional difference. The paper also argues that C3 remains informative when standard benchmarks are less discriminative because of saturation or contamination, since it does not rely directly on answer-key scoring.
