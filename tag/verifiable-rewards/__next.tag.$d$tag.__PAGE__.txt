9:T4dd,ProbePO adds a measurement layer on top of GRPO. First, it selects a small number of reasoning-boundary pivots using a reward-free uncertainty score, so the continuation budget is spent on states where value information is likely to matter. Second, for each selected pivot, it fixes the generated prefix, samples a capped number of continuations from the current rollout policy, scores the completed answers with the verifier, and averages those rewards to estimate the expected terminal reward of that partial solution. These probe continuations are used only for measurement, not as additional policy-update trajectories. Third, it shrinks the sparse value estimates toward the GRPO group mean μ_g to reduce variance. Differences between successive prefix-value estimates define segment-level adjustments. These adjustments are centered into a zero-mean residual and added to the original GRPO advantage. As a result, the mean token-level advantage of each trajectory remains exactly the GRPO value, while different reasoning segments receive different credit. If no pivot is selected, the method recovers GRPO. Compared with dense probing methods such as VinePPO, ProbePO trades exhaustive measurement for sparse, budgeted credit refinement.a:T8c4,The core system is a multi-agent synthesis engine called Data World. A world is formalized as a four-tuple: dependency skeleton G, canonical state X, observed environment E, and recomputable phenomena I. G is a DAG whose nodes are variable clusters and whose edges encode generative relations such as monotonic, threshold, saturation, lagged, or interaction effects. This is not just a graph template; the edge annotations define how the world is generated. X is the latent canonical state, the objective world state that actually governs the phenomena of interest. E is produced by an observation map O that projects X into analyst-visible artifacts such as CSVs, spreadsheets, and logs, with imperfect observability, aggregation, missingness, and noise. I contains target phenomena that can be recomputed and checked from the generated world and, crucially, are intended to remain recoverable from E.

A key design choice is the staged multi-agent pipeline. Separate agent sessions handle world specification, state generation, file projection, task and rubric synthesis, and auditing, while exchanging read-only artifacts. This is meant to preserve consistency without letting later stages directly exploit hidden latent variables. The auditor is central: it verifies both that the intended relationships were instantiated and that the target phenomena remain recoverable from imperfect observations. That addresses two common failure modes in synthetic environments: latent mechanisms that disappear after projection, and tasks that become trivial because the signal is too directly exposed.

Given a reference policy and temperature, there is a Gibbs target under which the KL-adjusted reward is constant on the support of the target distribution. GGPO does not estimate this target policy explicitly, and it does not apply learner-to-behavior importance weights. Instead, it uses groups of responses sampled from the behavior policy for the same prompt, and measures how far the within-group KL-adjusted rewards are from satisfying that constancy condition.
The resulting objective minimizes a log-mean-exp contrast of KL-adjusted rewards inside each response group. This is different from GRPO-style relative advantage updates, and also different from AGRO, which minimizes the variance of the same adjusted reward. The distinction matters: GGPO and AGRO share the same regularized optimum under common support, but they induce different optimization geometry and different finite-group guarantees.
The paper makes two theoretical moves. First, in the on-policy population setting, the GGPO objective is exactly the KL-regularized optimality gap. That gives the loss a clean interpretation: it is not merely a heuristic group contrast, but a direct measure of suboptimality under KL regularization. Second, in the off-policy setting, the analysis does not try to recover an unbiased estimate of that population quantity. Instead, it proves a direct reward comparison: under bounded coverage, the raw finite-group loss controls the gap between the learner’s expected reward from a single sample and the behavior policy’s best-of-K reward, up to explicit terms for regularization, reference-behavior mismatch, group size, and finite-sample error. This is the central conceptual shift of the paper.
A second major component is periodic reference refresh. With a fixed reference, GGPO optimizes toward a single Gibbs target. Refreshing the reference to the current learner changes the target across rounds. The paper shows that if each round exactly fits its Gibbs target, then T refresh rounds at temperature beta are equivalent to one Gibbs update at temperature beta/T. This explains why refresh can compound improvement rather than merely refine a fixed target. For imperfect updates, the paper derives finite-data guarantees in terms of aggregate tokenwise mismatch and inner optimization error. Expect roughly 1.5 to 2.5 hours for a careful read, with most of the time spent on the theory.11:T601,The training setup is GRPO. A step samples B prompts and n responses per prompt. Rewards are binary and converted to within-prompt advantages. The gold reward checks the final answer against a reference answer. The majority-vote reward marks responses correct if their final answer matches the most frequent answer among the n samples for that prompt.

Near the sampling policy, the GRPO update is a sum of prompt gradients. If the majority answer for a prompt is correct, the MV and gold prompt gradients are identical. The entire difference between the two batch updates comes from prompts where the majority answer is wrong. The paper decomposes the MV update relative to the gold update into shrinkage and misdirection. Shrinkage is the component along the gold direction and mainly changes the effective step size. Misdirection is the orthogonal component and is the part that changes the optimization direction.

The key claim is that misdirection is controlled by cross-prompt coherence of label errors. Independent wrong labels can be frequent and still average out at realistic batch sizes. Coherent wrong labels, such as a reward that systematically prefers confident outputs, frequent outputs, or outputs favored by a learned reward model, leave a directional bias that batch averaging cannot remove. To test this directly, the authors use per-response gradient sketches, allowing multiple reward functions to be evaluated on the same model samples and compared as update directions rather than as final training curves alone.12:T5fa,The empirical study uses five models from two model families. The main measurement is directional agreement between batch updates. MV updates align with gold updates about as well as two independent gold updates align with each other. After correcting for prompt-sampling noise, the cosine between MV and gold is statistically consistent with 1. The paper also reports that, at practical batch sizes, a prompt’s own gradient accounts for less than 1% of the change in that prompt’s output, supporting the batch-level interpretation.

Controls match the proposed decomposition. Randomly flipping up to 30% of gold labels leaves the update direction essentially unchanged, because the induced errors are incoherent. In contrast, rewards that consistently favor frequent answers, confident answers, or responses preferred by a learned reward model fall short of the gold direction, because their errors share structure. Training runs show the limits of the equivalence. The local gradient match holds at examined checkpoints, but MV and gold training do not become identical. Gold training remains ahead on held-out majority accuracy, while MV mainly sharpens single-sample answers. On held-out problems whose initial majority answer is wrong, MV training turns them into correct-majority problems at roughly three times the rate expected from resampling alone.
