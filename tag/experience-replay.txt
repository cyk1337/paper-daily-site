18:T529,Echo is organized into three stages: Experience Acquisition, Knowledge Extraction, and Model Optimization. In the first stage, the system collects raw interaction streams from deployed agents, including context, model proposals, and the user’s accepted or revised final output. The second stage is the core idea. Instead of training on the full human-agent editing trajectory, and instead of treating all trial-and-error steps as supervision, Echo extracts the final user-refined outcome as a compressed, high-value knowledge target. The underlying assumption is that in outcome-oriented applications, users are accountable stakeholders, so their edits naturally inject the missing knowledge needed to reach a correct result. In the third stage, the model is optimized to align its output distribution with these refined outcomes. This is closer to outcome imitation or refined-target distillation than to standard RL. It avoids the difficulty of modeling long noisy trajectories and sidesteps sparse-reward credit assignment. In deployment, the paper instantiates this pipeline in code completion using real interaction streams from Tencent Cloud developers. The main design choice is deliberate: keep the dense signal that contributes to the final correct answer, rather than replaying the entire interaction history.19:T40d,The main evaluation is conducted in a production code-completion setting. The headline result is an increase in online code acceptance rate from 25.7% to 35.7%, an absolute gain of 10 points. That is strong evidence that learning from user-refined outcomes can push beyond a static-data baseline. The paper also highlights two additional findings. First, the gains generalize to external users, suggesting the model is not merely memorizing the editing habits of a specific internal population. Second, performance continues to improve as more experience data is added, with no obvious saturation in the reported range. That matters because it supports the claim that open-world experience can serve as a scalable source of continual learning signal. Based on the available full text, the evidence is stronger on online effectiveness and scaling trends than on fine-grained algorithmic ablations. In other words, the paper shows that the loop works, but it is less precise about which design decisions are most responsible for the gains.1a:T530,The work studies replay buffers in realistic asynchronous LLM RL pipelines.

1) Asynchronous setup: GPUs are split into inference workers (generate rollouts) and trainers (backprop updates). Workers and trainers may hold slightly different (stale) weights, so some off-policiness exists inherently.
2) Replay buffer mechanism: workers write rollouts into a buffer (often FIFO keeping the last N samples). Trainers sample batches from the buffer; sampling does not remove items, enabling multiple reuses (replay ratio > 1). Sampling can be uniform or based on recency, reward, gradient norms, or reuse count; eviction/decay rules can be applied.
3) Theory (core): formalizes compute-optimal design via a bias–variance view of SGD that trades off:
- staleness-induced bias/variance (more replay → more off-policy),
- reduced sample diversity (too small/overused buffer),
- high rollout generation cost.
Derives qualitative/quantitative guidance for optimal buffer size and replay ratio: as inference cost increases relative to training, the compute-optimal strategy shifts toward more replay.
4) Empirical design space: systematically varies buffer hyperparameters and worker/trainer ratios (W/T), and explores smarter sampling (e.g., prioritizing positive trajectories) and alternative losses to extend stable replay regimes.1b:T581,must_read (deep technical + guide)
