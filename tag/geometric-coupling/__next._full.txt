19:T6cd,The work has three layers. First is the theoretical analysis. Starting from a standard sparse top-k MoE layer, the authors derive that for a token routed to expert i, the router weight vector associated with expert i and the expert weights processing that token receive gradients along the same input hidden-state direction, differing mainly by scalar coefficients. This is stronger than the generic statement that both modules are in the same computation graph. It means matched router–expert pairs accumulate updates from the same routed-token history and therefore evolve as coupled geometric directions. Second is empirical validation. In a 1B-scale SMoE trained from scratch, the paper compares router scores with the internal activations of experts on the same token and finds that experts ranked higher by the router also respond more strongly internally. So routing is not merely an external assignment layer; it is mirrored in expert computation. Third is a reinterpretation of the auxiliary load-balancing loss. Because this loss sends gradients to every router direction on every token, not only to selected experts, it mixes expert-specific directions and weakens the natural coupling induced by routed-token history. The paper supports this both theoretically and empirically. Motivated by that view, the authors introduce a parameter-free centroid router: each expert maintains an online K-Means or EMA centroid of the hidden states assigned to it, and routing is done by cosine similarity to these centroids, with sign-updated biases for load control. The point is not to beat learned routers outright, but to test whether a large part of routing can be explained as geometric summarization of expert-specific token regions.1a:T4b1,The experiments address three questions. First, does geometric coupling appear in practice? On a roughly 1B-parameter SMoE trained from scratch on about 50B tokens, the paper shows that experts receiving higher router scores also exhibit stronger internal activations for the same token, which supports the coupling hypothesis. Second, how does auxiliary balancing alter geometry? The authors train two otherwise matched 1B SMoEs differing only in the balancing rule and measure similarity among router directions. With the auxiliary loss, distinct router vectors become nearly three times more similar, indicating stronger directional homogenization. Load balance improves, but specialization weakens. Third, can a centroid router recover much of the learned router’s function? The answer is largely yes: the centroid router achieves the lowest load imbalance among the tested variants while keeping perplexity close to the learned router trained without the auxiliary loss, with only a modest degradation. The paper is careful not to conflate better utilization with better language modeling; the empirical emphasis is on explaining routing behavior rather than claiming absolute perplexity gains.1b:T46d,This one deserves a careful read. Start with the theory section and work through the gradient derivation; the key idea is why router and expert updates align along the same token direction. Without that, the later geometric claims can sound more intuitive than they really are. Then read the plots comparing router scores and expert activations, since they turn the theory into an observable training phenomenon. After that, focus on the analysis of the auxiliary load-balancing loss and the router-direction similarity statistics; this is the part with the clearest practical consequence for MoE training. Finish with the centroid-router section, especially the online K-Means or EMA update rule, cosine routing, and bias adjustment. The paper is best read as a mechanism paper, not as a benchmark paper.
