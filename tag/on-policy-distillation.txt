18:T66b,Ask-to-Distill (A2D) treats a prompt as a distribution elicitor: it determines which prefixes the student visits, hence where teacher token distributions become available for OPD.

Two-stage pipeline:
1) Train a prompt synthesizer before OPD.
- Initialize the synthesizer from the student.
- Optimize it with RL while keeping the scoring student and the teacher frozen.
- Crucially, the method avoids an expensive inner loop: it does not update the student per candidate prompt to estimate value.

2) A no-answer, distributional surrogate reward for prompt value.
- The reward aggregates token-level signals along student rollouts.
- Instead of rewarding pure disagreement (which can be dominated by support-mismatch), it uses a support-aware notion of teachability: combine teacher–student discrepancy with the teacher’s probability mass on the student’s candidate tokens, aiming to capture “corrective preference within student support” rather than “divergence outside support”.

Anti-gaming terms:
- Length control via sublinear length normalization plus a soft response-budget penalty to prevent inflating reward by longer outputs.
- A batch-level diversity term to prevent mode collapse into a narrow prompt family.

Curation:
- After training, prompts are format-filtered and lexically/semantically deduplicated; a frozen Qwen3-Embedding-4B is used only for semantic deduplication, not for reward computation.

Compared to synthetic SFT data, A2D does not need reference answers; compared to task-generation via success rewards, it optimizes the usability of teacher distributional supervision rather than task correctness.19:T509,The method starts from the sampled-token log-ratio r_t = log pi_student(y_t | c_t) - log pi_teacher(y_t | c_t). In token-level OPD, positive r_t means the student assigns higher probability than the teacher to the sampled token, and the local advantage A_t = -stop_gradient(r_t) pushes that token down. The paper notes that positive log-ratios dominate the training signal: in the analyzed vanilla OPD rollouts, tokens with r_t > 0 account for 83.5% of the total absolute advantage mass. To reduce token-level noise, responses are divided into contiguous blocks. Each block is summarized by the mean positive log-ratio D_i. A penalty jump is an abrupt increase in D_i between adjacent blocks. The diagnostic analysis finds that such jumps persist on held-out tokens and coincide with lower teacher confidence, measured by higher entropy and lower top-1 probability. AP-OPD responds by scaling down all signed supervision coefficients in the block after a detected jump. The preceding block serves as a local reference, and the scale is chosen so that the weighted average penalty of the post-jump block matches the previous block’s original mean. The rule preserves signs and relative token weights, introduces no extra strength hyperparameter, and requires no additional forward passes.1a:T7c7,The authors build a unified comparison framework, OPDArena. They train five released Qwen Base models under the same mathematical post-training setup. The supervision signals are: SFT on fixed reference solutions; RLVR with GRPO and verifiable correctness rewards; and OPD, where the student rolls out responses on-policy and receives reverse-KL-style token supervision from a teacher on student-generated prefixes. A key design choice is that the paper separates where supervision comes from from how the student distribution moves.

The OPD setting is further split into several teacher types: larger frozen base teachers, Self-OPD(SFT), Self-OPD(RLVR), and OPSD with privileged information. The self-distillation variants are especially useful because teacher and student share the same base initialization; the main difference is whether the teacher’s preferences were induced by SFT or RLVR. That makes the effect of teacher preference type much cleaner to study.

The analysis goes beyond accuracy. Learning and forgetting are measured as accuracy changes relative to the base model. Distribution change is measured by Jensen–Shannon divergence between next-token predictive distributions on fixed prefixes. The paper then introduces an update-selectivity view through the ratio ρ between OOD change and in-domain change. If a method concentrates its changes inside the target domain while causing limited OOD drift, it is more likely to improve the target task without erasing prior capabilities.

A useful mechanism-level point is that teacher information is not determined by teacher quality alone. It also depends on how well teacher-preferred responses align with the student’s current distribution. Even if the teacher encodes better preferences, the student may inherit them slowly if they lie in low-probability regions under the student rollout policy. That can produce the undesirable regime where task gains transfer slowly while OOD perturbation already accumulates.1b:T748,The experiments cover five Qwen Base students and four OPD teacher sources, all under a shared math post-training setup, with joint evaluation of target-domain learning, general capability retention, and predictive distribution change. The headline pattern is consistent throughout the paper: RLVR and Self-OPD(RLVR) combine math gains with strong retention; OPSD stays closer to the base model and changes little in both domains; OPD from larger teachers and Self-OPD(SFT) show more severe forgetting; and SFT has the largest average capability loss.

The most important empirical result is the correlation analysis. The paper compares how in-domain JS change and OOD JS change relate to forgetting, and finds that OOD change has a more stable positive association with forgetting across student models and teacher configurations. This is more informative than the generic statement that “larger distribution shift causes more forgetting,” because it shows that the harmful part is not necessary reshaping inside the target domain, but spillover into OOD regions.

Controlled teacher comparisons show that when teachers are obtained from the same base via SFT or RLVR, their learned preferences make student updates more concentrated in-domain, improving update selectivity. But the final benefit still depends on the match between teacher-preferred responses and the student rollout distribution. The implementation also uses a student-weighted Top-16 approximation for OPD token advantages, so the study is tied to a concrete training recipe rather than a purely conceptual setup. The provided full-text excerpt does not include the complete result tables, so it is not safe to quote exact benchmark-by-benchmark numbers here; still, the paper presents the findings as repeated across multiple bases and teacher settings rather than a single-model artifact.1c:T491,For a quick read, start with Figure 1 to place the methods on the target-gain versus retention plane. Then read the unified formulation of the three training signals, especially the fact that OPD applies reverse KL on student-generated prefixes. That is the foundation for the comparison. 但直接广播原始序列分数在实践里会塌。作者指出有两个原因。其一，越靠后的 token 越处在 student 自己生成出来的偏移轨迹上，teacher feedback 可靠性下降。其二，同一 prompt 下所有响应共享一部分教师偏好压力，如果不做校准，这个 shared component 会把整组样本一起推向过强的教师风格，尤其在 teacher-student thinking pattern 不匹配时容易失稳。

从机制上看，γ 控制 credit 的时间传播长度，λ 控制教师公共压力进入更新的强度。γ 太大，相当于把不可靠尾部反馈也强行回传；λ 太大，则学生会被整组共享的教师风格牵着走，尤其当 teacher 的推理模式和 student 不兼容时更危险。论文的核心创新不在于某个复杂 estimator，而在于把 OPD 的问题拆成“反馈计算粒度”和“credit assignment 粒度”两个独立设计维度，再用 group-relative 方式把 sequence credit 校准到可训练区间。1f:Ta49,The method has two layers: a theoretical reformulation and a practical calibration scheme.

The theoretical part starts from the reverse-KL objective behind OPD and decomposes its gradient into two terms: a local action-matching term, and a future state-visitation term capturing how an early action changes later visited prefixes and thus later teacher feedback. Standard sampled-token OPD keeps only the local term. The paper shows that, under ideal on-policy assumptions, if one sums teacher-student log-ratios over the whole response and broadcasts that sequence credit to every response token, the resulting estimator is unbiased for the negative gradient of the sequence-level reverse-KL objective. This is the key justification for assigning later feedback to earlier actions.

In practice, however, broadcasting raw sequence scores is unstable. The paper identifies two reasons. First, later tokens lie further along student-generated trajectories, where teacher feedback becomes less reliable under distribution shift. Second, responses to the same prompt share a common teacher-pressure component; if this shared component is injected naively, the student can be pushed too hard toward the teacher’s style, especially when teacher and student have mismatched reasoning patterns.

GR-OPD therefore adds two calibrations. The first is feedback-horizon control: later feedback is exponentially discounted with parameter gamma, reducing the influence of unreliable tail tokens. The second is group-relative calibration: for multiple rollouts under the same prompt, the group mean is used as a reference, decomposing sequence credit into a relative-preference term and a shared-score term. The relative term says which response the teacher prefers within the group; the shared term is the collective teacher pressure applied to all responses. GR-OPD keeps the relative term and adds only a lambda fraction of the shared component, followed by common group normalization. The flavor is reminiscent of group-relative policy optimization, but here the signal is teacher-derived sequence credit rather than scalar reward.

Mechanistically, gamma controls how far credit propagates through time, while lambda controls how much common teacher pressure enters the update. Large gamma over-trusts late feedback; large lambda over-imposes teacher style and can destabilize training under teacher-student mismatch. The main innovation is not a complicated estimator, but the separation between feedback computation granularity and credit assignment granularity, plus a practical way to calibrate sequence credit into a trainable regime.20:T60d,The experiments cover five Qwen3 teacher-student settings and six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, using macro Avg@K as the main metric.
