19:T69d,The setup follows standard OPD. For a prompt x, the student samples an on-policy trajectory y. At each context h_t=(x,y_<t), the teacher provides token-level supervision. The ideal objective is a reverse KL between student and teacher policies. In practice, the paper uses the sampled-token k1 approximation, leading to a policy-gradient style OPD objective: a sampled token is reinforced when the teacher assigns it higher probability than the student, and suppressed otherwise.

The contribution is an experimental decomposition rather than a new distillation algorithm. RQ1 keeps the domain fixed to math and varies training difficulty, evaluation language, and reasoning horizon. RQ2 examines cross-domain transfer between math, code, and science, while distinguishing same-origin teacher-student pairs from cross-origin pairs. Same-origin means the teacher and student come from the same base-model lineage, for example an SFT checkpoint and an RL-trained teacher derived from it. Cross-origin means they come from different bases. RQ3 studies multi-teacher OPD, where prompts are routed to domain-specific teachers, and tests whether routing isolates teacher effects.

The key mechanism-level result is that OPD does not require the teacher to solve the training problem. Even prompts on which the teacher never succeeds can still be useful, because the supervision is applied to the student’s visited states and encodes local teacher preferences over continuations. Same-origin teachers transfer more globally across languages, horizons, and domains. Cross-origin teachers tend to improve the trained distribution more narrowly and may transfer less than a weaker same-origin teacher.1a:T460,The provided content does not include the full numeric tables, but it lists a diverse set of teacher models, including Qwen3-32B, Light-R1-14B, Polaris-7B/4B, OpenMath-Nemotron-1.5B/7B, and JustRL-DeepSeek-1.5B.

The reported findings are consistent across the controlled settings. First, in-domain math transfer is largely insensitive to training-problem difficulty. Bucketing problems by teacher or student pass rate does not materially change the usefulness of OPD, and unsolved problems remain useful. Second, English short-horizon math OPD improves evaluation on Chinese math and longer-horizon composed math problems, indicating that the transfer is not just surface-distribution matching. Third, cross-domain transfer exists in both directions, but the origin relationship dominates: same-origin teachers move the student closer to the teacher across domains, while cross-origin teachers mostly help the trained distribution. Fourth, in multi-teacher OPD, domain routing does not isolate influence. Mixture choices create a capability seesaw because each teacher affects other domains as well as its assigned one.1b:Ta81,The method has three main components. First, alignment is performed in shared text space rather than token-id space. The student generates a response under its own tokenizer and chat template, then decodes it into surface text. The teacher is not fed student tokens directly; instead, the teacher reconstructs its own context using its own chat template, appends the exact same student response text, and re-tokenizes the full sequence with its native tokenizer. Distillation is then applied only at positions where a student token and a teacher token correspond to exactly the same text span. This avoids inventing artificial token correspondences across incompatible vocabularies and also sidesteps differences in chat formatting. The trade-off is that not every token can be aligned, but the paper argues that sparse high-confidence alignment is preferable to dense but noisy alignment.

Second, the paper addresses the central failure mode in long-teacher to short-student OPD: uncontrolled response-length growth. In naive OPD, the teacher evaluates each student-generated step. If the teacher itself prefers much longer chains of thought, the student is repeatedly encouraged to continue generating, which causes the thinking segment and final answer to grow until truncation becomes common. To counter this, the authors add a student reference KL loss that keeps the current student policy close to its initial policy. The role of this term is not generic regularization; it specifically limits excessive policy drift toward a trajectory distribution the student cannot support within its context budget. The paper explicitly notes that larger teacher-student gaps typically require stronger KL regularization.

Third, the authors mask advantages on special termination tokens. Their observation is that direct teacher supervision often discourages the student from emitting tokens such as </think> or <|im_end|>, because the teacher would prefer to continue reasoning. If this signal is passed through unchanged, the student learns to delay termination and training quickly becomes pathological. By masking the advantages on these special tokens, the method prevents the teacher from directly penalizing termination behavior. It is a small intervention, but it targets a very specific instability in long-form reasoning distillation.

Overall, SimpleOPD is not a new OPD framework so much as a compact recipe that combines cross-tokenizer alignment, length control, and termination stabilization. The paper also emphasizes that the gains are obtained without supervised warm-starting on teacher trajectories; the transfer is achieved primarily through the on-policy process itself.1c:T6f7,The experiments distill from the long-context reasoning model SU-01 into multiple shorter-context students from different families, including Qwen3, Qwen3.5, Intern-S2-Preview, GLM-4.7, and Gemma-4. The headline result is on ProofBench: Intern-S2-Preview improves from 34.0 to 55.2, a gain of 21.2 points, surpassing Gemini-2.5-Pro and approaching substantially stronger reasoning models. The paper highlights that the strongest gains appear on natural-language mathematical proof tasks, which suggests the transfer is not merely about answer formatting but about longer-horizon proof organization.

The distilled students also improve on science-oriented benchmarks such as HLE and HiPhO, despite being trained only on mathematical reasoning data. That indicates some degree of out-of-domain generalization in the transferred reasoning behavior. The ablations are especially informative. Removing the reference KL causes response lengths to grow rapidly, truncation rates to rise, and optimization to become unstable. Removing termination-token masking makes the model much less willing to end its thinking or final response. Increasing the distillation length can further help long-form reasoning, but only when the resulting trajectories still fit within the student’s context budget. The paper also reports that larger teacher-student gaps generally require stronger KL coefficients; otherwise the student is pulled toward a long-trajectory distribution it cannot sustain.

The most useful evidence is not just the benchmark gains but the training-dynamics analysis: response-length distributions, truncation behavior, and the role of termination tokens.
