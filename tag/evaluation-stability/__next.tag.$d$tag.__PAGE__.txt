9:T7f7,The experimental design is careful in one important way: candidate outputs are held fixed, and only the post-decision interaction with the judge is varied. The authors start from response pairs drawn from MT-Bench and AlpacaEval, obtain a baseline judgment, and then apply several follow-up conditions: repeated evaluation, neutral reconsideration, and targeted persuasive challenge. This lets them attribute reversals to interaction rather than to changes in the evaluated content.

The central protocol is the anti-baseline challenge, where the follow-up conversation explicitly argues for the side that lost under the baseline judgment. To separate generic reconsideration effects from actual persuasion, the paper also introduces a counterbalanced target audit. This decomposition matters. The results show substantial persuasion-induced reversibility, but little net target-directed steering beyond neutral reconsideration. In other words, the judge becomes easier to move after challenge, but not in a cleanly controllable way.

The mechanism analysis is stronger than a simple reversal-rate paper. The authors examine self-reported confidence and find a calibration failure: judges often express high confidence even on decisions that are easy to overturn later. They compare challenge framings and show that authority framing is more effective than reasoning-heavy argumentation. That is a notable result because it suggests the vulnerability is not mainly about better evidence, but about social or procedural cues. They also analyze justification overlap before and after reversals. When the decision changes, the new rationale often has low overlap with the original one, which is more consistent with post-hoc rationalization than with explicit diagnosis of an earlier reasoning error.

To quantify the phenomenon, they propose the Evaluation Robustness Score (ERS), which treats the evaluator itself as the object being measured. ERS is meant to capture susceptibility to conversational manipulation rather than task accuracy.a:T681,The main experiments use pairwise judgments from MT-Bench and AlpacaEval. Under repeated evaluation and neutral re-prompting, decisions are largely stable. Under anti-baseline challenge, however, 49% of baseline decisions reverse. That is the headline result and it directly supports the paper’s main distinction: stability does not imply robustness.

The counterbalanced audit reports persuasion-induced reversibility of P_S = 0.194, indicating a real challenge effect beyond ordinary reevaluation. At the same time, the signed target-direction effect is D_S^signed = -0.018, with clipped D_S = 0, which means the challenge does not reliably steer the judge toward a chosen target beyond the general destabilization caused by reconsideration. The judge is persuadable, but not precisely controllable.

Quality of reversals is another important result. Among reversals triggered by targeted post-decision challenge, 64% are harmful, meaning they move the judgment away from human preference rather than toward it. The paper also shows that these reversals can affect benchmark rankings, so the issue is not merely local inconsistency.

The mechanism studies add useful detail. Authority-based framing outperforms reasoning-based challenge. Reversals can occur even when confidence drops, which weakens the interpretation that the judge is simply becoming more careful. Low overlap between pre- and post-reversal justifications supports the rationalization story.
