9:T813,The central method is TokenSwap. Starting from a text-only sample, the pipeline identifies visualizable concepts that matter for the task, replaces them with semantically aligned natural images, and keeps the surrounding text unchanged. This is different from rendering the whole prompt as an image or building a conventional image-question pair. The replacement happens at the concept or phrase level, so the text-only and multimodal versions remain structurally close. That gives a cleaner controlled test: the main change is that some semantic units move from text tokens to visual tokens.

TokenSwap-Bench is built on top of MMLU. The construction has three main stages. First, Gemini-2.0-Flash is used to identify concepts in questions and answer options that can be visually grounded. Second, each concept is turned into an image with a text-to-image model. Third, the benchmark applies several filters to ensure that measured gaps are not artifacts of poor substitutions. The important filters are:
1. Faithfulness verification: the generated image must correctly represent the intended concept in context.
2. Task relevance verification: if removing the replaced concepts from the original text does not affect prediction, the sample is not useful for measuring a meaningful gap and is filtered out.
3. Semantic recoverability: the substituted image is captioned and the caption is inserted back into text form to check whether the original semantics can be recovered.

The training-side intervention is data-centric rather than architectural. The authors augment training with image-interleaved counterparts of text data, and they test this both in pre-training and post-training settings. The intuition is simple and plausible: repeated exposure to semantically matched text and image realizations should force the model to learn more stable cross-modal representations and more consistent reasoning under token-type substitution. Compared with generic image-text alignment losses or caption-style supervision, this directly targets substitution invariance.a:T6bc,There are four main empirical takeaways.

First, the modality gap is pervasive and large. Across 42 MLLMs, replacing text spans with aligned images causes performance drops ranging from 4.2% to 47.4%, with an average decrease of 19.6% and a reported 95% confidence interval of ±3.3%. This is not a small robustness issue; it is a systematic reliability problem.

Second, reasoning-oriented models show much smaller gaps. The paper reports an average gap of about 10.1% for reasoning models versus 25.5% for non-reasoning models. That suggests the difference is likely due to training-time mechanisms rather than prompting alone, such as stronger process supervision, more multimodal reasoning data, or post-training recipes that implicitly enforce consistency.

Third, common prompting strategies do not reliably solve the problem. Chain-of-thought and few-shot prompting provide limited benefit. This matters because it shifts the diagnosis away from inference-time reasoning and toward representation learning and training distribution.

Fourth, scaling compute helps only marginally. A 10× increase in training FLOPs reduces the gap by only about 2.8%. So this is not something current scaling trends fix automatically.

On the mitigation side, TokenSwap augmentation reduces the modality gap in both pre-training and post-training while preserving text-only and standard vision-language performance, with slight gains in some cases. They find that image and text embeddings are not unrelated distributions: their covariance spectra have similar long-tail decay, and their principal subspaces overlap much more than random baselines. This suggests that contrastive pretraining has already built a shared dominant semantic backbone. The residual modality gap is not explained by a global centroid shift. After moving text embeddings to the image centroid, most of the discrepancy remains. Spectral analysis further shows that the residual is anisotropic, concentrated along a small number of dominant directions rather than spread as isotropic noise. AnisoAlign follows from this diagnosis. It decomposes the shared space into a dominant subspace and its orthogonal complement, then uses a blockwise polar parameterization inside the dominant subspace to model radius and phase structure. Training is staged: first, a periodic phase prior is learned from target-modality samples only; second, source-modality embeddings are corrected through bounded residual updates so that they move toward target-modality support while preserving instance-level semantics. This differs from simple mean shifting and from unconstrained cross-modal mapping, both of which can either under-correct the gap or damage semantic neighborhoods.d:T653,The main contribution is the benchmark design, CrossMath, rather than a new model architecture. The task is a 2D grid of intersecting mathematical equations with missing values, and the model must infer the missing numbers in sequence. This task is chosen because it naturally requires spatial layout understanding, local constraint propagation, and multi-step deduction, making it more suitable for probing visual reasoning than standard OCR-heavy or text-biased multimodal tasks.

CrossMath is built around three design principles. First, the task is vision-first: optimal performance should depend on reasoning over spatial structure rather than exploiting language priors. Second, difficulty is stratified through procedural generation by varying grid size, number of missing equations, and operator complexity, so the benchmark can separate models across scales and avoid ceiling or floor effects. Third, and most importantly, each puzzle is instantiated in three strictly equivalent formats: image-only, text-only as a markdown table, and image+text. The evaluation set is manually verified for cross-modal information equivalence. This is the key methodological move, because it allows performance differences to be attributed to modality-specific reasoning rather than missing information.

The paper also includes a targeted intervention: Qwen3.5-9B is post-trained on image-only CrossMath data using SFT and GRPO. This is not presented as a new RL method; its role is to test whether the observed modality gap is trainable away and whether improving visual reasoning harms the model’s existing capabilities.e:T64f,The main empirical result is clear and somewhat uncomfortable for current VLMs: across strong models, text-only performs best, image+text is worse, and image-only is worst. In other words, adding visual input does not reliably help and often hurts. This directly supports the paper’s central claim that current VLMs lean heavily on textual reasoning rather than robust visual reasoning.

The benchmark itself contains three difficulty levels, with 5,000 training samples and 250 evaluation samples. The evaluation split is manually checked to ensure that the three modality versions are information-equivalent. The dataset is not large by pretraining standards, but that is not the point; the value lies in control and interpretability.

The post-training results are also important. SFT and GRPO on image-only CrossMath data significantly improve visual reasoning performance and reduce the gap across modalities. The paper further reports out-of-distribution evaluations showing that this targeted post-training preserves the model’s original capabilities and transfers to external vision-based math tasks. That suggests the modality gap is not purely an architectural ceiling; at least part of it comes from training objectives and data distributions that undertrain visual reasoning.

The most useful analyses to inspect are likely difficulty-wise breakdowns of the gap, why image+text degrades relative to text-only, and the relative contributions of SFT versus GRPO.
