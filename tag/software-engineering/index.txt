18:T433,SWE-Shift introduces a matched-continuation benchmark design:
- The agent runs normally on a repo task until a scheduled checkpoint; the runner saves a fully restorable execution state (agent-visible history, workspace, controller state, tool availability, system settings, counters).
- From the same saved prefix, evaluation forks into:
  (i) Clean continuation: no change, continue execution; measures whether the agent could succeed from that intermediate state.
  (ii) Shifted continuation: inject a task-relevant update, then continue; measures robustness to the change.
- Changes are curated to be relevant (ignoring them can invalidate an otherwise successful continuation) and recoverable (a valid completion remains possible via agent-accessible tools).
- The benchmark spans 12 change families and introduces each change at 9 different intervention positions per scenario.
- Success criteria are defined relative to post-update requirements, including preserving still-valid work/effects and handling stale or misbound evidence, not just passing the original tests.19:T60f,The stage trains at 256K context for hundreds of billions of tokens on top of converged Nemotron base models. The mixture is dominated by natural software-engineering data: packed repositories, merged pull requests, commit histories, and build logs. Synthetic agent traces are kept to a bounded 10–15% share and rendered in the Nemotron chat template. Tool responses are masked from the loss, because environment outputs should condition the model but should not be imitated. Natural engineering data is kept in its original format rather than converted into chat, which preserves repository and log distributions. The data pipeline applies permissive-license filtering, decontamination against SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench, secret redaction, and loss masking for spans such as repeated delimiters, hashes, and timestamps. A large pull-request corpus is built from public GitHub repositories with at least ten stars in supported languages. Each example can include repository metadata, date, file tree, code context, PR description, linked issues, review discussion, and patch evolution. The filtering removes unavailable repositories, missing Git objects, failed patch reconstruction, empty or oversized changes, generated content, and other low-quality patches. The resulting corpus contains 12.1 million PRs and between 200B and 2T tokens depending on the amount of repository context included. Issue-linked examples are additionally checked by a structured LLM judge for issue-patch consistency, while PR-only examples use deterministic filters.1a:T41e,The recipe is developed on Nemotron 3.5 Lightning-30B-A3B and scaled to Nemotron 3.5 Super-120B-A12B. On Lightning, 500B midtraining tokens followed by the same full SFT improve SWE-bench Verified by 3.8 points, SWE-bench Multilingual by 7.0 points, and SWE-bench Pro by 3.5 points over the no-midtraining control. The multilingual gains are reported as the most sustained. On Super, 280B midtraining tokens followed by identical full SFT improve DeepSWE from 11.7 to 15.7 and SWE-bench Verified from 72.9 to 74.3. The harness robustness result is also important: after 65B tokens and short SFT on Super, the model improves under every tested harness, with average gains of 8.6 points on SWE-bench Verified and 6.8 points on Terminal-Bench. The ablations examine the value of a small agent-trace share, learning rate, pretraining-data replay, tool-response masking, and effects on long-context ability. The key empirical pattern is that a small amount of agentic traces helps on top of natural data, but the stage should not become a long SFT substitute.1b:T623,The contribution is a benchmark design, not a new model. Each task simulates a customer engagement. The agent receives a sponsor instruction, a copy of the customer’s production system, documents and historical data, a staff directory, and a constrained runtime with Python and SQLite. General internet access is blocked. After the agent finishes, the contents of system/ are deployed without modification, and production still calls the original entry points. This forces real integration into an existing codebase rather than a side demo. Scoring is based on improvement in the customer’s business metric on held-out production data, minus stakeholder-contact cost. Three design choices matter. First, the anchors are explicit: the unchanged system scores 0, the expert reference solution scores 1, and literal compliance with the sponsor request is capped around 0.2. This sharply distinguishes “doing what was asked” from “solving the customer’s problem.” Second, the four instruction levels are a clean intervention. L0 fully specifies the problem; L1, L2, and L3 progressively remove constraints and true objectives until L3 is just the original sponsor request. Because the environment, stakeholders, and grader are fixed, score differences across levels isolate the cost of missing information and failed problem discovery. Third, stakeholder interaction is priced, but the agent is not told upfront which contacts are useful or wasteful. That means the benchmark evaluates information-gathering strategy, not just willingness to ask more questions.1c:T6c9,The benchmark contains 95 tasks from 20 customer scenarios spanning 18 sectors, adapted from real delivery projects by domain experts. The paper evaluates 13 model configurations from 7 providers. On L3, the setting closest to real sponsor requests, the best configuration is Claude Opus 5.5 with a mean score of 0.56 and only 14 of 95 tasks resolved. Five of the thirteen configurations resolve none. Even an oracle that picks the best model configuration per task reaches only 0.70 overall and resolves 30 tasks, still well below the expert reference. The most informative result is the bottleneck analysis. When relevant rules and constraints are provided, the top three models score 0.73–0.82. When they must discover those constraints themselves, scores drop to 0.39–0.62. That points to unstated-constraint discovery, not implementation, as the main failure mode. Another useful observation is that on the original tasks, L3 is at most 0.08 below L1, suggesting the sponsor request often contains partial clues; the hard part is turning scattered evidence into the right intervention. The paper also shows that leading models can obtain 72%–85% of key facts from stakeholders at L3, yet their delivered systems achieve only 0.60–0.70 of the reference improvement.
