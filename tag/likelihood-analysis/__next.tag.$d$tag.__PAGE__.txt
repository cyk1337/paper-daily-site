9:T5de,The paper combines an empirical diagnostic with a compact theoretical result. Empirically, it fixes the model and target sequence in LLaDA-2.1-mini, varies only the reveal order, and evaluates the pathwise conditional probabilities q_t(π)=pθ(x*_{π(t)}|R_{t-1},c). If these conditionals came from a coherent joint distribution, the chain rule would make the sum of log q_t invariant to order. The paper shows that this invariance fails in practice.

The theoretical contribution is the uniform-spreading theorem. The authors frame target recovery as a sequence of local argmax decisions. At each step, success depends not only on the target probability q_t itself, but on whether it wins against local competitors. Holding the total budget P=∏t q_t fixed, recoverability is uniquely maximized when confidence is spread uniformly across steps, q_t=P^(1/n). The structural penalty for uneven allocation is captured by Var(log q_t). This yields two complementary diagnostics: logP measures total confidence budget, while Var(log q_t) measures how efficiently a reveal order distributes that budget.

The paper then analyzes several decoding strategies: Forced-AR, Random, and confidence-first variants such as Max-prob and Top-margin. A notable empirical finding is that confidence-first decoding, although order-agnostic in principle, follows reveal paths that are close to left-to-right on content tokens. Its main deviation appears in short-answer settings, where it can trigger a tail-EOS cascade.a:T5d7,The main experiments use LLaDA-2.1-mini on fixed target continuations from C4. The authors sample 1,000 random C4 examples, split each into a prefix and target continuation, and compare path scores under different reveal orders. The headline result is that changing only the order can shift logP/n by as much as 0.49 nats per token. That is far beyond numerical noise and is strong evidence that the learned conditionals are not exact factorizations of one coherent joint distribution.

For generation behavior, the paper compares one-token-per-step strategies. Structured strategies—Forced-AR, Max-prob, and Top-margin—show similar external perplexity, but substantially lower Var(log q_t) than Random. Reveal-order analysis explains why: Max-prob and Top-margin traverse content-token paths that are very close to strict left-to-right decoding, so their behavior is much less exotic than the order-agnostic label suggests. The main difference comes from short-answer prompts, where confidence-first rules can exploit a tail-EOS cascade.

Downstream validation covers HellaSwag, CMath, MMLU-Pro, and IFEval. The point is not headline benchmark gains, but the relationship between correctness and the variance axis. Lower Var(log q_t) is consistently associated with correct generations, and the pattern is not reducible to Random simply being weak overall. That supports the claim that confidence-trace smoothness carries information about decoding reliability beyond total path likelihood.b:T4b7,For a skim, focus on three parts. First, the early introduction, which clearly defines OALMs, dLLMs, reveal order, and pathwise scoring. Second, §4.2, which contains the most important empirical result: logP/n for the same target can drift by about 0.5 nats/token across reveal orders. Third, Theorem 3.1 and its interpretation. You do not need every derivation detail, but you should understand why greedy decoding is vulnerable to a single low-confidence bottleneck, which makes Var(log q_t) a meaningful diagnostic.

If you have about 20 minutes, read in this order: introduction, the statement and intuition of Theorem 3.1, the fixed-sequence scoring experiment in §4.2, the reveal-order analysis in §4.3, and the benchmark correlation results in §4.4. One subtle point to keep in mind is that the paper does not claim low variance alone guarantees better task performance. The claim is narrower and more useful: variance is a complementary signal that captures decoding reliability better than total logP alone.
