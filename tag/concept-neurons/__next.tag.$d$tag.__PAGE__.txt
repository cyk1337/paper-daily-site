9:T791,The core idea is to identify cross-modal concept neurons. The authors adopt the key-value memory view of Transformer FFNs and treat individual FFN neurons as fine-grained units that can encode concept-selective directions. For a given label, such as angry, they run one forward pass with an audio example plus a question prompt, and another with a text description corresponding to the expected answer, such as “The speaker feels angry.” They then identify neurons that are strongly associated with that label on the audio side and on the text side, and measure their overlap.

If a shared subspace exists, matching audio-text label pairs should overlap in these label-relevant neurons more than mismatching pairs. This is the central departure from prior work that compares full hidden vectors using cosine similarity. The authors show that ambient-space similarity is dominated by dimensions unrelated to the label and therefore has weak discriminative value for concept alignment.

To move beyond correlation, they perform two causal interventions. First, they use steering: they shift activations along directions derived from text-side cross-modal neurons and test whether audio-based recognition moves toward the target label. Second, they deactivate the identified neurons by zeroing them out and check whether the target task degrades before unrelated tasks do. This makes the claim much stronger: text-defined internal directions can causally alter audio recognition, and the shared neurons are functionally necessary for at least part of the model’s cross-modal behavior.

Compared with earlier interpretability work, the paper is more specific in three ways. It does not just track where audio information flows across layers. It does not only identify audio-specific neurons. And it does not rely on probing alone. It asks whether audio and text meet in the same internal units, and tests that claim with interventions.a:T73e,The study covers three LALMs: Qwen2.5-Omni, DeSTA2.5-Audio, and Audio Flamingo 3, across four attribute types: animal sound, emotion, gender, and spoken language. Several quantitative findings stand out.

First, cosine similarity between full hidden vectors is a poor signal for label matching. The authors report that label-independent dimensions account for 88% to 100% of its variance, which explains why matching and mismatching audio-text label pairs are barely separable in ambient space. This is an important negative result because it directly challenges a common way of arguing for multimodal alignment.

Second, in steering experiments, text-derived cross-modal neuron directions alter audio recognition much more effectively than random neuron sets. For Qwen2.5-Omni and Audio Flamingo 3, this holds for 35 of 48 labels; for DeSTA2.5-Audio, for 7 of 24 labels. A striking example is that Qwen2.5-Omni can be pushed to answer “happy” for silent inputs: the unsteered baseline is 20%, while the best random neuron set reaches 45%, and the targeted steering is substantially stronger.

Third, deactivating the cross-modal neurons damages the target attribute task before unrelated tasks. This pattern holds in all 8 task combinations for Qwen2.5-Omni and Audio Flamingo 3, and in 2 of 4 for DeSTA2.5-Audio. In Qwen2.5-Omni, deactivating only 0.2% to 0.7% of FFN neurons is enough to cause damage comparable to removing the most audio-specific neurons, even though the cross-modal neurons are less audio-specific. That suggests they occupy a bottleneck position for integrating acoustic attributes into the text-anchored semantic machinery.
