18:T83c,The method is Cross-Scale Directional Parameter Injection (CDPI). Given a larger source MLLM and a smaller target MLLM from the same family, the model is partitioned into three components: vision encoder (ViT), cross-modal projector, and LLM. An injection space E=(C,P) specifies which components are selected and whether the injection is restricted to linear-layer parameters or extended to all alignable parameters. Because source and target differ in hidden size, attention-head count, and LLM depth, the paper defines a heterogeneous alignment operator A_pi that projects source parameters into the target parameter space. The layer mapping pi controls how source LLM layers are matched to target layers.

After obtaining the projected source direction Δ, the target is perturbed without training: Θ_F = Θ_T + αΔ. The point is not to perform aggressive fusion, but to scan α, parameter scope, component choice, and layer mapping, and then observe capability-specific local changes.

The theoretical analysis uses a second-order expansion around the target parameters. For a capability-specific loss L_c(Θ), the local gain is approximated by a first-order term αA_c minus a second-order term proportional to α^2 I_c, where A_c = -∇L_c(Θ_T)^TΔ is the transfer signal and I_c = Δ^T H_c Δ is the curvature response. This decomposition is the conceptual center of the paper. It explains why the same injected direction can help reasoning but barely affect perception, and why gains vanish once α becomes too large: the first-order alignment may be positive, but structural interference grows quadratically.

Unlike standard model merging, the method is not trying to build the best merged model, and it does not rely on learned mappings. It is a controlled probe for transferability. One notable design choice is the comparison between linear-only and full-parameter injection. The results suggest that transferable knowledge is more concentrated in linear transformations than in the full set of alignable weights, which is an interesting clue about where portable computation lives in MLLMs.19:T6ad,The experiments use four Qwen3-VL cross-scale model pairs and evaluate them on twelve benchmarks. The most informative results are capability-wise rather than aggregate.

First, gains are concentrated in reasoning, especially high-level reasoning, while perception remains close to the original target performance. This suggests that training-free cross-scale fusion is much better at transferring high-level decision or language-side computation than low-level visual competence.

Second, component-level ablations show that the LLM carries most of the high-level reasoning gains. Injecting the ViT or projector does not produce comparable positive transfer. This is consistent with the idea that visual capability is more tightly coupled to representation geometry and encoder-specific details, making it less amenable to local linear projection.

Third, restricting injection to linear-layer parameters is usually more efficient and more stable than injecting all alignable parameters. Full-parameter injection may contain more useful signal, but it also introduces substantially more structural conflict, which matches the second-order analysis.

Fourth, both layer mapping and injection ratio are highly sensitive. Effective layer mappings depend on the specific source-target pair; there is no universal best mapping. Positive selective transfer appears only in a narrow low-α regime, and performance degrades quickly as α increases, consistent with a linear benefit term competing against a quadratic interference term.

The most useful ablations are the capability breakdown, component-wise injection, linear-vs-full comparison, and α sweeps. Those are where the paper’s actual contribution sits.1a:T55d,Read it in three passes: setup, theory, then ablations.

Start with the introduction and the main method figure to lock in the paper’s goal. CDPI is not proposed as a stronger fusion recipe; it is a probe for transfer mechanisms. Missing that point makes the rest look underpowered when it is actually intentionally controlled.

Then focus on the definitions of the injection space, the alignment operator, and the layer mapping. These choices determine what is being held fixed and what is being varied. Pay attention to why the paper separates ViT, projector, and LLM, and why it distinguishes linear-only from full-parameter injection.

The theory section is worth careful reading, especially the interpretation of A_c and I_c. The formula itself is simple; the value lies in the explanation. Most empirical findings in the paper are direct manifestations of that decomposition.

In the experiments, prioritize four groups of figures/tables: capability-specific gains, component-level ablations, linear vs full injection, and layer-mapping / α sweeps. Around 30–45 minutes should be enough to extract the main message. If reading more deeply, the two questions worth pursuing are: why reasoning appears more locally linear and portable than perception, and why transferable directions in the LLM survive cross-scale projection better than those in the vision stack.1b:T5ca,Each weight matrix is analyzed in its SVD basis W = U Σ V^⊤, with Σ the singular-value spectrum and (U, V) the output/input singular frames. The central empirical observation is that freely trained RLVR checkpoints stay near-isospectral to their base, and this proximity is not merely a high-dimensional artifact. Three interventions test functional necessity: (1) restoring base Σ while keeping learned U, V in RL checkpoints preserves most acquired gains; (2) training with Σ fixed throughout RL while updating only U and V still achieves strong RL learning; (3) the reverse—freezing frames and updating only Σ—yields only marginal improvement. This leads to spectral inheritance: the base spectrum can be reused, with new behavior carried by both frames. Follow-up ablations show both frames must remain adaptable; remixing only within input/output spans or holding one frame fixed leaves substantially more of the endpoint change unexplained. ISO (Isospectral Optimization) operationalizes this in two complementary forms. ISO-Merger (offline) consolidates multiple specialists trained from a shared base by reusing the shared base spectra and directly combining expert frame changes into a single fixed-spectrum checkpoint, without extra data, rollouts, or distillation. ISO-Optimizer (online) constrains training to the fixed-spectrum manifold of the base weights while allowing both frames to adapt, and can be wrapped around base optimizers such as AdamW or Muon.1c:T460,Read carefully, about 1–1.5 hours. Recommended order: (1) Introduction through the definition of spectral inheritance—this is the conceptual core and situates RLVR against pretraining and SFT; (2) the near-isospectrality observation together with the high-dimensional calibration—important for distinguishing geometric proximity from functional reusability; (3) the three key interventions (spectrum restoration, fixed-spectrum training, spectrum-only training) — the strongest evidence for inheritance; (4) ablations on two-frame adaptability, to see why one-sided or span-restricted variants are insufficient; (5) ISO-Merger and ISO-Optimizer sections for how the parameterization is implemented in practice, especially how frame updates are regularized and how the scheme relates to Riemannian/orthogonal constraints. Points worth digging into: whether the same pattern holds for SFT and continual pretraining, whether attention and MLP weights behave differently, how frame-level updates relate to Muon’s orthogonal updates and Apollo’s subspace projections, and whether the story extends to MoE experts.1d:T6cc,The setup is designed to be as controlled as possible. All models start from the same Qwen3-8B base, use the same LoRA configuration, the same RL recipe, and the same benchmark. The authors train two specialists on AppWorld subsets split by difficulty level, plus a joint model trained on the union of those tasks. They then merge the two specialists using standard methods such as TIES and RAM+.

The technical center of the paper is not a new merge rule but a geometric diagnosis of why merge method barely matters here. Because all specialists adapt the same LoRA target modules, their task vectors have overlapping support by construction. This rules out the trivial explanation that they simply modify disjoint parameters. The authors therefore measure directional geometry, especially cosine similarity between task vectors, and calibrate the measurement with floor and ceiling references so that small cosine values cannot be dismissed as a low-rank LoRA artifact. The key finding is that the specialists’ task vectors are nearly orthogonal, with cosine around 0.06–0.10, despite large support overlap. If the conflicting shared component is already tiny, then support-aware or sign-aware merge rules have little left to disentangle.

A notable implementation detail is that, although the paper frames training in terms of LOOP, the actual compute-constrained regime is on-policy with one update per rollout batch and no rollout reuse. In effect, this reduces to RLOO / REINFORCE with a leave-one-out baseline rather than a stronger PPO-style off-policy variant. That matters for interpreting the scope of the result: it is about LoRA specialists produced by relatively lightweight RL, not deeply optimized large-scale RL agents.1e:T586,The main empirical result is simple. On AppWorld, the authors compare two specialists, their merges, and a joint model on 168 held-out test_normal tasks using task-goal completion. All merge variants and the joint model are statistically indistinguishable on TGC. The only clear gain is RL over the base model. In other words, at 8B scale, with roughly 10 RL iterations and only a 1–2 difficulty margin between task groups, the merge-vs-joint differences sit below the noise floor.

The geometry analysis provides the explanation. The specialists’ task vectors have cosine similarity around 0.06–0.10, i.e. close to orthogonal, and the paper argues that this is not an artifact of low-rank LoRA.
