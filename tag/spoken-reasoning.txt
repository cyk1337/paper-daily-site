18:T990,The work builds on GLM-4-Voice, an autoregressive speech language model that generates text tokens, audio tokens, and control tokens in an interleaved format. In the standard spoken-response setup, the model alternates fixed-size blocks: 13 text tokens followed by 26 audio tokens. If the text stream finishes first, generation continues in audio until the turn ends. The authors also study STITCH, where explicit reasoning-token chunks are inserted in addition to the normal spoken response. Each reasoning chunk contains 100 text tokens and is delimited by special markers. A central question is how much RL helps without reasoning tokens, and whether RL still adds value when STITCH-style supervised reasoning is already present.

Training has two stages. The first is in-domain supervised fine-tuning. Mathematical QA data are converted into speech-input, speech-output examples, largely through TTS synthesis, so the model can adapt to spoken mathematical phrasing and output formatting. This stage is important because direct RL on the base speech model would face sparse rewards and a domain mismatch between ordinary conversation and spoken math.

The second stage is RL with verifiable outcomes. For each audio prompt, the model samples G completions. An LLM judge reads the decoded text answer and returns a binary correctness reward. The reward is defined only on the text stream, not directly on the audio. Optimization uses a group-relative REINFORCE objective: rewards are centered within each prompt group, and policy-gradient updates are applied to selected generated positions. The paper notes that this is related to GRPO but does not use PPO clipping. Another implementation detail is that sampling uses a temperature-adjusted policy rather than the raw model distribution, so the training objective must stay consistent with that sampling process.

A particularly important engineering choice is the loss mask: which tokens receive RL updates. Because a spoken model must keep producing audio regularly, applying the reward indiscriminately to the full sequence could entangle answer correctness with speech timing and acoustic continuation. The paper therefore studies different token subsets for optimization, balancing answer-focused learning against preservation of the interleaved generation structure. This is one of the most informative parts of the work, because it directly addresses credit assignment in multimodal RL.19:T652,The experiments center on spoken GSM8K using GLM-4-Voice, with comparisons to variants that include explicit STITCH reasoning tokens. The main findings are threefold. First, in-domain SFT already improves formatting and baseline spoken-math accuracy, but the larger gains come from the RL stage. Second, even without extra reasoning tokens, RL substantially improves mathematical correctness, suggesting that the speech model already contains latent reasoning ability that can be amplified by outcome rewards. Third, combining RL with STITCH yields further gains and reaches the best accuracy claimed in the paper for this benchmark, indicating that explicit reasoning supervision and outcome-based RL are complementary rather than redundant.

The ablations focus on whether in-domain SFT is used, whether reasoning tokens are present, which tokens receive RL updates, and how stable the judge-based reward is. The overall picture is consistent: without domain adaptation, RL is harder to optimize; SFT alone is not enough to close the gap; reasoning tokens help but are not required for RL to work; and careful loss masking is important for both training stability and preserving spoken output quality.

The paper emphasizes trends and relative improvements more than large-scale scaling numbers. For a foundation-model reader, the most useful takeaway is not a single benchmark score. It is the demonstration that, on an interleaved speech-text LM, a reward defined only on textual correctness can still reliably improve final spoken reasoning performance, even without making long chain-of-thought tokens mandatory.1a:T458,A good reading path is straightforward. Start with the section that explains the GLM-4-Voice output format, especially the 13-text / 26-audio interleaving pattern and how STITCH reasoning chunks are inserted. Then read the reward and policy-gradient section, focusing less on the formula itself and more on two implementation choices: the group-relative baseline and the temperature-adjusted sampling policy. Finally, inspect the ablations on loss masking, reasoning-token usage, and the two-stage SFT-to-RL pipeline.

If the goal is a quick understanding, around 30 minutes is enough. For deeper reading, focus on three questions. Why does a text-only reward not noticeably damage audio generation? Which tokens actually receive RL updates, and how is credit assignment handled? And what does the comparison between STITCH and no-reasoning-token settings imply about explicit chain-of-thought in speech LMs: is it the source of capability, or mainly an optimization interface?
