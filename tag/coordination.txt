18:T788,The paper has three main components. First, it formalises the collaboration tax using team-decentralisation and cooperative-game language. The tax is the success-rate gap between a full-information solo policy and a split-information paired policy. Two propositions characterise when the quantity is positive and connect it to a violation of superadditivity in the underlying cooperative game. The theory is lightweight, but it gives the empirical phenomenon a precise reference point.

Second, the authors build a 32-task suite grouped into Spatial, Relational, and CSP families. These families are chosen to span different sources of grounding friction: reference-frame alignment, lexical/category alignment, and indexing/ordinal alignment. Each task is designed so that a single model can solve it when given the full view, after which the information is partitioned across two agents. This makes the benchmark much cleaner than many agent evaluations that mix in tool use, planning depth, or environment interaction.

Third, the paper develops a mechanistic account of failure. It identifies a four-stage conversational cascade: grounding, querying, integration, and re-derivation. Grounding failures occur when agents introduce facts supported by neither private view. Querying failures occur when they do not ask the partner for missing information. Integration failures occur when they fail to combine both views before committing. Re-derivation failures occur when the receiver accepts an answer without recomputing it. The authors then show that conversation-shape features predict the tax well: model capability largely sets the intercept, while conversation structure sets the slope. Finally, they test a simple prompt-only intervention that adds one clause targeting each stage. The simplicity matters because it supports the claim that the dominant issue is coordination process, not missing base reasoning ability.19:T61c,The evaluation covers 11 models from 7 providers, with solo, homogeneous-pair, heterogeneous-pair, and slot-swapped heterogeneous settings. Even from the provided full text excerpt, several findings are clear. First, the collaboration tax is systematic rather than anecdotal. It exhibits two no-exception orderings: one over task categories and one over model capability. Harder grounding-friction categories consistently incur larger tax, and stronger models show monotonically smaller tax. Second, the main failure mode is not reasoning deficit. Conversation analysis indicates that many failures happen before the relevant information is properly requested or after it is received but not integrated or checked. Third, in heterogeneous pairs the stronger model bears the asymmetry of the loss, suggesting that weaker partners often drag the interaction into a poorer coordination regime rather than contributing complementary value. Fourth, the prompt-level intervention recovers a substantial fraction of the tax, and leave-one-out ablations show that the dominant bottleneck differs by task family. That is more informative than a generic prompt-length effect because it points to task-structure-specific coordination failures.

The experimental design is strong in one respect: the solo-tractable construction makes causal interpretation cleaner.
