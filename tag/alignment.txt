18:T90e,CATCH is an adaptation of Continuous Adversarial Training (CAT). For each prompt x that contains pressure to misstate a proposition, the method constructs two target continuations: y+ is the honest answer consistent with the model’s knowledge or the provided context, and y- is the dishonest answer that complies with the pressure and asserts the opposite. Training then pushes the model toward y+ and away from y-.

This differs from refusal-style safety training in an important way. In harmful-query refusal, the positive target can often be a generic refusal string, so standard supervised or adversarial training has a shared target structure. Honesty does not have that convenience: both the truthful and dishonest continuations are instance-specific, because they depend on the proposition and context. That is one reason plain SFT is a weak baseline here. It can memorize seen prompt patterns, but it does not naturally optimize for robustness to new forms of pressure or strategic settings.

The key mechanism is targeted adversarial training in embedding space. Instead of searching over discrete prompt edits, which is too expensive, the method perturbs token embeddings within an epsilon-bounded region and uses projected signed gradient ascent for K steps to approximately maximize log p_theta(y- | x + delta). This yields a local worst-case prompt perturbation that makes the dishonest continuation more likely. The outer optimization then trains the model to suppress y- and favor y+ under those adversarially perturbed inputs. In effect, the model is not just trained to answer honestly on the original prompt, but to remain honest in a neighborhood of semantically nearby adversarial variants.

The paper also pays attention to capability preservation. CATCH is trained with a utility dataset in addition to honesty targets, and the authors explicitly study how the choice of that utility data affects general performance retention. That is a meaningful detail: if the method only improved honesty by broadly dampening compliance or increasing refusals, it would be much less interesting. Compared with inference-time honesty steering, CATCH aims for a more stable trained behavior. Compared with large-scale honesty SFT, it relies on adversarial objectives to get more transfer from much less data.19:T67d,Several concrete points are clear from the full text provided. The training set is small, around 200 targets built from self-reporting scenarios. The method is tested on two instruction-tuned models: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen3.6-27B. Generalization is evaluated across three distinct dishonesty settings: instructed dishonesty on MASK, incentive-driven dishonesty in Among Us, and training-induced concealment on AuditBench. That benchmark spread matters because it tests transfer across different causal sources of dishonesty rather than within one prompt format.

The headline empirical claim is that CATCH outperforms all baselines and is competitive with the strongest honesty steering method on MASK, while surpassing an honesty-SFT implementation trained on roughly 50 times more honesty data. That is the main result to pay attention to: a small targeted dataset plus adversarial training may produce more transferable honesty behavior than much larger direct supervision.

Another important result concerns the utility dataset. The paper reports that utility-data choice strongly affects capability preservation and proposes a better selection strategy that preserves general ability better than prior honesty steering. The excerpt does not include the tables, so I cannot reliably provide exact benchmark scores or deltas.
