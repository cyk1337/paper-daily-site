9:T633,The method, CLIK, is a context-conditioned parameter generator that emits LoRA weights for a frozen backbone. The design choice that stands out is joint generation. Instead of producing adapter fragments independently, CLIK first encodes the context with the frozen backbone itself and collects representations from all layers. A Transformer-based generator then uses cross-attention from adapter queries to these contextual representations and emits the full LoRA set jointly. The motivation is straightforward: LoRA updates live across multiple layers and modules, and long-context knowledge is unlikely to decompose cleanly into independent local pieces. Joint generation gives the model a way to coordinate updates across the network. At inference time, the generated LoRA is inserted into the frozen base model, and the model answers from a context-free query prompt. This makes the one-time cost of reading a document reusable across many later queries. The paper positions this especially for multi-turn settings, where repeated re-reading of long histories is costly. Another notable part is the analysis of generated adapters: the context-specific signal appears to concentrate in a compact substructure of the LoRA, suggesting that internalized knowledge may occupy a much smaller effective parameter subspace than the raw adapter size would imply. The central quantity is the real log canonical threshold, or RLCT, which replaces half the parameter dimension in the leading asymptotics of Bayesian generalization and marginal likelihood for singular models. Under a local linearization of the pretrained network, LoRA becomes a low-rank trace-regression problem. Its KL geometry is governed by a forward-backward covariance built from input activations and backpropagated sensitivities at the adapted layer. If this covariance is nondegenerate on the relevant low-rank directions, the classical reduced-rank regression RLCT is recovered. If its kernel intersects those directions, additional statistically indistinguishable parameters appear, changing the singular geometry. The paper characterizes this locally through observable tangent directions plus a residual singularity, and globally through affine linear sections of determinantal varieties. A notable conclusion is that the global learning complexity need not be determined by the ground-truth stratum; singularities on other rank strata can dominate the asymptotics.d:T5e2,The method starts from the SVD of a frozen pretrained weight matrix W0 = UΣV^T. Let Uk and Vk be the top-k left and right singular vectors. OPLoRA constructs two orthogonal projectors onto the complementary subspaces: PL = I - UkUk^T and PR = I - VkVk^T. Standard LoRA uses a low-rank update ΔW = (α/r)BA. OPLoRA replaces it with a doubly projected update, ΔW = PL·B·A·PR. This forces the learned update to live entirely outside the dominant singular subspaces of the pretrained weight.

The main technical claim is stronger than a regularization heuristic: under this construction, the top-k singular triples of the original weight are exactly preserved. In other words, the update cannot perturb that protected spectral structure. This is a meaningful distinction from methods that only penalize parameter drift, approximate importance with Fisher-style terms, or rely on replay. OPLoRA constrains the reachable update space itself.

The paper also introduces a subspace-interference metric, ρk, to quantify how much update energy lies in the dominant pretrained subspace. Even from the provided excerpt, it is clear that the metric is meant to connect geometry and forgetting: ordinary LoRA should exhibit nontrivial alignment with the top-k subspace, while OPLoRA should suppress it by construction. The practical trade-off is straightforward. The method requires SVD on target layers and a choice of k.
