9:T880,Revelio shifts the search space from continuous pixels to discrete semantics. Images are abstracted as scene graphs with entities, relations, and attributes. Domain concepts are defined as subgraph patterns, for example a pedestrian in front of the ego vehicle at close range, or a barrier co-occurring with rainy weather. These concepts are not used merely as annotations. They are compiled into scenario distributions through a scene specification layer, using Scenic and a renderer such as CARLA. As a result, the unit of evaluation is not a single rendered image but a family of physically plausible scenes satisfying the same semantic constraints.

The search problem is formulated as black-box constrained combinatorial optimization. For any candidate concept set, the framework samples scenarios from the corresponding distribution, queries the VLM with a task prompt, and estimates a failure rate. The objective is to discover multiple high-risk concept combinations under a limited query budget. To do that, the paper introduces two search procedures. The first is a diversity-aware beam search. It expands concept combinations greedily, but the scoring is not based on failure rate alone; it also encourages diversity so that the beam does not collapse onto near-duplicate modes. This makes it useful for rapidly mapping the failure landscape. The second is a Gaussian-process global search with Thompson sampling. The GP models uncertainty over untested concept combinations, and Thompson sampling balances exploitation of known risky regions with exploration of uncertain but promising ones. Physical compatibility constraints are enforced throughout, so the search does not waste budget on impossible scene combinations.

A notable design choice is that interpretability is built into the search object itself. The method does not first find arbitrary failures and then try to explain them post hoc. It searches directly over human-readable semantic factors. Another important point is that the framework is black-box by construction: it does not rely on gradients, hidden states, or token-level likelihoods, which makes it usable for frontier closed-source VLMs.a:T738,The experiments cover at least two safety-relevant domains: autonomous driving and indoor robotics. Qualitatively, the paper surfaces several concrete failure patterns. In driving scenarios, VLMs show brittle spatial grounding, including incorrect stop-versus-continue decisions around nearby pedestrians. They also miss hazardous elements such as barriers even when those elements should be central to the decision. In indoor robotics, the dominant pattern is different: the models can become excessively cautious, hurting task performance, while still failing to properly account for sharp or dangerous objects. That distinction matters because it shows the framework is not only finding reckless behavior; it also captures maladaptive over-caution.

The main quantitative result is that Revelio discovers on average 3–5 times more failure modes than unguided random search under the same budget. That is the key evidence that the search procedures exploit semantic structure rather than merely sampling more. The paper also positions the two search strategies differently: diversity-aware beam search is better for quickly surfacing a broad set of high-risk modes, while GP-based Thompson sampling is more useful for global exploration as the budget grows. Another important experimental choice is that failures are measured over scenario distributions, not single examples, which makes the discovered modes more stable than one-off adversarial cases.

The abstract does not expose all low-level numbers such as exact model names, sample counts per concept set, or absolute failure rates on each benchmark. Those details need to be checked in the full tables and appendix. Still, the central empirical message is clear: realistic semantic search is substantially more efficient than random search for uncovering systematic VLM vulnerabilities.b:Ta2d,The paper starts by defining the object of study carefully. It does not claim to access the model’s internal belief state. Instead, it defines a protocol-conditioned surrogate belief: for a fixed observation set D, prompt p, and query protocol π, the LLM induces a predictive distribution over values at queried locations. This framing is useful because it shifts the discussion from hidden cognition to observable optimizer-facing behavior.

The experiments compare two query protocols. Under POINTWISE, each query location is asked and answered independently. Under JOINT, the full list of query locations is answered in a single autoregressive completion. Even if the two protocols have similar marginal uncertainty at each point, they can imply very different cross-location dependence and therefore different curve-level uncertainty. The paper also varies three prompt dimensions: prompt style, explicit underdetermination warnings, and structural information ranging from no structure to function-family cues, structural hints, parameter hints, and intentionally incorrect cues. The key claim is that prompt text acts as an effective prior in a behavioral sense: it systematically shifts the surrogate belief toward certain function classes.

A central methodological contribution is the uncertainty-alignment criterion. Rather than checking whether confidence matches realized error, it compares model-side uncertainty against the ambiguity that remains among sample-consistent functions. In an underdetermined setting, a good surrogate is not just accurate where it can be; it should also remain uncertain where the data do not identify a unique continuation. The paper further distinguishes token-level confidence proxies from output-level uncertainty. Token-level confidence is derived from the NLL of the generated numeric answer, while output-level uncertainty is estimated by repeated sampling under fixed conditions. This distinction matters because token confidence in a language model is not the same thing as epistemic uncertainty over functions.

Another strong design choice is the sequential-evidence analysis. Instead of evaluating only the final state after all observations are shown, the paper adds evidence incrementally in different orders and tracks how the elicited surrogate belief changes. The resulting confidence updates are often non-monotonic, with a dip-and-recovery pattern whose location depends on evidence order. That is a clear sign that the exposed ICL behavior does not behave like a stable order-invariant Bayesian posterior, at least at the behavioral level.c:T7de,The experiments cover controlled sparse-function inference tasks and downstream Bayesian optimization studies. The function families include Gaussian, quadratic, linear, sinusoidal, and logistic objectives, chosen for interpretability and control rather than benchmark complexity. The model suite spans Llama, GPT, and Qwen families, including open- and closed-weight models as well as uni- and multimodal variants. The setup intentionally avoids chain-of-thought and probes more immediate numeric judgments, which is closer to how LLMs are often used as surrogates in practice.

There are four main empirical findings. First, structural prompts behave like priors: correct structural cues improve the induced belief, while incorrect cues systematically mislead it, and that shift propagates into acquisition choices and regret in Bayesian optimization. Second, POINTWISE and JOINT querying expose different surrogate beliefs. POINTWISE is reported to be more ambiguity-sensitive and more faithful to the observation constraints, while JOINT is more globally coherent but less faithful to local constraints. Third, under sequential evidence, confidence does not simply shrink as more data arrive. Instead, the paper observes a dip-and-recovery pattern reminiscent of a Dunning–Kruger curve, and the position of the confidence valley depends on the order of evidence. Fourth, realized error and standard calibration are insufficient diagnostics in underdetermined regimes: a model can look numerically reasonable while its uncertainty is badly misaligned with the remaining sample-consistent ambiguity.
