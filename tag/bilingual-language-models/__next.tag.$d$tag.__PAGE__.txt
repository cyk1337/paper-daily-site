9:T485,The technical contribution is a clean training framework rather than a new architecture. The paper keeps architecture, model size, tokenizer, and overall language mixture fixed, while varying two main factors: the L1 identity and the order in which the two languages are presented during pretraining. English is always the L2, and the suite spans 21 L1s. The authors train under five exposure regimes, covering more interleaved versus more staged curricula, at three FineWeb scales: 100M, 2B, and 24B tokens, plus a human-scale 100M condition. A strong design choice is the inclusion of matched monolingual baselines for 20 languages, which helps separate genuine bilingual transfer from simple data-composition effects. Conceptually, the framework unifies structured exposure and sequential bilingual training ideas from earlier work, but treats exposure timing itself as the primary intervention. Evaluation is also broader than perplexity: the main target is psycholinguistic alignment, especially L2 eye-tracking reading times on MECO, with additional probes on cross-lingual structural priming and CEFR-stratified learner-error discrimination on BLiSS.a:T4d9,The most consistent result is that ordered or staged exposure tends to align better with human L2 reading times than fully interleaved training. The paper argues this effect is not explained away by higher overall proficiency or better language balance, which is important because those are the obvious confounds. In other words, the point in training when a language is introduced appears to be a meaningful determinant of human-like L2 processing behavior. A second important finding is that the curriculum effect does not transfer uniformly across tasks. On structural priming and BLiSS learner-error discrimination, balanced exposure is often competitive or even better. So staged curricula seem to shape specific processing dynamics rather than universally improving bilingual competence. In scale terms, the suite includes 285 bilingual and 45 monolingual models across 100M, 2B, and 24B tokens, all with checkpoint trajectories. The paper also compares against larger massively multilingual models and reports that carefully designed bilingual curricula can match or exceed their L2 reading-time alignment under much smaller parameter budgets. For researchers working on scaling and data curricula, that is the most actionable result.b:T424,Read this as an experimental-design paper. Start with the setup sections and verify exactly what is held fixed and what is varied; that is where the credibility of the later claims comes from. Then focus on the exposure-regime definitions, data-scale settings, construction of matched monolingual baselines, and checkpoint release strategy. In the experiments, prioritize the MECO reading-time analysis, since that is the main empirical claim. Pay attention to how the authors rule out alternative explanations such as proficiency and balance. After that, read the structural priming and BLiSS sections to understand where the curriculum effect does and does not generalize. The most useful deeper questions are: whether exposure schedule should be treated as an independent scaling axis, whether early checkpoints reveal transfer-emergence phases, and whether matched monolingual controls can be imported into broader multilingual or continual-pretraining studies.
