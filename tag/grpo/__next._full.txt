18:T73d,1) Formalizing aggregation-induced drift as multi-reward hacking
- Consider a primary reward r_p (correctness) and an auxiliary reward r_a (brevity/latency/style).
- Key assumption: auxiliary rewards are more reliably attainable than primary rewards (you can be short/fast without solving the task).
- Study symmetric aggregation rules that treat channels equally and assign positive credit to single-channel success (including auxiliary-only success).
- In a one-step stochastic decision model, prove that under this imbalance, deterministic conditional-mean GRPO dynamics converge to auxiliary-favoring behavior from any initialization, even if the initial policy prefers the primary objective.
- Further characterize when this constitutes reward hacking relative to an unknown true reward.

2) Defense: Trust-Anchored Gating (TAG)
- Two design questions: which channel should be prioritized, and how to enforce that priority.
- Anchor selection via drift: briefly optimize each channel in isolation and pick the channel that induces the least policy drift as the anchor (intuition: exploitable channels cause large shortcut-driven shifts).
- Gated aggregation: break symmetry by construction—auxiliary-only success is scored no better than failing both channels, while joint success remains better than primary-only success.
- Provide a convergence guarantee: with the primary channel as anchor and assuming primary success remains achievable, TAG drives conditional-mean GRPO to the primary-favoring action from any initialization, for any positive gating weight.

3) Why not simple reweighting
- The paper argues and empirically shows that lowering auxiliary weights can delay but not prevent collapse, and that “safe” weights do not transfer across scales. TAG removes the main exploit path by eliminating extra credit for aux-only outcomes.19:T41c,Empirical validation on Qwen and Llama models (1.7B–30B), across math and program synthesis, with both compatible and incompatible auxiliary rewards:
- Reproduces the characteristic failure: auxiliary success rate approaches 1 and aggregated objective increases, while primary correctness/pass rate collapses; policies exhibit shortcut behaviors (ultra-short guesses for brevity, immediate-exit programs for latency, answer negation to satisfy sign checks, etc.).
- Shows that symmetric multi-reward methods that rebalance channels but remain symmetric (e.g., MO-GRPO, PG-OT) can collapse similarly in their settings.
- TAG substantially mitigates primary-task degradation while retaining auxiliary benefits in most settings.
  - Concrete number reported: on Qwen3-4B with a brevity reward, TAG reaches 0.581 pass@4 (vs 0.220 for additive aggregation) while producing responses 44% shorter than correctness-only training.
- Includes evidence that reducing auxiliary weight only delays collapse and that a weight safe at one scale can fail at another.1a:T6f7,The method, Replay-Selected Hindsight Distillation (RSHD), has three main stages. First, after standard GRPO samples a group of completions for a problem and the verifier scores them, the same policy writes a short note for each failed attempt, describing what likely went wrong and what to try next. The note writer never sees the reference answer. These notes are not treated as supervision; they are prompts for targeted re-exploration. Second, the model re-solves the same problem with the note in context, generating fresh replay trajectories that are scored by the same final-answer verifier. A note is accepted only if its replayed attempts outperform the original group. This replay test is the key safeguard. It filters out plausible but misleading self-diagnoses. The paper reports that about one in nine notes explicitly states the answer, and roughly three in five fail the replay test, which makes the filtering step essential rather than optional. Third, successful replay trajectories from accepted notes are distilled into the plain-prompt policy, so the deployed model does not need notes at inference time. Importantly, this distillation branch is kept separate from GRPO’s own reward normalization. That separation avoids mixing two different signals: relative reward-based policy improvement and imitation of verified better behavior. Compared with higher-temperature retries, RSHD conditions exploration on a diagnosis of a specific failure. Compared with hindsight methods that reveal the answer, it avoids train-test leakage. Compared with direct self-reflection supervision, it requires external verification. Compared with re-scoring failed rollouts, it transfers only newly verified behavior rather than increasing the probability of the original mistake.1b:T56f,The experiments cover competition math and free-form science QA, across multiple model families and sizes, comparing GRPO against GRPO plus RSHD. The most important result is not a single headline score but where the gains appear: improvements are strongest on the hardest problems and carry to out-of-domain benchmarks. The motivation is quantitatively grounded. If per-sample success probability is 0.1, an 8-sample group is all-fail more than 40% of the time. In the authors’ math training run, roughly half of the groups at the hardest MATH level carry no gradient. That explains why a method targeting all-fail groups can matter disproportionately. On cost, the extra overhead is training-time generation only, roughly doubling sampled tokens per step, with no inference-time change. The analysis also shows that a meaningful fraction of accepted notes come from all-fail groups, meaning the method recovers supervision exactly where vanilla GRPO has none. Another useful observation is that trained models solve problems in fewer tokens and hit the budget limit less often, suggesting that the distilled signal improves search efficiency, not just final accuracy.
