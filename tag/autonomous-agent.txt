18:T4cc,The system has three main components. First, Kaggle-MLE Factory converts historical Kaggle competitions into executable MLE tasks with task descriptions, public data, and metrics. The Interaction Harness preserves experimental state and candidate submissions while leaving action selection to the model, instead of forcing a fixed workflow. Second, hierarchical agentic SFT transfers tool use, coding, and MLE optimization skills through selected trajectories and segments. The selection is guided by execution outcomes, which is important because long trajectories are noisy: successful runs can contain unhelpful branches, and failed runs can still contain useful local behaviors. Third, Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning uses completed experiments as window boundaries. The decisions made since the previous experiment result form an execution window, and those decisions receive a shared outcome-based reward. This gives a denser and more causally aligned signal than final-task reward, while avoiding the overly local view of single-turn rewards. WIRL also uses local progress rewards, asynchronous rollout collection, and policy correction to handle expensive experiments and policy drift during online learning.19:T448,This is not a pretraining paper in the narrow sense, so its value for foundation-model researchers is indirect. The main contribution is a concrete design pattern for turning language models into more reliable open-ended discovery agents: persistent memory, executable hypotheses, external statistical validation, and reflection over accumulated claims. Three aspects matter. First, it separates propose, evaluate, and reflect as distinct functions rather than treating the whole loop as prompt chaining. Second, it insists that discoveries must be grounded in executable code and statistical tests, which is a useful reminder that in open-ended research settings the LLM should generate candidate analyses, not serve as the evidence source. For a pretraining-oriented reader, this is better viewed as an agentic discovery paper with implications for memory, tool use, and test-time search.1a:T73b,The central move is to formalize open-ended discovery as a stateful Propose-Evaluate-Reflect loop. A hypothesis is not a text statement but an executable program over the dataset X. The program returns a binary judgment, supported or rejected, together with statistical evidence. Only hypotheses that pass significance and effect-size thresholds are promoted into a persistent claim store C_t. Rejected hypotheses are also retained. This design constrains the LLM to hypothesis generation and analysis scripting, while outsourcing factual adjudication to statistical procedures.

The main novelty is the Reflect stage. Instead of simply appending prior findings into the next prompt, DiscoPER periodically analyzes the accepted and rejected claim sets to produce structured guidance G_t. The paper highlights two kinds of reflective signals: under-explored variables and recurring confounds across multiple discovered relationships. That guidance then conditions the next round of hypothesis generation. In scientific terms, the system tries to do two things humans often do: fill obvious gaps in coverage, and infer higher-order structure from repeated local findings.

The framework is also multimodal and code-based. Compared with classical structure-learning methods, it supports a much broader hypothesis space than pairwise relations or fixed graph templates. Compared with AI-scientist systems that require seed questions, it aims for question-free exploration. Compared with generic code agents, it adds persistent claim accumulation and explicit meta-reflection. The use of held-out validation is also important: discoveries are not accepted solely because they fit the same data used to search for them. That does not fully solve multiple-testing issues, but it is a more defensible setup than unconstrained pattern mining on a single split.1b:T5d4,The headline result is that DiscoPER outperforms baselines on multiple discovery benchmarks, and that the reflection mechanism improves exploration quality. On the newly introduced iNatDisco benchmark, the system reportedly rediscovers more than 60% of real-world expert-verified patterns. That is the most important number in the paper because it directly measures whether the system can recover meaningful latent regularities in an open-ended setting.

From the available full-text sections, several experimental choices are clear. First, the benchmarks are constructed from real scientific data with literature-grounded expert claims, rather than from fixed question-answer tasks. Second, outputs are filtered through significance and effect-size criteria, so evaluation is tied to data-backed findings rather than fluent textual hypotheses. Third, the paper includes comparisons with and without reflection, which is critical for testing whether meta-level guidance adds value beyond simple claim memory.

If those requirements do not hold, the rest of the system matters less.
