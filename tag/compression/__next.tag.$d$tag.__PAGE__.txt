9:T556,PeekKV introduces a one-token peek on the decode path. After prefill, the system briefly continues generation before compressing the cache. It skips format tokens and inspects attention-related signals only at the first content token. The paper argues that this single observation is enough because head activity is relatively stable across subsequent content tokens.

From that one decode step, PeekKV derives two signals. The first is a head-group score, used to allocate KV budgets across groups of heads in proportion to their estimated importance. The second is an output-perturbation score, denoted as Δo, which measures how much retaining or removing a token affects the output of a given head group. This score is then used to decide which historical tokens each group should keep.

This two-level design is the key distinction. Unlike HeadKV, DuoAttention, or RazorAttention, PeekKV does not assume a fixed set of retrieval-style heads should always receive large budgets. Unlike AdaKV, it does not infer decode requirements from prefill attention. It reads the signal directly from the decode path, which is the central methodological claim. The runtime allocation also adapts across workloads: retrieval-heavy tasks naturally route budget to induction/retrieval-like heads, while code generation or tool-use workloads can shift budget toward other heads.a:T59f,The evaluation spans four public benchmarks covering long-context retrieval, text generation, and code generation. The headline model in the paper is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Two reported numbers stand out: on RULER, PeekKV preserves 95.7% of full-cache accuracy at a 10% memory budget; on LongBench, it preserves 95.9% at only a 1.5% budget. The paper also reports that AdaKV, which uses prefill-time adaptive allocation, underperforms the static alternative on 10 out of 12 workload categories in their measurements, supporting the claim that prefill-based signals are misaligned.

The more informative part of the experiments is the diagnosis. First, head activity varies most strongly around the prefill/decode transition and on format tokens; after filtering those, content-token decoding is much more stable. Second, the identity of important heads is workload-dependent: retrieval heads dominate recall-heavy tasks, but non-retrieval heads can dominate tool-calling or code-generation outputs. Third, ablations indicate that head-budget allocation alone or token retention alone is weaker than combining both. Fourth, the overhead analysis shows that the one-token peek mainly delays compression by a few decode steps, so peak memory briefly remains at full-cache level, but the extra runtime cost is limited. If the upcoming bytes can be copied from recent context, they are old information; if they cannot, new information is entering the stream, and that is where a patch should start. Formally, for each position t, m_t is the length of the longest prefix of the future byte stream that appears within the previous 128 bytes, capped at 16. Novelty is defined as -m_t, so shorter matches mean higher novelty. Since m_t depends on future bytes, it cannot be used directly online. The authors therefore build a causal approximation: a lookup table indexed by the previous two bytes, storing the average value of -m_t observed under that context. The table is fit on 4 MB of raw text in seconds on CPU, with backoff to one-byte context and then the global mean for rare contexts. At inference time, patching is just a table lookup plus thresholding to hit a target patch rate.

Several design choices are notable. The method needs no labels, no reference model, and no loss-based ablation signal, making it cheaper than both entropy patching and the earlier dependence-table approach. It changes the routing principle from surprisal-seeking to novelty-seeking. The paper also evaluates two variants: nov, fit on GSM8K and MATH training solutions, and novc, fit on a raw pretraining-corpus sample, to test whether the effect depends on math-specific fitting. Baselines include BLT’s entropy threshold, the prior dependence table, and a hand-written syntax+entropy rule. Models are BLT-style systems trained from scratch with cross-attention pooling, a windowed local decoder, and 128-byte context, at 1M, 12M, and 50M parameters on a 1.9 GB math-heavy corpus, with matched patch rates around 11% and three seeds per setup.d:T79b,The main gains appear only once the model is large enough to use the extra global compute effectively. At 1M parameters, all methods are near the floor on computed results, around 3–5%, and only the hand-written syntax rule noticeably improves final answers. At 50M, the gap becomes large: computed results reach 57.5%, compared with 20.7% for entropy and 35.1% for dependence; final answers reach 89.5%, compared with 59.9% and 78.2%. The paper checks that gradient clipping at 50M is neutral for the entropy baseline, so the result is not an artifact of training stabilization.

Generalization results are mixed in an informative way. The corpus-fitted novelty table, built from raw pretraining text rather than math-solution data, preserves the main gain, which means the signal is not purely task-specific. Outside math, novelty lowers bits per byte on prose, but only ties entropy on source code and synthetic execution traces, both in bpb and exact token accuracy. The mechanism analysis explains why: computed results often have ordinary surprisal, so entropy misses them, while novelty tends to spike where a new result string begins in math solutions. In code and traces, important structure does not align as cleanly with “new literal starts here,” so the advantage disappears. The discussion of boundary enrichment is also useful: the metric optimized by learned chunkers such as SOMBRERO is actually highest for entropy, even though entropy is worst on computed results. For a document s, tokenized by the evaluated model's own tokenizer into x1:T, the model assigns conditional probabilities to observed tokens. The ideal arithmetic-coding length is -sum log2 p(x_t | x_<t), which is next-token NLL in bits. The compression rate divides this ideal code length by the original UTF-8 size, 8 times the number of bytes. Lower CR means better prediction. The UTF-8 denominator provides a common text-level scale across different tokenizers, although each score remains a property of the model-tokenizer pair.

The data pipeline is a central part of the benchmark. It periodically collects recently published fiction, source code, scientific papers, news, encyclopedia articles, documentation, and related sources, covering 14 text categories. The curation pipeline applies quality filters, removes near-duplicates with MinHash locality-sensitive hashing, and normalizes Unicode with NFC. The non-English encyclopedia subset is sampled in a language-balanced way. Because tokenizers produce different token counts for the same text, each sample is checked with a diverse set of tokenizers and truncated to fit a shared token budget.

Within each category, CR is aggregated as a byte-weighted ratio of total ideal code length to total original byte size. The Overall score is an unweighted arithmetic mean over category rates.
