For a document s, tokenized by the evaluated model's own tokenizer into x1:T, the model assigns conditional probabilities to observed tokens. The ideal arithmetic-coding length is -sum log2 p(x_t | x_<t), which is next-token NLL in bits. The compression rate divides this ideal code length by the original UTF-8 size, 8 times the number of bytes. Lower CR means better prediction. The UTF-8 denominator provides a common text-level scale across different tokenizers, although each score remains a property of the model-tokenizer pair.

The data pipeline is a central part of the benchmark. It periodically collects recently published fiction, source code, scientific papers, news, encyclopedia articles, documentation, and related sources, covering 14 text categories. The curation pipeline applies quality filters, removes near-duplicates with MinHash locality-sensitive hashing, and normalizes Unicode with NFC. The non-English encyclopedia subset is sampled in a language-balanced way. Because tokenizers produce different token counts for the same text, each sample is checked with a diverse set of tokenizers and truncated to fit a shared token budget.

Within each category, CR is aggregated as a byte-weighted ratio of total ideal code length to total original byte size. The Overall score is an unweighted arithmetic mean over category rates. The representation has three parts. First, for each layer and KV head, the method selects k anchor positions and stores their keys and values exactly. The anchor budget includes a recency window, so recent tokens are preserved with high fidelity by construction.

Second, every non-anchor token is approximated separately in K-space and V-space by projecting it onto the direction of its closest anchor. This turns a D-dimensional vector into an anchor index plus a scalar coefficient. It is more expressive than replacing tokens with a shared centroid, because magnitude remains token-specific even when direction is shared. Keys and values share anchor positions, but assignments, projection coefficients, and residual sets are separate, which makes sense because K and V affect attention in different ways.

Third, only a subset of positions receives an additional stored residual under a fixed byte budget. Residuals are rotated with a randomized Hadamard transform to spread energy and reduce outlier sensitivity, then quantized at 2 bits per coordinate using per-token absmax scaling and a four-level Lloyd-Max codebook. The important part is not just the codec: residuals are allocated according to how much they reduce attention-output error, not according to generic reconstruction loss. Residual budget is shared across heads within a layer, so heads compete for bytes where they matter most.

A notable design choice is what the method avoids. It does not rely on decode-time retrieval from host memory, so there is no fetch scheduling in the critical path. It also leaves the attention arithmetic intact, which shortens the deployment path relative to more invasive kernel or retrieval schemes. The third is perturbation faithfulness, which probes whether the compressed model responds appropriately when intermediate reasoning content is perturbed in controlled ways. The authors are careful not to claim that KV states physically decompose into separate “answer” and “evidence” components; the distinction is behavioral rather than mechanistic.

The experimental suite covers ten token-eviction compression methods and one quantization method, across three models, multiple tasks, and multiple compression budgets. Tasks include mathematical reasoning, scientific QA, clinical calculation, and long-context retrieval. The paper also reports end-to-end results to show that the observed pattern is not an artifact of the replay protocol. The model has two coupled sets of variables: token-selection variables indicating which candidate substrings enter the vocabulary, and segmentation-choice variables indicating which candidate segmentation is chosen for each pretoken. The objective minimizes frequency-weighted total token count.

The technical core is the greedy-consistency constraint. If a pretoken is assigned a particular segmentation, then at every segment start there must not exist a longer selected token that also matches the bytes at that position; otherwise GL2R would emit the longer token and the chosen segmentation would not be realizable. This is the mechanism that ties segmentation feasibility to vocabulary content.

A second important piece is scalability. A pretoken of length n has 2^(n-1) possible segmentations, so full enumeration is impossible. JOLT therefore works with a restricted candidate set and adds segmentations on demand, based on the optimization state, instead of instantiating the full combinatorial space. The corpus is first split into pretokens with a regex and aggregated by count; optimization is then run on the top-N most frequent pretokens. The LP solution is rounded to a fixed-size vocabulary, and the paper argues that rounding is not the main source of suboptimality because the rounded solutions stay extremely close to the LP bound. Relative to BPE, JOLT removes the dependence on local merge heuristics. Relative to UnigramLM, it is not a probabilistic latent-segmentation model. Relative to ConvexTok and ToaST, the feasibility constraints are driven by GL2R longest-match behavior.1f:T4d3,The experiments focus on compression, LP-bound diagnostics, and rounding loss rather than downstream language-model quality.
