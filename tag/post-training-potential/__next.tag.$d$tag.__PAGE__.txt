5:T86d,方法核心是 RuDE（Rubric-based Discriminative Evaluation）。它不要求 base model 直接生成高质量回答，而是让模型在“好回答 / 坏回答”的对比中做判别，用判别能力去代理未来的生成潜力。这个设计建立在文中引用的 generation-evaluation consistency 假设上：一个模型若能稳定分辨更符合任务要求的回答，通常也更有可能在 post-training 后学会生成这类回答。

为了解决开放式任务评估标准不统一的问题，作者提出 4C taxonomy，把回答质量拆成四类约束：Competence、Content、Control、Compliance。虽然全文片段里没有把每一类展开到最细，但从任务构造可以看出，它们分别覆盖能力完成度、内容正确与充分性、格式/风格/结构控制，以及对指令和规则的遵守。作者不是直接写一个总分 rubric，而是为每个任务手工定义细粒度 rubric，再用这些 rubric 组合出 preferred / dispreferred response pairs。

数据构造上，作者把四类开放任务改写成 response discrimination 形式，覆盖 Medical、Legal & Finance、Complex Instruction Following、Creative Writing。关键是 generator-verifier pipeline：先生成候选回答，再通过 rubric 控制，构造“满足全部 rubric 的 preferred response”和“故意违反部分 rubric 的 dispreferred response”。这样得到的不是粗糙的正负样本，而是带有可解释错误类型的对比样本。RuDE 评估时，base model 只需判断哪一个回答更好，或者对候选回答做相对偏好打分。

和已有代理指标相比，区别很明确。MMLU 测的是知识回忆和选择题判断，PPL 测的是 token-level 拟合；两者都不直接触及开放式对齐任务中的格式控制、意图理解、约束满足。RuDE 则把评估对象从“知识是否记住”转成“是否具备学会对齐行为的判别基础”。这也是它与传统 reward model eval、preference eval 的不同之处：这里评的是未经 post-train 的 base model，并且目标是预测其可塑性，而不是评估一个已对齐模型的当前偏好质量。6:T733,The core method is RuDE, short for Rubric-based Discriminative Evaluation. Instead of asking a base model to generate a strong open-ended answer, RuDE asks it to discriminate between a preferred and a dispreferred response. The motivation is that discrimination is easier than generation for a base model, and that the ability to recognize better responses may be a good proxy for the ability to learn to generate them after post-training.

To make open-ended evaluation systematic, the paper introduces a 4C taxonomy: Competence, Content, Control, and Compliance. These dimensions cover whether the response completes the task, whether the content is correct and adequate, whether formatting/style/structure constraints are followed, and whether explicit instructions or rules are obeyed. For each task, the authors manually define fine-grained rubrics under these dimensions.

The data pipeline is also important. Four open-ended domains are converted into response-discrimination datasets: medical, legal and finance, complex instruction following, and creative writing. A generator-verifier pipeline constructs contrastive pairs. Preferred responses satisfy all rubrics, while dispreferred responses intentionally violate selected rubrics. This gives the benchmark more structure than generic preference pairs, because the negative examples are tied to interpretable failure modes.

Relative to prior proxies, the distinction is clear. MMLU mainly measures knowledge recall and multiple-choice discrimination; perplexity measures token-level fit to pretraining data. Neither directly targets the ingredients that matter in post-training, such as instruction adherence, controllability, or alignment with human intent. RuDE instead evaluates whether the base model already contains a discriminative substrate that post-training can exploit.7:T522,The main empirical claim is strong correlation with downstream post-training outcomes: RuDE scores achieve Pearson correlation above 90% with the performance of fully instruction-tuned models. This is substantially stronger than the weak correlation reported for traditional base-model metrics such as MMLU on open-ended post-training tasks.

The evaluation spans four domains and multiple pretrained base models, followed by actual post-training through instruction tuning or RL. The most concrete case highlighted in the paper is that RuDE predicts Qwen3-4B-Base to have higher post-training potential than Qwen2.5-7B-Base, and this ranking is later validated after RL. That result matters because it shows RuDE is not merely a size proxy.

The likely ablation message is threefold. First, discriminative evaluation is more reliable than direct generative evaluation for base models because it avoids the instruction-following bottleneck. Second, fine-grained rubric-controlled contrastive pairs are more informative than coarse preference labels. Third, evaluating across diverse open-ended domains improves robustness of the ranking signal. The excerpt does not include all tables, but the central result is clear: the ranking induced by RuDE tracks post-training outcomes under both instruction tuning and RL.8:T44e,For a skim-level read, focus on the introduction, task formulation, the 4C taxonomy, and the main correlation tables. Figure 1 is the anchor: it shows why MMLU-like metrics are insufficient for this problem. Then read the formal ranking setup to understand what exactly is being predicted. After that, inspect the generator-verifier pipeline and a few rubric examples to see whether the benchmark construction is careful or superficial.

Three questions are worth keeping in mind while reading. First, is RuDE measuring genuine post-training plasticity, or mostly latent knowledge plus local preference discrimination? Second, how well do the 4C dimensions cover reasoning-heavy or tool-using post-training objectives? Third, if used for checkpoint selection, could it favor models that are good judges but not necessarily the easiest to optimize into strong generators?
