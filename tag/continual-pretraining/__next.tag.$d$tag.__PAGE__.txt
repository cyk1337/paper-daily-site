9:T848,The method has two conceptual layers. The first is a reparameterization of the problem. Under the standard unweighted objective, a domain’s sampling share controls both token exposure and its average contribution to the gradient. The authors fix the sampled-data mixture and instead multiply each domain loss by a domain-specific coefficient. This keeps the observed data stream unchanged while altering the relative pull each domain exerts on optimization. In controlled knowledge (K) versus mathematics (M) experiments, they show that K→M staging, M→K staging, direct mixing, and fixed loss reweighting land at different points in capability space even under matched 30B-token exposure. A suitable math coefficient can improve both MMLU and GSM8K simultaneously, even beyond the best values seen anywhere along the staged trajectories.

The second layer is the adaptive controller Lare, short for Loss-Adaptive Reweighting under fixed Exposure. Fixed coefficients work, but require expensive sweeps. Lare estimates useful domain weights online from running domain-loss statistics. After learning-rate warmup, it applies a normalized inverse-square-root rule to derive relative coefficients. Intuitively, this avoids letting high-loss domains dominate linearly while still increasing their optimization influence; the square-root form and normalization make the adjustment smoother. The normalization is important: the mixture-averaged coefficient remains one, so the method changes relative optimization scale across domains without globally rescaling the objective. This keeps the intervention focused on domain balance rather than effective learning-rate changes.

Relative to prior work, this is not another data-schedule search method and not a standard curriculum-learning variant. Its key contribution is to separate “what the model sees” from “how hard the model learns from it,” then show empirically that the second axis is independently powerful. It is also distinct from importance sampling or token-level weighting: the intervention is domain-level objective reweighting under fixed exposure.a:T5ad,The experimental design is one of the paper’s strengths. In the controlled K–M setting with matched 30B-token exposure, the authors compare K→M staging, M→K staging, several direct-mixture ratios, fixed loss reweighting, and Lare. Direct mixtures usually settle at an interior compromise between knowledge and math. In contrast, fixed reweighting—especially a 1:2 math coefficient under 7:1 K:M exposure—moves the model into a better region that exceeds both the knowledge peak and the math peak observed along the staged trajectories. Figure 1 is the key empirical result.

At larger scale, under 4× Chinchilla with K:M = 7:1, Lare improves over direct mixing by 0.39 on MMLU, 13.57 on GSM8K, and 8.40 on MATH. Extending training to 8× Chinchilla with additional math and code data yields gains of 0.83, 17.06, 9.00, 6.71, and 5.60 on MMLU, GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, and MBPP, respectively, relative to direct mixing. This matters because it shows the effect is not confined to a toy two-domain setup and extends into code capability as well.

The paper also checks robustness across optimizers, sampled-data ratios, domain compositions, and training budgets. An important practical takeaway is that Lare may not beat an oracle fixed coefficient on every metric, but it reaches a competitive region of the trade-off in a single run, avoiding costly coefficient sweeps. The model first routes each token to a small subset of attention columns, and then, within each selected column, applies standard MoE-style expert routing in the feed-forward path. Concretely, each block contains C columns, and a column router selects Kc active columns per token. Each column has its own attention parameters and computes causal attention only over the subset of tokens routed into that column. This creates two kinds of attention-side sparsity at once: parameter sparsity, because a token only uses a subset of attention parameters, and map sparsity, because attention is computed only within the routed token subset. Inside each column, each layer then has E experts, of which Ke are selected by an expert router, giving the usual MoE sparsity on the MLP side. The effective computation path for a token is therefore hierarchical: column selection times expert selection. This differs from standard sparse-attention methods, which mainly reduce the attention map while keeping parameters shared, and from mixture-of-attention-head approaches, which often route attention more locally and independently per layer. NESTOR’s design is more structured: the attention subnetwork itself is conditionally selected, and that selection naturally nests with MoE routing. The paper emphasizes three distinct but coupled sparsity axes: attention-weight sparsity, attention-map sparsity, and expert sparsity. That framing is useful because it suggests the gains come not only from fewer FLOPs but from better allocation of model capacity. In continual pretraining, this structured routing may also help absorb new domains or longer contexts through specialized subpaths rather than by overwriting a large shared parameter core.d:T624,The experiments have two main parts. Given a base model θ_B and its instruction-tuned counterpart θ_I, the instruction-following vector is Δ_inst = θ_I - θ_B. New knowledge is learned by extended pretraining the base model on the target documents with the next-token objective, producing a knowledge vector Δ_know. - Inputs: a document corpus plus an evidence graph (citation graph or Wikipedia hyperlink graph).

- Key trade-off: no live retrieval environment and no executed policy rollouts; candidate sets and document contents are assembled offline, prioritizing scale and controllability over perfect interaction realism.
