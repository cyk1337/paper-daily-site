18:T73a,The method is simple, but the design choice is precise. The authors analyze token-level entropy distributions in long CoT responses and find that neither average entropy over all tokens nor average entropy over only the high-entropy tokens reliably separates good and bad reasoning samples. What works better is the sum of entropy over only the top-k% highest-entropy tokens. This leads to High-Entropy Sum (HES): compute token entropies for a response under a scoring model, select the top-k% tokens by entropy, and sum their entropy values to obtain a sample-quality score.

Why use a sum instead of an average? That is the key idea in the paper. Averaging dilutes the few critical decision points inside long reasoning traces. Summation preserves two signals at once: whether the sample contains genuinely difficult or branching moments, and how many such moments it contains. For long CoT, that is a better proxy for multi-step useful reasoning than a global average over mostly low-information tokens.

The paper then plugs HES into three training paradigms:
1. SFT: rank existing reasoning samples by HES and train only on the top fraction.
2. RFT: sample multiple candidate responses for the same query and use HES as the selection function for which traces to keep.
3. RL: oversample rollouts, then keep only the top-ranked subset by HES for policy updates instead of updating on all rollouts.

Two technical properties matter. First, HES is training-free: no reward model and no auxiliary selector need to be trained. Second, it is relatively aligned with the target model because the score comes from the model’s own token uncertainty, not from an external judge. The paper also shows that HES computed by a 0.6B model can be nearly as useful as HES computed by an 8B model, which makes it practical as a low-cost data curation component.19:T91c,The experimental coverage across SFT, RFT, and RL is the strongest part of the paper.

In SFT, the authors report that training on only the top 20% of samples ranked by HES already reaches performance comparable to using the full dataset, while using the top 80% consistently surpasses the full-data baseline. This suggests that a substantial fraction of raw reasoning data is low-yield, and that simply scaling data volume does not guarantee better reasoning supervision. A particularly important result is that HES computed by a lightweight 0.6B model performs comparably to HES computed by an 8B model for data selection, which sharply lowers the cost of offline curation.

In RFT, HES is used as a training-free selection signal among multiple sampled responses for the same prompt. It significantly outperforms random selection. The point here is not that it beats every sophisticated reward mechanism, but that it improves self-bootstrapped data quality with almost no additional training overhead.

In RL, the paper uses an oversample-then-filter strategy: generate more rollouts than usual, rank them by HES, and keep only the top 50% for policy updates. This outperforms the standard setting where all rollouts are used. The implication is important for reasoning RL: more rollouts are not always better if many of them are low-quality and inject noisy gradients. HES acts as a lightweight rollout denoiser.

The ablation logic is also strong. The paper compares HES against length, perplexity, average token entropy, total entropy over all tokens, and average entropy over only the high-entropy tokens. The conclusion is that only the sum over high-entropy tokens consistently separates sample quality. That matters because it shows the gain is not from “using entropy” in general, but from using entropy in a way that respects the sparse structure of long reasoning.

The explicit numbers available from the provided text are: top 0.5% high-entropy tokens, top 20% HES-ranked data for near-full SFT performance, top 80% for surpassing the baseline, top 50% rollout retention in RL, and 0.6B scoring models matching 8B scoring models closely. When reading the full paper, it is worth extracting the absolute benchmark scores and gains, because the paper’s credibility depends heavily on consistency across paradigms.1a:T7c3,This one deserves a careful read. The main value is not the formula itself, but how a very small token-level statistic becomes a unified data selection principle.

Start with the introduction and Figure 1. Figure 1 is the foundation of the paper: it shows that several natural entropy-based summaries fail, while high-entropy sum separates high- and low-quality reasoning samples. If that empirical observation is weak, the rest of the method is much less convincing.

Then read the HES definition and think through two design choices: why only the top-k% tokens, and why sum rather than average. These choices encode a structural hypothesis about long CoT: the most useful learning signal is concentrated in a few planning, branching, or reflection points, not spread uniformly across the whole trace.

After that, read experiments by training paradigm. In SFT, focus on retention ratio versus performance to judge whether HES is removing noise or discarding useful diversity. In RFT, compare it against random and other heuristics. In RL, pay attention to why oversampling plus filtering beats using all rollouts, and whether this introduces bias or variance trade-offs.

The most important ablations are: sensitivity to k, dependence on the size of the scoring model, and comparisons against length / perplexity / average entropy baselines. Those are the sections that tell you whether HES is robust or just a narrow heuristic.

Two deeper questions are worth keeping in mind. First, because HES depends on model entropy, it may prefer samples that are hard-but-learnable for the current model, not necessarily samples that are objectively best. That could be useful as an implicit curriculum, but it could also bias selection. Second, the paper is about reasoning data; whether the same principle transfers to pretraining-stage token value estimation remains open.
