9:T7c1,The method has three main layers. First is purely online orchestration. The system starts from the raw video file and the query. It does not build a query-agnostic memory, does not parse the whole video into a database upfront, and does not rely on a fixed segmentation-and-sampling stage. The agent reasons step by step and invokes tools only when needed. The motivation is straightforward: in long videos, relevant evidence is often sparse, so fixed preprocessing can both miss critical moments and flood later reasoning with irrelevant content.

Second is capability-driven tool design. Instead of assembling tools heuristically, the authors mine a taxonomy of atomic capabilities from MINERVA expert reasoning traces. They then map these capabilities to 60+ expert tools spanning scripts, VLMs, and specialist models, including overview, temporal localization, OCR, ASR, detection, and face-related recognition. The key point is not the number of tools but the clarity of their boundaries. The paper argues that many video agents fail because tool interfaces are ambiguous, which makes planning and invocation unstable.

Third is the division of labor between a lightweight orchestrator and expert tools. In this setup, the central model is no longer responsible for directly understanding the entire long video. It acts as an adaptive planner and evidence integrator. Once the relevant evidence has been compressed into a short context by tools, even a visually weak or text-centric orchestrator can perform competitively. That is an important systems claim: gains come not only from stronger end-to-end VLMs, but from decomposition, evidence acquisition order, and tool-use policy.

At a higher level, the paper assumes that the main bottleneck in long-video understanding is not single-pass representation capacity alone, but efficient evidence search and intermediate-state management.
