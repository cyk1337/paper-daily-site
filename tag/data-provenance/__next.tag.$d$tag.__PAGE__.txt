9:T5f3,The core premise is a hard utility constraint on rational adversaries: no matter what obfuscation tactics are used, a model that retains expert-level domain performance cannot fully erase intrinsic statistical fingerprints of proprietary data, which sit at the intersection of semantic substance (e.g., domain reasoning logic) and lexical form (e.g., terminological preference). The Distribution Provenance Audit (DPA) framework implements this idea with two components: (1) Joint Semantic-Lexical Similarity (JSLS), a metric that enforces dual matching of semantic consistency and lexical preference, avoiding both the fragility of surface-level lexical metrics to paraphrasing and the inability of pure semantic metrics to distinguish generic domain knowledge from specific proprietary data; (2) Self-Instruct Data Extraction (SIDE), a black-box sampling algorithm that bootstraps from the model's own high-likelihood regions without external prompts, eliminating prompt-induced bias to faithfully reconstruct the model's intrinsic output distribution. The audit is formulated as a formal statistical hypothesis test that compares fingerprints from the extracted model distribution against the proprietary corpus distribution to determine unauthorized data use. The analysis further notes that the same high-fidelity distributional fingerprints enabling audit can be repurposed for distribution-level privacy attacks and data extraction, revealing an inherent structural tradeoff between auditability and privacy leakage.a:T825,The main contribution is not a new training method but a recursive auditing system, ModSleuth, built around a stronger definition of dependency. The authors explicitly argue that with systems like Claude Code, raw information extraction is no longer the main bottleneck. The harder problems are semantic: deciding what counts as a dependency, and resolving references across inconsistent names, versions, repositories, and artifact families.

The first component is dependency formalization. The paper separates direct dependencies from indirect dependencies. Direct dependencies are upstream artifacts that materially affect downstream model weights or training data content, such as models used for synthetic data generation, rewriting, filtering, OCR, preference annotation, or reward modeling. Indirect dependencies influence development decisions without directly entering training, such as judge models used during evaluation or model selection. This distinction is useful because it separates influence on the training loop from broader influence on development.

The second component is an operation-centered graph representation. Instead of a generic 'built on' edge, ModSleuth attaches operation semantics to edges: generation, filtering, rewriting, OCR, evaluation, and so on. That makes the graph more informative for auditing contamination paths, license propagation, and evaluation bias.

The third component is an identity lattice for entity resolution. Public artifacts refer to the same model or dataset under family names, checkpoint names, repository names, internal aliases, and versioned releases. The identity lattice reconciles these heterogeneous references into a recursive graph construction process, while the agent gathers source-grounded evidence from papers, model cards, repositories, and dataset pages.

The real technical challenge is therefore schema design rather than agent orchestration: which edges to keep, how to score evidence strength, how to merge ambiguous references, and how far recursive tracing should proceed before confidence degrades.b:T6c4,The main empirical result is that modern LLM dependency chains are deeper, broader, and more recursive than standard public narratives suggest. The paper reports chains extending up to eight hops, and the recovered dependencies go far beyond initialization and direct dataset reuse. They include synthetic data generation, rewriting, annotation, OCR, and the training data of those auxiliary components themselves. In other words, a downstream model can inherit data, licensing constraints, and contamination risks through long indirect paths.

The paper also highlights several recurring findings. First, public artifacts often underreport or inconsistently describe dependencies across papers, code, and model cards. Second, there are potential license and terms-of-use issues because upstream sources behind generated or derived datasets are not always fully disclosed. Third, contamination and evaluation bias can propagate recursively, especially when judge models, synthetic data generators, and evaluated systems share ancestry. Standard one-hop decontamination procedures are poorly suited to this setting.

One notable observation is that some recovered dependencies were reportedly unknown even to original developers. That suggests the issue is not merely incomplete documentation by individuals, but a structural mismatch between current development practices and the recursive nature of modern model pipelines.

In the embedding stage, the signal is injected at the dataset level by modifying word-pair co-occurrence statistics rather than token-level probabilities. The authors first select moderately frequent words from the dataset, avoiding extremely common words that would look too natural and very rare words that would yield weak signal. Random pairs from this pool form the secret key. For each selected word, lexical variants are generated with ChatGPT, including synonyms and commonly associated terms. The dataset is then rephrased so that selected word pairs co-occur more often: examples containing variants of both words become insertion candidates, while examples containing one but not the other become deletion candidates. A rewriting model edits the text while trying to preserve semantics and utility. The resulting watermark is therefore a distributional correlation pattern rather than a fixed phrase.

In the detection stage, the auditor queries the target model through its API, collects generated text, and measures whether the secret word pairs co-occur more often than non-selected pairs. A statistical test is then used to reject the null hypothesis. The paper emphasizes that the false-positive guarantee is distribution-free and model-agnostic. This is the main difference from prior methods such as STAMP, which require open-model access to compare perplexities, or radioactive-style approaches that rely on persistent token-level watermark traces.

The design trade-off is deliberate: by moving to coarse but aggregatable output statistics, the method becomes usable in the closed-model setting and admits cleaner statistical guarantees, at the cost of relying on a weaker and more indirect signal.e:T4fc,The empirical evaluation is centered on fine-tuning rather than large-scale pretraining. Across multiple model architectures and benchmark datasets, the method achieves statistically significant detection, with the paper explicitly reporting p<0.01. One notable result is that detection remains effective even when the watermarked dataset contributes only about 1% of the total fine-tuning tokens in a mixed-data regime.

The robustness evaluation tests lightweight text modifications such as random deletion, synonym substitution, and emoji insertion. Detection degrades only modestly, and the method appears more robust than prior approaches that depend on more fragile surface forms. On the utility side, the paper reports that benchmark semantics and task usefulness are largely preserved after watermark embedding.

The limitations are also important: the evidence is strongest for fine-tuning, not trillion-token pretraining; detection power depends on query budget and the amount of generated text collected; and aggressive downstream filtering or training dynamics could wash out the induced correlations.
