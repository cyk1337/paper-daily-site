18:T57b,The key idea is to turn long-horizon decision quality into a binary choice at a decision fork. The authors observe that in software engineering and machine-learning research tasks, agents often make multiple attempts at the same task, or they take a detour and later correct themselves within a single run. If two trajectories share an equivalent prefix and then diverge, and if later evidence clearly shows which branch was better, that divergence can be converted into a taste question. Each question contains the task, the trajectory prefix up to the fork, and two candidate directions. The model must choose the better direction without seeing the later outcome. The benchmark is built from two mining procedures: parallel trajectories, where independent attempts diverge on the same task, and detour trajectories, where a single run reveals a wrong turn followed by correction. To reduce label noise, the authors do not simply use final success as supervision. They keep forks where the outcome difference can be credibly attributed to the judgment at the fork, using later evidence such as test results or research scores. On top of the benchmark, they introduce a training setup where a teacher model, given knowledge of the correct branch, produces reasoning that is then distilled into a student. This turns hindsight into a trainable judgment policy rather than a static benchmark-only signal.19:T437,The paper releases Taste-Bench, a benchmark of 502 taste questions spanning software engineering and machine-learning research tasks. The first set of experiments validates the benchmark itself: questions become harder as the horizon from the fork to the eventual outcome increases, which supports the claim that the benchmark measures long-horizon judgment rather than shallow pattern matching. The authors also perform human review and report close agreement between mined labels and human judgments when reviewers are allowed to inspect the recorded outcomes, which supports label quality. Model evaluations show that current systems still struggle on taste, and strong end-to-end performance does not automatically imply strong taste. The most important result is that taste appears trainable: distilling teacher reasoning conditioned on the correct direction improves fork judgments on unseen tasks and also leads to end-to-end gains on held-out agent tasks.
