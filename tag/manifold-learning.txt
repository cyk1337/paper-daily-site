18:T489,The method models prompt selection as a manifold-structured bandit problem with endogenous non-stationarity. Instead of treating each prompt as an independent arm, it first builds Latent Task Trees from model embeddings, giving a hierarchical approximation of the task manifold. It then performs Bayesian decision-making over this structure via Bayesian Manifold Curriculum (BMC), allowing observations on one prompt to inform beliefs about nearby prompts. This is meant to improve exploration efficiency and reduce the need for expensive batch regeneration. The paper also argues that curriculum quality cannot be summarized by final benchmark accuracy alone, and introduces three diagnostic axes: productivity, diversity, and utility. To handle target-specific training goals, it proposes BMC-T, a utility-aware variant that biases sampling toward target-relevant regions while preserving structure-aware exploration. Compared with DAPO-style dynamic sampling, the method tries to predict informative regions rather than repeatedly resampling until non-zero variance appears. Compared with standard adaptive curricula, it explicitly uses latent task geometry.19:T7b8,The method has three main components. First, the authors fit an activation manifold and a behavior manifold. They use tasks with known latent structure: cyclic concepts such as weekdays and months, sequential concepts such as ages and letters, graph-structured concepts learned in context, and physical position in a video world model. For each task, they collect internal activations from a chosen layer and fit a representation manifold Mh. In parallel, they restrict the output distribution to task-relevant concepts and fit a behavior manifold My. The important point is not just visualization, but the use of manifold geometry and geodesic distances.

Second, they introduce geometry-aware steering. The baseline is standard linear steering, which adds a vector in activation space and scales its magnitude. Their alternative, manifold steering, moves activations along geodesic or approximately geodesic paths on Mh rather than along straight Euclidean lines. The motivation is to keep interventions near the support of natural activations.

Third, they test the link between representation geometry and behavior geometry. One result is a scaled isometry relation: geodesic distances on Mh align closely with geodesic distances on My, while Euclidean distances do not. The other result is path-level causality: steering along Mh induces output trajectories that stay close to My. They also run the reverse experiment by directly optimizing for paths that remain natural in behavior space; the recovered activation trajectories bend to follow Mh rather than staying linear. That reverse recovery makes the argument stronger than a simple “our steering works better” claim.

The main conceptual contribution is to reframe steering from a problem of choosing a direction to a problem of choosing a geometry. In that language, linear steering is just geodesic motion under a flat metric, while manifold steering uses a metric induced by natural activations or behaviors.1a:T5ae,The experiments span both language models and a video world model. On the language side, the tasks include cyclic concepts such as weekdays and months, ordered concepts such as ages and letters, and graph-structured concepts learned in context. The main empirical findings are:

1. Distance alignment: geodesic distances on the activation manifold and the behavior manifold are close to isometric up to a scale factor, whereas Euclidean distances fail to capture the same structure.
2. Naturalness of trajectories: linear steering often passes through low-density regions of behavior space and produces unnatural intermediate distributions, degraded fluency, or diversity collapse; manifold steering yields trajectories that more closely match the model’s natural output transitions.
3. Reverse recovery: when the optimization is performed in behavior space to stay on My, the resulting activation trajectory bends to follow the curvature of Mh rather than remaining a straight line.
4. Cross-modal consistency: similar geometry-behavior coupling appears in the video world model for physical position, suggesting the effect is not limited to toy text tasks.
