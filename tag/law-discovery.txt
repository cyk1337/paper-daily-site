18:T7cb,There are three main design choices. First, the dataset is built from active but less canonical scientific literature rather than textbook equations. The final benchmark contains 118 problems, 291 candidate laws, 381 papers, and about 8M real data points across six disciplines. It also includes both single-group and multi-group settings. In the multi-group case, models must infer a shared functional form while allowing some parameters to vary across groups, which is closer to real scientific modeling than fitting one global curve.

Second, evaluation is split into two complementary settings. In SciLaws-Real, the model receives fixed real observations and must propose a closed-form law. Published formulas are treated as references, not mandatory targets. Proposed laws are judged along two separate axes: held-out predictive fit and source-grounded scientific validity. This is an important distinction. A formula can fit well numerically and still be scientifically wrong if it violates domain constraints, behaves badly in the relevant regime, or introduces unsupported mechanisms.

Third, SciLaws-Parallel converts the same scientific problem into an active experimental environment. The hidden law is not the published one; it is a newly synthesized structural variant derived from the published form, with coefficients and residual noise calibrated to the corresponding real records. Models start without observations, choose measurements under a fixed budget, and try to recover the hidden law. This setup preserves scientific context while making it possible to test novel-structure recovery rather than literature recall.

Operationally, the authors evaluate nine frontier LLMs under a unified ReAct-style agent framework, with a Python sandbox and, for the parallel setting, a fixed-budget experimentation interface. Each trial allows up to 30 interaction turns, so the benchmark measures multi-step reasoning and tool-mediated exploration rather than one-shot text generation.19:T5e1,The main empirical findings are threefold. First, models struggle to balance predictive accuracy and scientific validity. The paper gives a nuclear-physics example where the best-fitting formula introduces a nonexistent resonance peak, so it fails the validity check despite good numerical fit. This shows why held-out error alone is not enough.

Second, models are much better at reproducing known laws than at discovering new structure. A cold-recall audit indicates that SciLaws-Bench is less memorized than textbook-style benchmarks. Under weaker recall, models do explore beyond published formulas more often, but successful recovery of genuinely novel structure remains rare. Exploration is present; robust structural induction is not.

Third, models can generate stronger candidates than they can reliably select. Best-of-N search consistently exposes better formulas, but self-selection captures only a small fraction of that gain. This is an important result because it shifts part of the bottleneck from proposal generation to ranking, verification, and candidate selection.

In terms of scale, the benchmark covers 118 problems, 291 candidate laws, 381 papers, and roughly 8M real data points, and evaluates nine frontier models including GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gemini 3.5 Flash, and DeepSeek-V4 Pro.
