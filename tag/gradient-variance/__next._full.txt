18:T871,CADFT is built on top of Dynamic Fine-Tuning. DFT starts from the observation that standard SFT has inverse-probability gradient amplification: rare or low-probability tokens can dominate updates. DFT rewrites the objective so that updates scale with a bounded term proportional to (1 + log pt), avoiding the blow-up as pt approaches zero.

CADFT argues that token-level correction is not enough because instruction datasets are highly heterogeneous at the sample level. Some demonstrations are well aligned with the model’s current capabilities, while others are too hard, too mismatched, or too noisy. To address this, the method introduces a dynamic compatibility signal, computed from the model’s own likelihoods over a sample, to estimate how compatible a demonstration is with the current policy.

This compatibility score is then used to modulate sample-level update strength. High-compatibility samples retain stronger supervision; low-compatibility samples are downweighted rather than discarded. Three design choices matter.

First, compatibility is treated as a relative quantity, not a fixed threshold. It is normalized adaptively so that the weighting remains meaningful as the model improves over training.

Second, sample-level compatibility is combined with DFT’s token-level correction. So the final update depends both on whether individual tokens are pathological and on whether the sample as a whole lies within the model’s current feasible learning region.

Third, the paper adds delayed rewriting for persistently incompatible samples. Instead of rewriting from the start, the method waits for a warm-up period and triggers rewriting only at low frequency. The rationale is to avoid early self-reinforcement, where a weak model would rewrite difficult but valuable demonstrations into easier yet lower-quality targets.

The theoretical framing is that CADFT is a variance-controlled estimator: DFT reduces token-level variance, while CADFT further reduces sample-level variance. The theory is more interpretive than foundational, but it gives a coherent explanation for why CADFT might be more stable than vanilla SFT or plain DFT.19:T702,From the provided full text, the experiments span language, code, multimodal reasoning, and cold-start RL initialization. The empirical claims are aimed at three outcomes: better optimization stability, better generalization, and better downstream RL initialization.

The introduction does not expose the full result tables, but the intended evidence pattern is clear. First, low-compatibility samples are claimed to induce higher gradient variance, and compatibility-aware reweighting is reported to stabilize training. When reading, the most important evidence to verify is whether the paper shows gradient norm statistics, variance by compatibility bucket, loss spike reduction, or other direct optimization diagnostics.

Second, CADFT is reported to outperform both SFT and DFT across reasoning-heavy tasks, code generation, and multimodal reasoning. The most informative result is not the average score alone, but whether gains are concentrated on hard or shifted examples without hurting easier ones.

Third, the paper claims better cold-start performance for downstream RL. If true, that would suggest CADFT produces not just a better imitation model, but a lower-variance and more optimizable initial policy. This part should be read carefully: unless the RL setup controls for base model, data volume, and RL budget, it is hard to isolate the source of the gain.

Fourth, the rewriting mechanism should have its own ablations: when it starts, how often it is triggered, and whether it is restricted to persistently low-compatibility samples. Without these, the contribution of rewriting can easily be conflated with the effect of compatibility reweighting.
