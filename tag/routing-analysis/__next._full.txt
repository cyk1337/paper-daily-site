1a:T500,The paper proceeds in two stages. First, it performs a descriptive analysis with anchor-conditioned routing. Repeated anchor token ids are aligned across rollouts, and the corresponding sparse routing vectors are compared to test whether routing still separates problem identity, trajectory context, and effort mode. The authors define three anchor families—trajectory, boundary, and delimiter—and vary the routing readout window around the anchor. Routing representations are built from selected expert ids and router weights across layers, and then analyzed with nearest-neighbor leakage and KNN basin-purity style metrics.

Second, the paper proposes RAD, Routing Agreement Decoding. Given a pool of completed rollouts for one problem, RAD locates a fixed anchor by token id, represents each rollout using the routing window around that anchor, and selects the rollout that lies in the densest Weighted-Jaccard KNN routing neighborhood. During selection, it never parses, normalizes, compares, or executes answer strings. The answer text is only read after the routing-based choice is made. In that sense, RAD is analogous to majority voting in objective, but not in interface: majority aggregates agreement in answer space, while RAD aggregates agreement in routing space.1b:T52a,The evaluation covers 10 sparse-MoE configurations and 6 datasets, including gpt-oss and Qwen3-MoE variants, with math, GPQA, LiveCodeBench, and SWE-bench Verified. The results have three main parts. First, identical token ids do not imply identical routing states: routing still separates problem and effort conditions, with reported cross-problem and cross-effort nearest-neighbor leakage below 5%, suggesting token identity is not a sufficient statistic for router state. Second, routing neighborhoods align with final-answer basins, especially near answer-opening delimiters; delimiter@16 gives the strongest basin purity, while even marker-only delimiter@marker is already informative. Third, RAD is roughly on par with textual majority in math and GPQA, slightly ahead in the authors’ rollout pools, while using no answer-string comparison. Its more distinctive use case is code and agentic settings: on code generation, where lexical variation makes exact string voting awkward, RAD can directly select rollouts and report pass@1; on the decidable subset of SWE-bench Verified, boundary-based re-anchoring improves over random selection by about 4–5 points.

The evidence is best read as a feasibility result rather than a universal selector. It depends on access to router traces and on sensible anchor design.1c:T49d,The authors first run three routing probes. They compare native refusal continuations with teacher-forced compliant continuations for the same harmful prompts. They test whether refusal-enforced requests change the routing of benign prompts. They also create benign counterparts that preserve topic and surface form while removing unsafe intent, which separates topical specialization from harmful intent. In all three settings, routing changes induced by safety behavior or intent are smaller than changes induced by topic. Motivated by this, they introduce RASET, Router-Agnostic Safety-critical Expert Tuning. RASET identifies candidate experts using a contrastive routing-sensitivity score: activation on harmful instructions minus activation on benign instructions. This suppresses topic-general experts and highlights experts more specifically associated with harmful-request processing. It then applies parameter-efficient tuning only to the selected experts, while freezing the router, shared components, and all non-selected experts.
