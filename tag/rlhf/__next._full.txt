18:T73d,1) Formalizing aggregation-induced drift as multi-reward hacking
- Consider a primary reward r_p (correctness) and an auxiliary reward r_a (brevity/latency/style).
- Key assumption: auxiliary rewards are more reliably attainable than primary rewards (you can be short/fast without solving the task).
- Study symmetric aggregation rules that treat channels equally and assign positive credit to single-channel success (including auxiliary-only success).
- In a one-step stochastic decision model, prove that under this imbalance, deterministic conditional-mean GRPO dynamics converge to auxiliary-favoring behavior from any initialization, even if the initial policy prefers the primary objective.
- Further characterize when this constitutes reward hacking relative to an unknown true reward.

2) Defense: Trust-Anchored Gating (TAG)
- Two design questions: which channel should be prioritized, and how to enforce that priority.
- Anchor selection via drift: briefly optimize each channel in isolation and pick the channel that induces the least policy drift as the anchor (intuition: exploitable channels cause large shortcut-driven shifts).
- Gated aggregation: break symmetry by construction—auxiliary-only success is scored no better than failing both channels, while joint success remains better than primary-only success.
- Provide a convergence guarantee: with the primary channel as anchor and assuming primary success remains achievable, TAG drives conditional-mean GRPO to the primary-favoring action from any initialization, for any positive gating weight.

3) Why not simple reweighting
- The paper argues and empirically shows that lowering auxiliary weights can delay but not prevent collapse, and that “safe” weights do not transfer across scales. TAG removes the main exploit path by eliminating extra credit for aux-only outcomes.19:T41c,Empirical validation on Qwen and Llama models (1.7B–30B), across math and program synthesis, with both compatible and incompatible auxiliary rewards:
- Reproduces the characteristic failure: auxiliary success rate approaches 1 and aggregated objective increases, while primary correctness/pass rate collapses; policies exhibit shortcut behaviors (ultra-short guesses for brevity, immediate-exit programs for latency, answer negation to satisfy sign checks, etc.).
- Shows that symmetric multi-reward methods that rebalance channels but remain symmetric (e.g., MO-GRPO, PG-OT) can collapse similarly in their settings.
- TAG substantially mitigates primary-task degradation while retaining auxiliary benefits in most settings.
  - Concrete number reported: on Qwen3-4B with a brevity reward, TAG reaches 0.581 pass@4 (vs 0.220 for additive aggregation) while producing responses 44% shorter than correctness-only training.
- Includes evidence that reducing auxiliary weight only delays collapse and that a weight safe at one scale can fail at another.1a:T412,URW has an offline calibration stage and an online weighting stage.
- Offline: For each criterion judgment, URW queries the same verifier using a fixed pool of paraphrased judgment templates. Template-to-template variability provides a soft uncertainty proxy even under deterministic decoding. Using a small calibration set with gold labels, URW measures how this soft signal correlates with correctness.
- Online: For each new criterion, URW converts the soft signal into a split-conformal p-value, and uses that p-value directly as the aggregation weight for the criterion in a weighted-sum rubric score.
The key design choice is conformal calibration: it yields distribution-free coverage control, framed here as controlling the probability of assigning spuriously low weight to a correct judgment. Compared to prior work, URW is criterion-level (not only response-level), training-free (no uncertainty head/probe), and compatible with single-verifier, zero-temperature deployments (unlike sampling-based consistency or committee methods).1b:T426,The paper formulates response generation as an undiscounted episodic MDP: the prompt is the initial state, tokens are actions, transitions append tokens deterministically, and the scalar response reward is assigned at EOS. It then derives policy-gradient estimators under samples drawn from a behavior or inference policy. A key distinction is made between sequence-level importance sampling, which uses the product ratio over the whole response, and token-level importance sampling, which applies local ratios per decoding step; these estimate different quantities. The main technical point is that, without ratio regularization, the weighted score has zero expectation, so constant reward shifts vanish. After clipping, truncation, or masking the ratio, this zero-mean property generally fails. The proposed fix is to use the covariance between the reward and the regularized importance-weighted score as the estimand, equivalently centering the score term as well as the reward.
