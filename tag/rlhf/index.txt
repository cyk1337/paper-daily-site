18:T49f,CoCA samples two kinds of rollouts from the same policy: query-only responses and rubric-conditioned responses. It then compares criterion-level satisfaction across the two groups to estimate where the policy currently stands. If query-only responses already satisfy a criterion, the criterion is becoming less useful for learning. If rubric-conditioned responses satisfy it while query-only responses do not, the behavior can be elicited but has not yet been internalized. If neither group satisfies it, the criterion may be beyond the current exploration frontier. CoCA uses this diagnosis to recalibrate criterion difficulty while preserving the intended evaluation target. For policy learning, it does not rely only on scalar rubric rewards. A conditioned response is treated as a guided frontier only when it satisfies the diagnosed targets, and then it is used for token-level self-distillation. Otherwise, conditioned rollout optimization is used to expand the frontier before later distillation. Compared with standard rubric RL, the method adds criterion-level capability assessment. Compared with rubric evolution alone, it ties rubric updates to policy learning decisions.19:T650,DATC defines token credit via a work-based analogy: credit is proportional to the component of the policy update ‘force’ along the reward-improving ‘displacement’ direction.

Concretely, it brings two vectors into the same actor-logit space at each position t:
- Reward direction g_t: an end-to-end surrogate gradient of the frozen reward model’s score w.r.t. the actor logits for the sampled response. Because tokens are discrete, the method uses a straight-through surrogate to backpropagate through the sampled tokens into the reward model and then back into the actor’s logit space.
- Policy update direction k_t: the gradient direction that increases the probability of the sampled token (log-prob gradient). GRPO’s response-level advantage A determines whether the update reinforces (A>0) or suppresses (A<0) that sampled action direction.

Directional credit is then d_t = ⟨g_t, k_t⟩. Under a local first-order approximation, this inner product predicts whether moving along the GRPO-induced direction will increase reward locally at that token. DATC converts this into token weights by redistributing the response-level advantage across positions using a softmax over A d / τ, producing a token-level advantage while preserving the overall sign structure. A KL regularizer constrains deviation from vanilla GRPO.

The paper also provides a theoretical link: the end-to-end surrogate gradient can be written in an advantage-weighted form consistent with REINFORCE, and d_t can be interpreted as aggregating local advantages from a linearized reward model along the sampled-action direction.1a:T435,The audit is applied to eighteen released scorers and to six matched training conditions at two sizes within one base-model family. The rating sets include HelpSteer2, HelpSteer, and Stanford Human Preferences. A consistent finding is that reward model scores cluster by prompt more than human ratings do. On HelpSteer2, six reward models show between-prompt shares around 0.34 to 0.64. For the core 7B–8B models, substantial clustering remains even when a fixed instruction replaces the original prompt, although prompt means change; on HelpSteer, agreement with human prompt-level ratings weakens. A practically important negative result is that subtracting prompt means does not reliably improve global selection. On HelpSteer, it lowers helpfulness, including when the prompt means are estimated from independent reference responses. The matched training study also finds that a large mismatch penalty does not require correct prompt-mean supervision: the mean penalty differs by about 0.3 score standard deviations or less between shuffled-mean and true-mean conditions.1b:T70f,The method has three main components. First, the authors build a controlled set of moral dilemmas from four classic works in PG19 instead of relying on synthetic dilemmas. Gemma-4 31B extracts a situational premise P and two competing duty assertions D1 and D2. A Z3 SMT solver verifies that P∧D1 is satisfiable, P∧D2 is satisfiable, and P∧D1∧D2 is unsatisfiable. This filters out cases where a trivial compromise would satisfy both duties. Out of 185 raw candidates, 156 pass all three checks. Second, each dilemma is paired with five ethical lenses: Kantian deontology, utilitarianism, virtue ethics, care ethics, and social contract theory, yielding 780 training prompts. The policy is initialized from instruction-tuned Gemma-4 E4B, described as 4.5B effective parameters and 8B total parameters. For each prompt, it generates 8 completions with a separated thinking block and visible answer. Third, rewards come from a self-judged tournament. The same policy judges its own completions pairwise using a prompt based on Wachsmuth’s 15-dimensional argument-quality taxonomy. It reasons over the dimensions but emits four preferences: logic, rhetoric, dialectic, and overall quality. The overall preference is used as the reward. All 28 pairs among 8 completions are judged in both presentation orders, producing 56 judgments per prompt. Presentation flips are treated as ties. Because ties are explicit, the authors use a Rao-Kupper preference model rather than a plain Bradley-Terry model, then convert latent strengths into RLOO advantages. Masked thinking restricts the policy gradient to visible answer tokens. Compared with scalar outcome rewards or label-only moral training, this setup directly optimizes relative argument quality while controlling for trivial compromise and order bias.1c:T47b,The experiments address judge reliability, training dynamics, and transfer. First, the self-judge’s argument-quality preferences broadly align with human annotators. Increasing judge scale mainly reduces presentation flips rather than changing the task into a different preference problem. Second, RL induces a prescriptive register. The policy becomes more decisive, uses more forward-looking obligations, and relies less on caveats and retrospective justification. The change is not simply dogmatism; it is a shift toward a confident normative style. Third, transfer to external moral judgment benchmarks is negative or mixed. For the training setup, the main objective is a listwise ranking loss equivalent to the ranking term of PRO. Given a prompt and K ranked responses, the model is trained with a sequential Plackett–Luce objective that prefers the best remaining response over the lower-ranked ones at each stage. Response scores are average per-token log-probabilities without a reference model. When K=2, the objective reduces to a pairwise ranking form closely related to reference-free, length-normalized SimPO.

The central analysis focuses on final hidden states. The authors observe that hidden representations acquire a nearly shared directional shift across token positions, prompts, and both chosen and rejected samples. They decompose hidden states into a common component and a residual component, and show that the common-mode offset keeps growing during preference training. Because chosen and rejected representations are translated together, the relative ranking loss barely reacts: both scores move by similar amounts, so the margin changes little. The paper quantifies this directly: the offset can move chosen and rejected scores by tens of nats while changing their margin by less than 3 nat on average.

The next step is to connect this hidden drift to generation collapse. Since logits are obtained by projecting hidden states through the output matrix, a shared offset induces a systematic vocabulary-direction bias. If one token’s output vector aligns best with the offset, that token receives a persistent logit boost, forming a vocabulary well. Once the well is deep enough, decoding collapses onto that token. Importantly, the token need not be rare or have an unusually large output norm; in UltraFeedback, the collapse token can even be an ordinary token such as “Conf”.

The causal evidence is unusually strong. Keeping only the common component reconstructs the vocabulary well. Injecting the learned offset into an untreated base model recreates collapse, while norm-matched random directions do not. Centering the rejected-side loss to hide or cancel the common shift prevents well formation in the flagship setting. Explicitly penalizing the offset also stabilizes training under longer budgets.

A further conceptual contribution is the notion of loss demand: the per-token margin up to which the loss continues to push. High-demand objectives keep applying pressure and are more likely to amplify the common-mode offset until it aligns strongly with a vocabulary direction. Low-demand objectives saturate earlier, so the offset may still grow but is less likely to form a destructive well. This explains why textbook DPO at standard settings is often relatively safe, why SimPO sits in between, and why stronger ranking objectives can become unstable under longer training.
