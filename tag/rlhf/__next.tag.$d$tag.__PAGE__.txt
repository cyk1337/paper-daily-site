后半部分把这个模板推广到 KL-regularized RL、OPD 和 test-time search。虽然给出的全文片段主要覆盖 Part I，但从摘要与行文结构可以看出，作者采用的是统一的 Gibbs posterior 形式：q*(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x) exp(U(x,y)/β)，其中 U 可以是 log-likelihood、reward、advantage 或 search-induced utility。SFT 对应 U=log q_data 或等价的 teacher log-density；KL-regularized RL 对应 reward-weighted posterior；OPD 则是在 student on-policy 轨迹上，用 teacher 分布去近似某个参考后验；search / CoT 则可被看成 test-time 对 latent reasoning path 的 posterior reweighting。这个统一视角的关键区别在于，它不把这些方法按“训练范式”分开，而是按“先验 + 效用 + 近似器”来分解。这样能更清楚地解释：为什么 reverse-KL 风格的 policy matching 往往 mode-seeking，为什么 SFT 的 forward-KL 更像覆盖 teacher 支持集，以及为什么 few-shot demonstrations 有时像给 posterior 提供额外观测，有时却会和 RL 后的 policy 发生冲突。a:T46f,This note is not proposing a new training recipe. It is trying to give a single probabilistic lens for several procedures that are usually discussed separately: few-shot ICL, SFT, KL-regularized RL, on-policy distillation, and test-time search / chain-of-thought. The central claim is that they can all be written in the same two-step pattern: first construct a generalized Bayes or Gibbs posterior from a prior/reference policy plus a utility signal; then fit a parametric model or policy to approximate that posterior or its posterior predictive. The paper focuses on two key identifications. First, under a hierarchical task distribution, few-shot ICL targets the Bayes posterior predictive. Second, SFT is a forward-KL projection onto a teacher conditional distribution, while KL-regularized RL and OPD approximate reward- or advantage-reweighted posteriors under a reference policy. The framework is meant to explain why ICL and SFT can partially substitute for each other, why few-shot prompting can behave oddly on RL-tuned reasoning models, and why OPD, RLHF/RLVR, and search-based inference are less disjoint than they appear.b:T436,Its value for pretraining research is conceptual rather than algorithmic. It gives a cleaner interface between pretraining, post-training, and test-time adaptation by forcing three questions: what is the target distribution, what role does the prior/reference model play, and whether training and inference-time search are approximating the same posterior or optimizing different objects. For pretraining researchers, two points matter. First, ICL is framed not as a prompting trick but as amortized Bayesian inference learned during pretraining; that changes how one thinks about task mixtures, context structure, and data organization. Second, the differences among SFT, OPD, and KL-regularized RL become easier to localize: KL direction, posterior temperature, utility definition, and approximation family. That helps explain why some post-training signals preserve the pretraining distribution while others overwrite it more aggressively. The contribution is moderate in scale and mostly about unification, but it is useful for thinking about the pretrain↔post-train loop.c:T806,The paper is a theory-oriented note. Part I starts from standard SFT and rewrites conditional maximum likelihood as a forward-KL projection onto the true teacher conditional q_true(y|x): minimizing expected negative log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing E_x[KL(q_true(.|x) || p_theta(.|x))] up to an entropy term independent of the model parameters. This matters because it makes SFT explicitly a problem of approximating a target conditional distribution. The paper then introduces a hierarchical Bayesian task model: sample a task function f, then sample within-task demonstrations D_k and a query. For any in-context predictor M(D_k, x), the Bayes posterior predictive is the unique minimizer of expected log-loss. If a Transformer is pretrained over the same task distribution, then in the infinite-data/infinite-capacity limit its optimal predictor is exactly that posterior predictive. The point is not merely to restate “ICL is Bayes,” but to place ICL and SFT inside the same language of target-distribution approximation.

The later sections extend the same template to KL-regularized RL, OPD, and test-time search. The visible excerpt mainly covers Part I, but the abstract and setup make the intended form clear: q*(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x) exp(U(x,y)/β), where U can be log-likelihood, reward, advantage, or a search-induced utility. SFT corresponds to a likelihood-defined target; KL-regularized RL corresponds to a reward-weighted posterior; OPD can be viewed as fitting the student to a teacher-defined posterior on student-generated trajectories; and search / CoT acts like posterior reweighting over latent reasoning paths at inference time. The useful decomposition is not by training stage but by prior, utility, temperature, and approximation family. That decomposition clarifies why reverse-KL style policy matching is often mode-seeking, why forward-KL SFT is more support-covering, and why few-shot demonstrations sometimes behave like extra observations for posterior updating but can also conflict with an RL-shaped policy.d:T524,The method is Adaptive Multi-Reward Projection, or AMRP. Instead of using a fixed weighted sum of reward dimensions throughout training, AMRP updates the projection weights online based on training dynamics. It uses three signals.

The first is relative shortfall, which measures whether a reward dimension is lagging behind the others. If a dimension remains under-optimized, its weight is increased. The second is reward volatility, which tracks instability in a dimension. High volatility suggests that the policy has not stabilized there, so AMRP keeps attention on it. The third is recent progress, which measures whether a dimension is improving, stagnating, or regressing. Saturated and stable dimensions get down-weighted; stagnant or regressing dimensions get up-weighted.

This design addresses the two identified failure modes in a practical way. It does not eliminate the information loss of scalarization entirely, but it reduces persistent aliasing by making the projection time-varying rather than static. More importantly, it counters shortcut lock-in by reallocating learning pressure toward neglected dimensions. Compared with manual reward tuning or simple normalization, the key difference is that AMRP is driven by observed reward dynamics during training rather than by fixed prior assumptions.e:T4a0,The evaluation covers three multi-reward settings: rule-based mathematical reasoning, citation-grounded generation with automatic evaluators, and open-ended alignment using learned reward-model scores. The main empirical claim is that AMRP consistently improves downstream performance across all three, with especially strong gains in math reasoning and citation-grounded generation, and smaller but still consistent gains in the more correlated open-ended alignment setting.

The paper also provides mechanism-level evidence. It shows that under static aggregation, training is often dominated early by dense rewards such as format or length, while harder dimensions such as correctness remain under-optimized. AMRP changes the reward-profile dynamics by shifting weight toward lagging or unstable dimensions, which leads to more balanced profiles over time. Another useful result is compatibility: the method composes with PPO, GRPO, and GDPO, suggesting that it is best viewed as a reward-aggregation module rather than a replacement for the RL optimizer itself.
