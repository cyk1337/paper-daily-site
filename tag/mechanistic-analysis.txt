18:T725,The paper compares three state structures under a shared setup. Frontier superposition stores only the active alternatives at the current step. Cumulative superposition stores the full reached history. General weighted superposition allows arbitrary weight allocation over that history. All three use the same graph, candidate bank, downstream query family, and decoder, so the comparison isolates only one design choice: what the continuous state preserves.

The theoretical core is a critical-dimension analysis. Objects receive independent Gaussian embeddings, and the latent state is a normalized superposition of those embeddings. A common decoder then tries to distinguish a useful direction from competing directions. The authors derive an exact finite-dimensional score law and use it to compare the minimum hidden dimension needed for reliable decoding. The main result is that for a broad class of state-independent, nonlocalized, bounded-influence queries, cumulative superposition benefits from coherent reinforcement among historically useful components, while unrelated components behave mostly as random interference. So even though cumulative states have larger support, the signal relevant to future computation can be stronger rather than weaker.

The paper then maps this abstraction back to Transformer mechanics. Attention expands the current computation, while residual writing determines whether previous objects are discarded or preserved. In weighted superpositions, the weakest retained coefficient becomes the routing bottleneck: if some historical items receive too little weight, later attention cannot reliably reuse them. This leads to a minimax design principle: when future queries are unknown, uniform cumulative weighting is robustly near-optimal, and in the formal setup it is exactly minimax.19:T4e1,The experiments are structured to validate the theory rather than to chase a benchmark headline. First, there are controlled simulations checking that the finite-dimensional score law predicts actual retrieval and discrimination behavior. Second, width-scaling experiments compare frontier, cumulative, and weighted superpositions as hidden dimension varies. The central empirical pattern is that cumulative superposition reaches usable performance at smaller widths than frontier superposition for the nonlocalized query classes studied in the theory. Third, the paper implements the attention-residual construction in lightweight models and GPT-2-style token-level settings to test the mechanism-level claim that the weakest retained coefficient is the practical routing bottleneck.

Two aspects are especially useful. One, the cumulative advantage is not restricted to trivial symmetric or purely additive queries; the query family is allowed to be continuous, correlated, asymmetric, and identity-dependent. Two, the failure mode of weighted superposition is interpretable: the problem is not simply insufficient total energy, but poor allocation, where potentially useful historical items are assigned coefficients too small to be reused later.1a:T4b0,Start with the setup and the definitions of the three superposition structures. The paper is easy to misread if one forgets that the task and decoder are held fixed; only the retention policy changes. Then read the critical-dimension section carefully. The key ideas are why nonlocalized queries allow cumulative history to create coherent gains, and how the finite-dimensional score law reconciles larger support with better recoverability. After that, read the Transformer mechanism section, which is where the abstract result becomes relevant to actual model design through attention and residual writing.

If time is limited, read the introduction, the three state definitions, the main theorem and its figures, the minimax result for weighted superpositions, and the GPT-2 or token-level experiments. About 30 to 45 minutes should be enough for the main line. The deeper questions are whether the query class matches real next-step computation in LLM reasoning, how sensitive the conclusions are to the independent Gaussian embedding assumption, and how one would train a recurrent or latent-CoT model to preserve history in a balanced way without flooding the state with irrelevant information.1b:T7bf,The method is deliberately simple but well targeted. The authors define two axes. Direct rate (DR) measures how often the model repeats its top answer over 30 samples of the original prompt at temperature 0.6. This captures concentration under one wording. Framed rate (FR) then asks whether that same direct top answer survives 30 matched scenario reframings of the underlying question. Importantly, FR is not the concentration of answers under reframing; it is the reappearance rate of the original direct top answer. That design choice is the core of the paper, because it measures persistence of a specific preference rather than generic consistency. The final bias depth score is π = DR × FR, which is high only when the model is both concentrated under direct prompting and robust to reframing.

The dataset construction is also central. They build 4,442 opinion prompt families, each grounded in a real Olmo-3-7B-SFT training example, and create matched everyday-scenario reframings for each family. Because the task is open-ended generation rather than multiple choice, answer normalization is necessary to merge semantically equivalent outputs and avoid contaminating FR with superficial phrasing variation. The contribution is therefore less about a new model and more about turning a causally meaningful question—does a preference survive paraphrastic and situational variation?—into a scalable evaluation protocol.

The paper then runs this protocol across the full Olmo-3-7B release pipeline: Pretrained → SFT → DPO → RLVR. That allows stage-wise tracking of bias depth. It also adds two attribution analyses: whether the post-SFT top answer matches the pretrained top answer, and whether a biased answer can be traced back to a specific SFT training example. Finally, it tests removability using continued LoRA-SFT and prompt-based debiasing with GEPA, asking whether Deep and Shallow biases differ not just in origin but also in how hard they are to erase.1c:T5c3,There are four results that matter most. First, across four post-SFT models, 35.8% to 77.7% of the 4,442 prompt families look biased under direct prompting, but only about a quarter of those direct biases survive reframing. So most of what a single-prompt protocol would count as bias is shallow. Second, along the full Olmo-3-7B pipeline, most Deep bias is already present at the pretrained stage, whereas Shallow bias keeps increasing through SFT and every later post-training stage. That is the clearest empirical support for the paper’s main interpretation. Third, biases that survive reframing are much more likely to match the pretrained model’s top answer, suggesting inheritance from pretraining rather than creation by SFT alone. At the same time, clean attribution to one specific SFT example works only for a small minority of cases, which argues against a simplistic single-example memorization story and points instead to broader distributional effects. Fourth, debiasing is asymmetric: continued LoRA-SFT reduces Deep bias by only 4.5 percentage points versus 10.3 points for Shallow bias, and GEPA reduces Deep bias by 1.6 points versus 5.8 points for Shallow. Those removability gaps line up with the stage-of-origin analysis.

For a pretraining reader, the main signal is not any one absolute score but the consistency of the whole picture: biases with stronger evidence of pretraining inheritance are more robust to reframing and harder to remove later.1d:T483,This is a skim paper, but a careful skim is worthwhile. Start with the introduction and Section 2 to internalize the DR/FR split. The key detail is that FR tracks recurrence of the direct top answer under reframing, not the top answer under each reframed prompt. Without that detail, the paper can look like a generic robustness study, which it is not. Then read the dataset section closely enough to understand how prompt families, scenario reframings, and answer normalization are built; the validity of the Deep/Shallow distinction depends heavily on these choices. In the experiments, prioritize the stage-wise analysis, the pretrained-versus-SFT attribution, and the debiasing section. When looking at figures, focus on whether the same answer persists across checkpoints and how much each debiasing method moves Deep versus Shallow cases.

A solid read takes about 25–35 minutes. If your work touches data or alignment, the two parts worth deeper inspection are the open-ended answer matching procedure and the cases that cannot be traced to a single SFT example, since those are the ones most suggestive of distribution-level pretraining effects.1e:T44f,The protocol represents each behavioral evaluation as a set of conditions with measured rates. A condition may be an MMLU subject in a sycophancy test, a decision-topic and demographic cell in a discrimination evaluation, or an agentic scenario. A prediction method outputs one rate per condition, and performance is scored by correlation and calibration on frozen splits. The paper varies three axes independently: information available to the model, from abstract descriptions to exact items and behavior history; the subject of the prompt, including “you,” a named model, and “capable AI agents in general”; and the question format, either direct rate prediction or forced choice. The crucial design choice is the subject control. If self-directed prompts perform no better than generic-agent prompts, positive correlations cannot be interpreted as privileged self-knowledge. 第二步是机制诊断。作者把问题归因到 attention-sink corruption。现代 LLM 往往会把大量注意力质量集中到少数早期位置，这些位置的 hidden state 又被少数高幅值固定特征维度主导，形成 sink。论文的假设是，这种 sink 有助于稳定每个位置对关键表面 token 的注意力分配，从而支撑格式敏感任务。VL fine-tuning 会扰动 query/key 投影 Wq、Wk；由于 logit shift 会随输入幅值放大，这种扰动在 sink 位置被特别放大，导致 per-head sink collapse。作者进一步分析不同 QK normalization 设计的差异：如果是 per-head QK-RMSNorm，可以直接去掉对原始输入幅值的显式依赖；如果只是 layerwise normalization，则每个 head 的尺度仍受整层能量分配影响，弱 head 会被进一步压缩，因此到 VL 训练开始前，某些 backbone 的 per-head sink 已经偏弱。

First, it isolates the phenomenon. The authors compare released VLMs against their reference LLM backbones on a text-only evaluation suite, and add text-only further-training controls plus a matched VL-versus-text training trajectory from the same base model. This is meant to show that the degradation is not just a generic consequence of continued training, but is tied to multimodal adaptation.

Second, it proposes a mechanism: attention-sink corruption. Many modern LLMs place a large fraction of attention mass on a few early positions. Those positions are dominated by a small set of high-magnitude feature dimensions and act as sinks. The paper argues that these sinks help stabilize attention allocation in ways that matter for tasks graded on exact output form. During VL fine-tuning, perturbations to the query and key projections, Wq and Wk, induce logit shifts that scale with input magnitude. Because sink positions have unusually large magnitude, the perturbation is amplified there, causing per-head sink collapse. The architecture analysis then distinguishes per-head QK-RMSNorm from layerwise normalization: per-head normalization removes explicit raw-magnitude dependence, while layerwise normalization can still compress weak heads because each head’s scale is tied to the layer’s total energy.

Third, the paper introduces a predictive metric and tests interventions. Sink Strength is a single scalar computed on the base LLM, before VL training, to quantify head-level sink concentration. It predicts how much text capability will be lost after VL adaptation. The authors then separate base-side vulnerability from update-induced corruption by combining pre-VL sink weakness with post-VL sink-gap perturbation. Finally, they run two negative controls: injecting QK-RMSNorm after pretraining and then doing VL training, and applying several post-VL weight-merging methods. The language backbone is extracted from each VLM and evaluated as a standalone causal LM on a nine-task text suite against a reference LLM.

Several results stand out. First, the text-capability gap reaches double-digit points on multiple format-sensitive tasks, while matched text-only further training causes much smaller degradation. That supports a modality-specific effect. Second, Sink Strength is a strong predictor of post-VL damage: the headline rank correlation is Spearman rho = 0.97, and correlations remain high across six pairs and four format-sensitive tasks, with rho >= 0.88. Fourth, native per-head QK-RMSNorm is associated with better sink preservation, but post-pretraining injection of the same module does not reproduce the benefit. Fifth, post-VL weight merging, including linear, spectral, and sparsified variants, fails to recover the lost text capability under the tested settings.
