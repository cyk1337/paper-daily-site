Four parameterizations are compared: FAFO (both circuits factorized), CAFO (collapsed attention, factorized output), FACO (factorized attention, collapsed output), CACO (both collapsed). Gradient-flow analysis shows that factorized parameterizations induce a state-dependent rescaling of gradient updates in the corresponding collapsed parameter space, so factorized and collapsed models follow different optimization trajectories even when they reach similar losses. Under orthogonality and symmetry assumptions, closed-form population gradient-flow dynamics are derived: the output-value scalar grows as log(t), while the query-key scalar grows as log^2(t), with constants controlled by the learning-rate ratio η_QK / η_OV. Given a larger source MLLM and a smaller target MLLM from the same family, the model is partitioned into three components: vision encoder (ViT), cross-modal projector, and LLM. An injection space E=(C,P) specifies which components are selected and whether the injection is restricted to linear-layer parameters or extended to all alignable parameters. Because source and target differ in hidden size, attention-head count, and LLM depth, the paper defines a heterogeneous alignment operator A_pi that projects source parameters into the target parameter space. The layer mapping pi controls how source LLM layers are matched to target layers.

After obtaining the projected source direction Δ, the target is perturbed without training: Θ_F = Θ_T + αΔ. The point is not to perform aggressive fusion, but to scan α, parameter scope, component choice, and layer mapping, and then observe capability-specific local changes.

The theoretical analysis uses a second-order expansion around the target parameters. For a capability-specific loss L_c(Θ), the local gain is approximated by a first-order term αA_c minus a second-order term proportional to α^2 I_c, where A_c = -∇L_c(Θ_T)^TΔ is the transfer signal and I_c = Δ^T H_c Δ is the curvature response. This decomposition is the conceptual center of the paper. It explains why the same injected direction can help reasoning but barely affect perception, and why gains vanish once α becomes too large: the first-order alignment may be positive, but structural interference grows quadratically.

Unlike standard model merging, the method is not trying to build the best merged model, and it does not rely on learned mappings. It is a controlled probe for transferability. One notable design choice is the comparison between linear-only and full-parameter injection. The results suggest that transferable knowledge is more concentrated in linear transformations than in the full set of alignable weights, which is an interesting clue about where portable computation lives in MLLMs.1b:T6ad,The experiments use four Qwen3-VL cross-scale model pairs and evaluate them on twelve benchmarks. The most informative results are capability-wise rather than aggregate.

First, gains are concentrated in reasoning, especially high-level reasoning, while perception remains close to the original target performance. This suggests that training-free cross-scale fusion is much better at transferring high-level decision or language-side computation than low-level visual competence.

Second, component-level ablations show that the LLM carries most of the high-level reasoning gains. Injecting the ViT or projector does not produce comparable positive transfer. This is consistent with the idea that visual capability is more tightly coupled to representation geometry and encoder-specific details, making it less amenable to local linear projection.

Third, restricting injection to linear-layer parameters is usually more efficient and more stable than injecting all alignable parameters. Full-parameter injection may contain more useful signal, but it also introduces substantially more structural conflict, which matches the second-order analysis.

Fourth, both layer mapping and injection ratio are highly sensitive. Effective layer mappings depend on the specific source-target pair; there is no universal best mapping. Positive selective transfer appears only in a narrow low-α regime, and performance degrades quickly as α increases, consistent with a linear benefit term competing against a quadratic interference term.

Start with the introduction and the main method figure to lock in the paper’s goal. CDPI is not proposed as a stronger fusion recipe; it is a probe for transfer mechanisms. Missing that point makes the rest look underpowered when it is actually intentionally controlled.

Then focus on the definitions of the injection space, the alignment operator, and the layer mapping. These choices determine what is being held fixed and what is being varied. Pay attention to why the paper separates ViT, projector, and LLM, and why it distinguishes linear-only from full-parameter injection.

The theory section is worth careful reading, especially the interpretation of A_c and I_c. The formula itself is simple; the value lies in the explanation. Most empirical findings in the paper are direct manifestations of that decomposition.

In the experiments, prioritize four groups of figures/tables: capability-specific gains, component-level ablations, linear vs full injection, and layer-mapping / α sweeps. If reading more deeply, the two questions worth pursuing are: why reasoning appears more locally linear and portable than perception, and why transferable directions in the LLM survive cross-scale projection better than those in the vision stack.1d:T765,The main idea is to disentangle context-length effects from order effects. For each target position, the authors construct two reveal conditions. In the intact condition, the preceding context is revealed in its original order. In the scrambled baseline, the same revealed span is permuted, preserving the word inventory and much of the topical signal while destroying sequential arrangement. A pretrained autoregressive transformer then provides conditional probabilities, or equivalently token-level surprisal, for the target span. By extending the revealed boundary farther back, they measure the incremental predictive gain contributed by each distance range. The difference between intact and scrambled gains defines the order-specific marginal benefit, and its dependence on distance d is the CPF, P(d).

Several design choices matter. They measure incremental gain rather than only comparing full versus truncated context, which localizes contribution by distance. The baseline uses permutations of the true words rather than random replacements, so lexical content and topic recurrence are controlled as much as possible while order is removed. The LLM is treated as a probe of structure in natural language, not as the object of analysis. The paper also includes controls with random-token sequences, synthetic corpora, partial order corruption, and analyses of whether the effect is concentrated in a few anchor tokens or broadly distributed.

Relative to Shannon-style context-length studies or Hilberg-type arguments, the novelty is the counterfactual baseline. Earlier work measured the total value of longer context but could not separate lexical availability from arrangement. Mutual information studies pairwise dependence, not dependence on ordered configurations. The contribution here is therefore a measurement design for sequence arrangement as its own statistical object.1e:T523,The study covers ten written and spoken corpora spanning six language families. The headline result is that CPF follows an approximate power law, P(d) ∝ d^-α, over nearly two orders of magnitude in distance, with mean α = 1.04, standard deviation 0.15, and median r^2 = 0.96. The concentration of the exponent near 1 is central to the claim that this is a reproducible law rather than a loose curve fit.

The control experiments are important. The effect largely disappears on scrambled or random-token controls and on synthetic corpora, which argues against the pattern being a trivial artifact of the probe. When sequential integrity is progressively degraded, CPF weakens accordingly, supporting the interpretation that it is measuring order-specific signal rather than simple lexical recurrence. The paper also reports that the contribution is broadly distributed across prior sentences rather than being dominated by a few distant anchor tokens.

The experimental strength is not a model-size sweep or benchmark gains. It comes from replication across corpora and from careful controls that rule out obvious confounds.
