18:T430,The authors introduce GridUMM, a small controlled environment that keeps the structural ingredients of UMM training: a fully shared decoder-only Transformer trunk, two asymmetric objectives, an order-of-magnitude token-budget imbalance, and a contrast between single-token classification and autoregressive generation. The environment allows exact measurement of both task accuracies and an exact Pareto frontier, avoiding benchmark noise. The audit combines observational correlations, bootstrap intervals, partial correlations controlling for training progress, test–retest reliability ceilings, and a causal dose–response intervention. The intervention projects the generative gradient with a controlled strength α, monotonically changing the conflict metric while keeping the data, schedule, and parameters fixed. The audited metrics include global and layerwise gradient cosines, conflict rate, removed energy, and norm ratio. The paper also compares these with functional interference measures, which operate closer to model behavior than raw gradient geometry.19:T4fa,The framework defines a world as a pair of a reusable mechanism and a task rule. The learner first trains on task-independent source records, then adapts the same parameters using terminal correctness rewards, and is evaluated on held-out prompts. In the Sequential family, the mechanism is a transition table θ and the task rule is an initial operation η. The authors characterize a family of transformations that preserve all training rewards while flipping held-out answers for half of the worlds. Local source observations break this ambiguity by revealing transitions before their effects cancel in the reward data. In the Contextual Memory family, the mechanism is a retrieval map and the rule is a context–cue decision rule. The theory shows that finite source coverage leaves an exact error floor on unseen contexts, and additional terminal rewards cannot remove it. The optimization analysis then constructs finite sampled-Adam paths from specified random initializations. Source prediction trains transition execution or attention-based retrieval in the same parameters; reward adaptation then learns the task binding. This is stronger than a representation-existence argument because it specifies how gradient-based training can realize the division of labor.1a:T5ce,The theoretical results provide explicit guarantees. In the Sequential setting, Theorem 4.1 identifies the reward-indistinguishable mechanism family, with half of the alternatives reversing every held-out answer. Theorem 4.2 gives a sampled-Adam path in which source prediction teaches execution on generated prefixes, and two REINFORCE updates using 2B_R terminal verifications select the initial operation, reaching at least 7/8 held-out success. In Contextual Memory, Theorem 5.1 gives the exact error floor induced by unobserved contexts. Theorem 5.2 shows that source Adam can acquire retrieval and reward Adam can learn an input-dependent rule, reaching at least 19/20 success; freezing randomly initialized query/key attention under the same schedule keeps success at most 1/64 above chance. The Qwen2.5 experiments test the same decomposition on real checkpoints. Across eight matched worlds, correct-source preparation followed by Sequential action-target reward training reaches 82.61% success, compared with 44.15% for a private-random source control. For memory tasks, replay preserves retrieval, and an independent eight-world confirmation reaches 75.32% task success versus 49.86% with alternative retrieval. A single-world comparison shows rapid reward learning even when task success is zero at reward entry, provided the correct source preparation was used. GSM8K and HotpotQA are used to separate entry accuracy, reward-stage gains, and final accuracy on public tasks.1b:T4aa,Its value for pretraining-oriented research is mainly conceptual and mechanistic. The paper offers a concrete hypothesis: some of CoT’s gains come from coupling targeted retrieval with compact internal state representations, not just from spending more inference compute. That matters in several ways. First, it suggests that the difference between reasoning and non-reasoning models may lie in how internal states are organized over time, not only in the final decoding policy. Second, it points to a possible training implication: if one wants stronger long-context reasoning during pretraining or mid-training, it may help to encourage sequential enumeration, explicit state updates, and separable progress representations, rather than only scaling context length or adding more long documents. Third, it connects three threads that are often studied separately—long-context retrieval, representation geometry, and CoT generation. This makes it relevant to latent reasoning, scratchpad training, and the question of whether reasoning traces are useful training targets. The contribution is better viewed as mechanistic analysis plus controlled reproduction, not as a new training recipe.1c:T925,The paper operates at three levels. First, it establishes a behavioral contrast. Across 12 model comparison groups, it compares Thinking and Non-thinking modes on long-context counting while varying needle count and context length. The important move here is that CoT traces are treated not just as text, but as observable retrieval trajectories: each enumerated item in the trace can be aligned with a record in the prompt.

Second, it performs a representation-level and attention-level analysis. The authors map prompt needles and CoT trace items into hidden-state space and define a running index k corresponding to how many records have been encountered or enumerated so far. Hidden states associated with this progression are called counter states. They then analyze how these states evolve and how the answer-token representation corresponds to the final count N. This leads to a contrast between two mechanisms. Non-thinking models rely on broad retrieval: at the answer position they attend broadly to many needles, but the resulting representations are noisy and less separable. Thinking models rely on targeted retrieval: through step-by-step enumeration in the CoT trace, the model successively retrieves relevant needles while updating a more compact and separable representation of progress. “Compact” here is not rhetorical; it refers to a geometry in which count states are more cleanly organized and easier to distinguish.

Third, the paper adds causal and synthetic evidence. It does not stop at visualization or correlation. It performs interventions to test whether evolving counter states causally influence subsequent retrieval and final count prediction. It also trains small transformers from scratch under autoregressive objectives with and without CoT traces, asking whether the two mechanisms can be reproduced under controlled training conditions. This is an important design choice because it shifts the claim from “large models happen to behave this way” to “different training targets can induce different internal algorithms.”

Methodologically, the paper makes a deliberate trade-off: it chooses a low-semantic-ambiguity task instead of an open-ended reasoning benchmark. That makes the mechanism cleaner to isolate, because failures are less likely to be confounded by semantic misunderstanding.1d:T653,The abstract does not provide the full numerical tables, but the structure of the paper makes the main empirical findings clear. First, across 12 model comparison groups, Thinking mode consistently outperforms Non-thinking on long-context counting, with the gap becoming larger as the number of needles increases. This suggests that CoT helps most when the task requires sustained state tracking rather than trivial counting.

Second, attention and retrieval-path analyses show a qualitative difference in how information is gathered. Non-thinking models tend to aggregate broadly from many prompt needles at the answer token. Thinking models instead retrieve records progressively during the enumeration trace, making retrieval more focused and temporally structured.

Third, representation-geometry analyses show that Thinking models form more compact and separable counter states. That is, the model appears to maintain an internal progress variable that evolves with enumeration, rather than trying to aggregate everything only at the end. Fourth, the small-transformer training experiments reproduce the same contrast: training with CoT traces induces mechanisms resembling targeted retrieval, while training without CoT more often yields broad retrieval.

For research use, the most important results are not the benchmark deltas themselves, but the decomposition of CoT gains into three measurable components: retrieval style, state geometry, and causal influence. The attacker is allowed to control only the bytes of one tool result. They cannot modify the system prompt, user task, tool schema, or serving configuration. The defender controls only the tokenizer call, and therefore the mapping from bytes to token ids. Around this setup, the paper defines three key conditions.

Reserved keeps the forged chat-template markers as native reserved token ids. Split keeps the bytes identical and therefore the rendered text identical, but forces those same marker strings to be encoded as ordinary subwords rather than reserved ids. This is the core fixed-bytes contrast: same bytes, different ids. Because splitting reserved markers also increases token count, the paper adds Matched, which preserves the reserved ids but injects the same number of extra tokens elsewhere in ordinary text. Split and Matched therefore share bytes and token count, differing only in whether the forged markers retain their reserved representation. The attack-success-rate difference between them is defined as the identity gap.

This design is stronger than prior work because it does not rewrite the marker text and does not rely on crude character-level splitting. It changes only token identity while holding the visible string fixed. That directly answers whether the authority comes from text or representation.

The paper then goes beyond behavioral measurement. It analyzes the embedding layer and shows that the average of the marker's subword embeddings does not substitute for the reserved token vector.
