9:T7c5,Agon trains two policies head-to-head with competitive GRPO. On each problem, one policy acts as a drafter and produces a worked solution; the other acts as a challenger, reads the opponent’s draft, and then attempts the same problem. Reward depends not only on correctness but also on out-solving the rival. Roles alternate across training steps so both policies learn both drafting and challenging.

The paper argues that two ingredients are necessary. First, there must be information exchange: the challenger needs access to the opponent’s reasoning, otherwise the setup cannot say anything about whether the trace itself is useful or exploitable. Second, the interaction must be competitive rather than cooperative. Pure cooperation or agreement-seeking tends to regress toward consensus and can dilute quality when the two models differ in strength. Competitive pressure instead rewards reasoning that remains strong even when exposed to a peer.

To isolate these effects, the paper uses a 2×2 design over competition versus cooperation and exchange versus no exchange. One cell, competition without exchange, is argued to be inert under group-relative normalization, so the meaningful comparison is adversarial exchange versus cooperative exchange. That analytical point matters because it prevents over-interpreting a control that is structurally unable to carry the intended signal.

Implementation is deliberately lightweight. Rather than training two full models, the authors instantiate the pair as two LoRA-style adapters on top of one frozen base, with roughly 2% extra memory overhead instead of 2× model cost. Different initialization and separate update streams are used to keep the two policies divergent. The communication channel is plain text in this version; latent-space exchange is mentioned as a future direction. Importantly, the optimization core remains a stock GRPO trainer. The novelty is in rollout structure and reward shaping, not in a new optimizer.a:T661,The experiments are run on hard math under matched generation budgets. The main findings are threefold. First, dual-model training with information exchange outperforms single-model vanilla GRPO. Second, given exchange, competitive training outperforms cooperative training, supporting the claim that adversarial pressure is more effective than mutual assistance for improving reasoning. Third, the gains are larger for smaller models, which is plausible because smaller models have lower reasoning ceilings and benefit more from an external source of signal.

A particularly useful result from the abstract is that, at the same doubled inference budget, the trained draft-and-challenge cascade nearly doubles the accuracy of an untrained Mixture-of-Agents orchestration over the same base, while the untrained orchestration itself barely improves over GRPO. That suggests the gain is not merely from adding another pass or another model at inference time; it comes from the co-training dynamics and the induced specialization under competition.

The evaluation design includes self-refinement as a control, which is important for the paper’s argument that self-auditing tends to plateau. It also includes zero-training orchestration and a budget-matched self-cascade GRPO control, helping separate “more inference structure” from “competitive joint training.” The excerpt does not expose the full parameter scales, training lengths, or all absolute scores, but it is clear that the paper tracks pass@1, reasoning length, and fairness under matched generation budgets rather than relying on a single cherry-picked endpoint.b:T5d5,Start with the Introduction, especially the framing of the “unscored trace” problem. After that, go straight to the 2×2 experiment design to understand why competition and exchange must be disentangled, and why one control cell is structurally inert.
