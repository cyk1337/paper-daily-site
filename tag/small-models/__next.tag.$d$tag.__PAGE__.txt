The method has three main components.

First, BCQ, or Binary Candidate-included Question. A question is marked hard when the student’s mean rollout accuracy is below 0.5. For such questions, the prompt is reformulated by appending two anonymized candidate answers: one correct teacher answer and one incorrect student answer, shuffled in order. The student is then asked to solve the problem again while consulting the candidates. The key point is that the student still generates its own final response. The policy gradient is applied only to the student’s generated answer, not to the teacher answer. This is the central mechanism behind “teacher in prompts, not gradients.”

Second, NCQ, or Negative Candidate-included Question. The prompt includes all wrong student responses collected for the same question, explicitly marks them as incorrect, and asks the student to answer again. This turns independent failures into a shared negative signal. In ordinary grouped rollouts, failed attempts do not see one another. NCQ is the first place where the model can condition on its own collective mistakes.

Third, a prompt replay buffer. Only hard prompts are stored, not responses. These prompts are repeatedly sampled until they “graduate,” meaning rollout accuracy reaches at least 0.5, or until they are evicted by FIFO when the buffer is full. This operationalizes the “zone of proximal development” idea: repeatedly practice questions that are currently unsolved but potentially learnable with limited guidance. Storing prompts without responses is also a deliberate choice to avoid drifting into offline imitation.

On the optimization side, the method uses GRPO with DAPO’s clip-higher and token-level policy gradient loss, plus two recipe choices highlighted from REINFORCE++: more iterations per step and batch-level advantage normalization, with zero-advantage groups excluded from the normalization statistics. The paper argues that these details matter materially in the small-model regime.
