18:T6a1,The design is intentionally simple to isolate causality. The pipeline has only two stages. The executor produces an initial answer A0. The reviewer sees the problem and A0, then emits accept/reject plus a written critique. If rejected, the executor revises once to produce A1. There is no planner stage, specifically to avoid extra confounds.

The key methodological choice is a paired design. The executor solves each problem once, and the exact same A0 is reviewed under every reviewer condition. This removes executor sampling variance from condition-to-condition comparisons. The four conditions are: no review, self-review, a cross-family mid-tier reviewer, and a weak reviewer. The executor is Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite with 52% standalone accuracy on the task set. The mid-tier reviewer is gpt-oss-20b. The weak reviewer is Llama-3.1-8B, which can solve essentially none of these problems on its own. The benchmark uses 100 numeric-answer Olympiad math problems from Omni-MATH, filtered to allow exact automatic grading with no LLM judge.

The main contribution is not a new pipeline architecture but the rejection-outcome decomposition. Instead of counting rejections or final accuracy alone, the paper tracks what each rejection actually did: fix a wrong answer, damage a correct one, fail because the executor ignored the critique, or induce a wrong correction. This decomposition exposes two common evaluation mistakes. High rejection rate does not imply useful review, and low observed damage does not imply reviewer quality if the executor simply refuses to follow bad critiques. In other words, reviewer quality and critique uptake are separate variables and must be analyzed separately.19:T69e,The headline result is straightforward. The fixed executor solves 52 out of 100 Olympiad math problems correctly. Replacing no-review with a cross-family mid-tier reviewer raises final accuracy to 64%, a gain of 12 percentage points with p=0.0005, and with zero damaged correct answers. This shows that a reviewer does not need full task-solving ability to create net value if it can reject the right cases.

Self-review is the more revealing condition. It achieves the highest detection rate, yet yields no significant accuracy gain. The paper reports that self-review rejects about 2.1 times as often as the effective mid-tier reviewer, but repairs only about one-third as many answers. It also falsely rejects 35% of the executor’s correct answers. The crucial detail is what happens after those false rejections: whenever the executor actually follows the critique, the revised answer becomes wrong. Cases that appear harmless are harmless only because the executor ignored the critique. This is the empirical basis for the paper’s claim that apparent self-review safety is often revision inertia in disguise.

At the low end, the weak reviewer is effectively inert. It changes none of the 100 final answers while roughly doubling token cost. That gives a practical capability floor: below some threshold, review neither repairs errors nor triggers useful revisions, and only adds overhead.

There is also an implementation detail worth noting. An initial run revealed truncation in the mid-tier reviewer’s outputs, so token limits were adjusted and documented. That matters because reviewer usefulness depends on complete critiques; truncation can directly distort rejection quality.1a:T4a0,For a quick read, focus on the Introduction and Method to understand the paired design and the rejection-outcome decomposition. For a closer read, pay attention to three things. First, how ignored critiques are defined and counted; this is central to the argument that self-review risk is underestimated. Second, why the paper uses a cross-family reviewer rather than a same-family smaller model; the goal is to separate capability effects from model-identity effects as much as possible. Third, inspect the dataset filtering and exact-grading protocol, because the conclusions are strongest for numerically graded math tasks and should not be casually generalized to open-ended writing or code review.

- Report-only identification barrier: different latent ancestry structures can induce the same observable report profile (including similarity/diversity patterns) yet require different Bayes-optimal posterior updates. Therefore, no aggregator that only sees report content can be generally correct.
