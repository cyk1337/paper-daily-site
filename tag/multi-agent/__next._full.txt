18:T557,The work extends the Gemini-based Co-Scientist system into an execution-grounded multi-agent framework spanning hypothesis generation, experiment planning, tool use, result interpretation, and manuscript drafting. The key idea is not a new foundation model, but adaptive orchestration across domains with different verification costs and safety constraints. In materials science, the system interfaces with a semi-automated CVD setup, proposes candidate recipes under lab constraints, and relies on experts to select and execute promising options. In another materials setting, Gemini 3 Deep Think is used for fast inference and direct hardware control to drive single-attempt growth of 2D semiconductors. In biology, the system works with experts who iteratively refine the task framing, while the model predicts emergent swarming phenotypes of engineered E. coli from sparse imaging data and the predictions are checked against wet-lab measurements. In computer science, the system operates autonomously from a high-level research directive to discover an inference-time scaling architecture and evaluate it on medical benchmarks. The unifying design choice is selective autonomy: the system does not insist on full automation, but allocates decision authority between AI and humans based on execution risk, observability, and the availability of reliable feedback.19:T52e,The evidence is presented mainly as real-world case studies rather than a single standardized benchmark. In materials science, Co-Scientist proposes a non-hazardous precursor route for Ti3C2Tx MXene growth; after expert optimization and physical execution, the resulting structures show characteristics similar to Ti3C2Tx MXene, although the paper explicitly notes that further experiments are needed to confirm the atomic structure. In a second materials setup, the system uses Gemini 3 Deep Think for rapid reasoning and direct hardware control, achieving successful single-attempt growth of monolayer MoS2, MoSe2, and WS2 while adapting to lab constraints within minutes. In biology, the system predicts swarming phenotypes across IPTG concentration gradients and is quantitatively validated against unpublished wet-lab morphology measurements, with the claimed benefit of reducing combinatorial screening. In computer science, the system autonomously discovers an inference-time scaling architecture that outperforms six frontier models on HealthBench Hard and Professional and reduces potential clinical harm in blinded physician evaluation, though the excerpt does not provide the full numbers. Overall, the experimental contribution is breadth of deployment and grounding, not tightly controlled algorithmic comparison.1a:T788,The paper has three main components. First, it formalises the collaboration tax using team-decentralisation and cooperative-game language. The tax is the success-rate gap between a full-information solo policy and a split-information paired policy. Two propositions characterise when the quantity is positive and connect it to a violation of superadditivity in the underlying cooperative game. The theory is lightweight, but it gives the empirical phenomenon a precise reference point.

Second, the authors build a 32-task suite grouped into Spatial, Relational, and CSP families. These families are chosen to span different sources of grounding friction: reference-frame alignment, lexical/category alignment, and indexing/ordinal alignment. Each task is designed so that a single model can solve it when given the full view, after which the information is partitioned across two agents. This makes the benchmark much cleaner than many agent evaluations that mix in tool use, planning depth, or environment interaction.

Third, the paper develops a mechanistic account of failure. It identifies a four-stage conversational cascade: grounding, querying, integration, and re-derivation. Grounding failures occur when agents introduce facts supported by neither private view. Querying failures occur when they do not ask the partner for missing information. Integration failures occur when they fail to combine both views before committing. Re-derivation failures occur when the receiver accepts an answer without recomputing it. The authors then show that conversation-shape features predict the tax well: model capability largely sets the intercept, while conversation structure sets the slope. Finally, they test a simple prompt-only intervention that adds one clause targeting each stage. The simplicity matters because it supports the claim that the dominant issue is coordination process, not missing base reasoning ability.1b:T61c,The evaluation covers 11 models from 7 providers, with solo, homogeneous-pair, heterogeneous-pair, and slot-swapped heterogeneous settings. Even from the provided full text excerpt, several findings are clear. First, the collaboration tax is systematic rather than anecdotal. It exhibits two no-exception orderings: one over task categories and one over model capability. Harder grounding-friction categories consistently incur larger tax, and stronger models show monotonically smaller tax. Second, the main failure mode is not reasoning deficit. Conversation analysis indicates that many failures happen before the relevant information is properly requested or after it is received but not integrated or checked. Third, in heterogeneous pairs the stronger model bears the asymmetry of the loss, suggesting that weaker partners often drag the interaction into a poorer coordination regime rather than contributing complementary value. Fourth, the prompt-level intervention recovers a substantial fraction of the tax, and leave-one-out ablations show that the dominant bottleneck differs by task family. That is more informative than a generic prompt-length effect because it points to task-structure-specific coordination failures.

The experimental design is strong in one respect: the solo-tractable construction makes causal interpretation cleaner. The limitations are also clear: the suite is still relatively small, focused on two-agent natural-language coordination, and does not cover richer settings with tools, memory, or learned communication protocols.1c:T455,A matched evaluation benchmark of 4,181 competition-level math problems is constructed, with every problem run on an identical solver stack (gpt-oss-120b) across four protocols: direct solve, iterative self-correction, planner-executor-reviewer multi-role collaboration, and multi-agent broadcast deliberation. Paired solve rate and token cost outcomes are collected for every problem-protocol pair, and a retrospective cost-ordered oracle is built as a diagnostic upper bound for routing performance. Performance is compared across three router families: static fixed policies, logistic regression routers trained on metadata and TF-IDF n-gram features, and frozen LLM routers, with metrics including solve rate, average token cost, excess tokens relative to the oracle, and rates of under- and over-escalation. The main grid is {D0, D1, D2} × P0 × 100 task variants across two models, with additional D0 P0/P1 policy-access tests. D0 is a single-loop baseline with no component boundary; D1 and D2 add decomposition. The benchmark contains 100 variants generated from 5 seed scenarios by varying personas, jurisdictions, amounts, and ownership percentages, including data-driven flips such as 26% versus 24% ownership. The main finding is boundary-sensitive attenuation. D0 has 0% fact attenuation for both models. On Qwen2.5-32B, D1 drops 56% of discovered facts and D2 drops 85%. On gpt-4.1-mini, the rates are much lower, 3% and 6%, but the ordering remains the same. Overall governed success is 8/596, so the benchmark is more informative as a failure-mechanism probe than as a simple pass-rate leaderboard.1e:T45d,The study simulates about 10,000 language-model agent communities across models, questions, communication graphs, and episodes. Empirically, group states cluster into indifference, polarization, and consensus, while individual trajectories include frozen opinions, one-time switches, reversals, and repeated oscillations. Interaction tends to increase conviction and move communities toward more ordered states. On objective math questions, communication improves average collective accuracy: initially wrong majorities switch to the correct answer more often than initially correct majorities become wrong. On subjective political statements, interaction can induce directional ideological drift. The fitted model predicts held-out questions better than standard baselines, generalizes from random graphs to unseen network families, and approximately reproduces population-level outcome distributions. Parameter analysis indicates a low-temperature ordered regime, stronger concordant than discordant interactions, and greater fitted influence from agents currently holding the correct answer on objective questions.1f:T622,The work has two components. The first is Social Gym, a unified environment for multi-agent social games. Its design follows two principles: every game must have an algorithmically verifiable outcome such as win/loss, score, or survival; and the game suite must cover multiple facets of social intelligence rather than a single domain. System-wise, it extends the SOTOPIA interaction loop to arbitrary N-agent settings, uses a finite-state-machine engine for complex phase transitions, and introduces a visibility layer with public, team-private, and private channels. This makes hidden-state inference, deception, coalition reasoning, and Theory-of-Mind explicit parts of the environment. The 21 games are organized by information structure and communication mode, and a unified Elo tournament produces both per-game and aggregate rankings.

The second component is SPaRTan, a training-free self-improvement loop. A model first plays self-play games, then reads its own trajectories and writes a transferable strategic playbook, and finally injects that playbook into the system prompt for future games.
