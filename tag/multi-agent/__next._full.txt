18:T86b,方法建立在 Sparta Alignment 式的多模型协同框架上，但把 instruction sampling 提升为 leader-follower 的 Stackelberg game。leader 不是生成答案的模型，而是一个控制 instruction 分布的 bandit；followers 才是参与 duel 并通过偏好信号更新的 LLM 池。每轮训练中，leader 从 instruction pool 上给出采样分布，随后进行 D 次 duel。每次 duel 选一条 instruction，两名模型作答，其余模型做 peer judges，在给定分数区间内对回答打分，再聚合成 preference pairs 用于 RL fine-tuning。

核心创新在 leader 的更新规则。作者使用 EXP3，而不是 UCB 或简单 moving-average reweighting，原因是 reward 非平稳：同一条 instruction 的价值会随着模型能力变化而改变，而且不同 instruction 的反馈噪声较大。EXP3 适合 adversarial / non-stationary bandit 场景，并且天然支持只观察被采样臂的 bandit feedback。为了避免 leader 只追逐“难题”或只追逐“分歧大但无意义的题”，论文设计了 composite reward，把 instruction difficulty 与 duel discriminability 结合起来。前者刻画题目是否仍具挑战性，后者刻画该题是否能稳定地产生可学习的胜负差异。只有同时具备一定难度、又能区分模型优劣的 instruction，才会被持续加权。

另一个关键部件是 reputation 机制。每个模型维护历史 duel 表现得到的 reputation，未参赛模型作为 judge 时，其投票按 reputation 加权；参赛对手的匹配也按 reputation 接近原则进行，避免强弱差距过大导致 duel 失去信息量。这一点延续了 Sparta 的思想，但在这里与 adaptive instruction selection 形成耦合：题目和对手都被调到“刚好能产生学习信号”的区域。方法上值得注意的设计细节还有两点。其一，每轮并不遍历整个 instruction pool，而是只按 leader 分布抽样固定预算的 duel，这不是近似实现，而是课程学习本身。其二，采样分布保留非零 floor probability，避免某些 instruction 被永久遗忘，从而保留探索能力。19:T894,The framework is cast as a Stackelberg game. A leader chooses a distribution over the instruction pool, and the follower models then duel on sampled instructions and update from the resulting preference data. Concretely, at each training iteration the leader defines a sampling distribution over instructions, and D duels are drawn from that distribution with replacement. In each duel, two models answer the selected instruction, while the remaining models act as peer judges and score the responses. These scores are aggregated into preference pairs for RL fine-tuning.

The central technical choice is to implement the leader with EXP3 rather than a simpler heuristic or a stationary bandit. That choice is motivated by non-stationary rewards: the usefulness of an instruction changes as the model pool improves, and only sampled instructions reveal feedback. The reward is composite, combining instruction difficulty with duel discriminability. This is important. Difficulty alone would overemphasize questions that are too hard to learn from; discriminability alone would overemphasize shallow questions that separate models but no longer push the frontier. The leader therefore shifts probability mass toward instructions that are both challenging and still capable of producing informative preference signal.

A second component is the reputation mechanism inherited and extended from prior collaborative alignment work. Each model maintains a reputation score based on past duel outcomes. When a model serves as a judge, its vote is weighted by reputation. When duel participants are selected, models are matched by reputation proximity so that contests remain competitive. This interacts naturally with adaptive instruction selection: both the task and the opponent are kept near the current learning frontier. Two implementation details are also worth noting. First, the method intentionally does not sweep the full instruction pool every iteration; sparse sampling under a fixed duel budget is the curriculum mechanism, not an approximation artifact. Second, the leader keeps a nonzero probability floor for every instruction, preserving exploration and preventing permanent exclusion.1a:T6df,The evaluation uses three heterogeneous model pools—specialized expert models, academic research models, and general-purpose models—across 12 benchmarks spanning scientific discovery, reasoning, coding, instruction following, and knowledge tasks. The main result is consistent: Stackelberg Alignment achieves the best macro-average in all three pools. The reported gains reach up to 7.4% over other training-time collaborative algorithms and 12–25% over the best static inference-time baseline.

The more informative part is the ablation evidence. The paper shows that the gains are not merely from adding another control module, but from adaptive curriculum itself. Replacing the learned leader with uniform instruction sampling leads to systematic degradation. Using only difficulty or only discriminability in the reward is also weaker than the composite reward, which supports the paper’s claim that informative instructions must be both learnable and differentiating. Reputation-weighted judging and reputation-based opponent matching contribute additional stability by reducing noisy preference labels and trivial mismatches. The paper also analyzes how the instruction distribution moves during training: earlier iterations focus more on medium-difficulty, high-disagreement instructions, while later iterations shift toward previously too-hard instructions that become learnable as the pool improves. That trajectory is consistent with the proposed moving-disagreement-frontier hypothesis.

For reading the results, the right unit of comparison is duel budget rather than epochs over the instruction set. The method is explicitly budgeted by sampled duels per iteration, so sample efficiency should be interpreted under equal interaction cost.1b:T431,Start with the introduction and Figure 1 to understand the actual question: not how models judge each other, but how the system decides which instructions are worth spending duel budget on today. Then read the method section carefully around three pieces: the composite reward for the leader, the EXP3 update, and how reputation enters both judge weighting and opponent matching. Those parts determine whether the method is a real online curriculum or just heuristic reweighting.

In the experiments, focus on three things: the gap to uniform/static baselines, the ablations removing adaptive sampling or simplifying the reward, and the plots showing how instruction mass shifts over training. For a skim-level read, 30–40 minutes is enough. If reading more deeply, two follow-up questions are worth keeping in mind: whether the same reward design could transfer to pretraining data mixture scheduling, and whether EXP3 will become too high-variance when the instruction pool is very large, in which case contextual bandits or learned utility models may be more scalable.1c:T85b,The paper formulates recursive social improvement as an RL problem over a population of N agents. Agent i faces its own task distribution D_i and is rewarded only on its own tasks. Each round has two decisions. First, the agent chooses a learning action: search privately, observe a peer, or abstain. Second, it chooses a task action by executing a skill from its current library. A skill can be a simple option in a controlled environment or a full skill file in the LLM setting.

A central design choice is token accounting. Search, observation, and acting all draw from the same budget, and the cost of discovering a copied skill is counted as part of the total social-learning cost. This avoids the common mistake of treating copied knowledge as free.

The experiments proceed in two stages. In the controlled stage, options have known value structure, allowing the authors to test whether established social-learning algorithms can in principle yield an advantage. In the LLM stage, agents write, revise, and exchange skill files for instruction-following tasks. These skill files are inference-time textual procedures rather than weight updates. Policies are implemented by fixed-weight LLMs learning in context: the model decides whether to revise its own skill, copy a peer’s skill, or act.

The paper also makes an important distinction between two notions of success. Efficient social learning means better performance per token. Effective social learning means higher final performance than independent learners at the same total cost. This distinction is one of the strongest parts of the paper because many multi-agent results show speed or cost gains without showing superiority under matched compute.

The authors further test simple interventions such as reserving tokens for acting and requiring some private search. These do not resolve the core issue. Another notable analysis tracks skill lineage over time. Copied skills are often revised again, so the process is not mere imitation. But most final skills end up tracing back to only one or two early revisions, indicating rapid lineage collapse and path dependence.1d:T6d7,The empirical picture is consistent and informative. In the controlled environment, a hand-designed social-learning policy does achieve a social advantage. That shows the setting itself does not rule out social learning. The failure appears when LLMs are used as the policy controllers.

Across tested LLMs, populations with peer observation are 30% to 52% less efficient than solo populations of the same model. In other words, useful social information exists, but the models do not manage it well enough; they reduce exploration too aggressively.

In the skill-file experiments, peer access changes learning speed and cost but not the matched-cost conclusion. With peer access, GLM-5.3-Flash achieves on average 1.3 percentage points higher held-out accuracy over the course of learning. GPT-OSS-120B uses about one-third fewer learning tokens. These are real benefits, but they are benefits in trajectory shape or efficiency, not in final performance at equal token cost. When learning tokens are matched, neither model shows a clear advantage over independent learners.

The lineage analysis is especially useful. Skills are copied and then revised again, so there is genuine recursive improvement. But by the end, most agents’ skills descend from only one or two of the population’s earliest revisions. This explains the main negative result: copied options often beat what an agent found alone at that moment, yet population-level exploration collapses, so the group does not surpass independent search overall.

The paper is careful not to oversell a benchmark win. Its strongest evidence comes from matched-token comparisons that separate “social learning looks smarter” from “social learning is actually better under equal cost.”1e:T4bf,A good reading order is: formal problem setup, controlled environment results, LLM skill-file experiments, then lineage analysis. A world is decomposed into heterogeneous subsystems such as transportation, construction, resources, and social relations.
