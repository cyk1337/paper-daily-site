18:T702,The method has two layers. The first is the diagnostic metric, Contextual Preference Gain (CPG). For the same response pair, the model is evaluated with and without additional helpful context. CPG measures how much the preference margin increases once that context is added. The margin follows the usual DPO or Bradley-Terry view, based on the relative reward or log-probability gap between preferred and dispreferred responses. If a model truly leverages context, the correct response should become more strongly preferred when grounding information is available, so CPG should be positive and meaningfully large. Empirically, the authors show that higher CPG is strongly associated with lower hallucination, while standard DPO and several variants produce CPG distributions concentrated near zero.

The second layer is C²-DPO. On top of the original preference objective, the authors add a calibration term that explicitly rewards the preference margin under enriched context being larger than the margin without that context. This is the key difference from vanilla DPO. The change is not about a new reward parameterization; it is about writing context sensitivity directly into the optimization target. The same idea is also attached to SimPO and RDPO, which suggests that the proposal is a fairly general calibration add-on rather than a method tied to one specific DPO implementation.

Mechanistically, the method is well matched to settings where the preferred answer is only clearly preferred once grounding evidence is present. If chosen and rejected responses are already easy to separate without extra context, the gain may be limited. But for visual grounding, factual evidence, and other evidence-conditioned preference tasks, the objective is well aligned with the failure mode.19:T5aa,The experimental story is straightforward. First, the paper validates CPG as a diagnostic: models with higher CPG consistently show lower hallucination rates. That gives the proposed optimization direction a credible empirical basis. Second, C²-DPO reduces hallucination on multimodal benchmarks by a clear margin. The headline numbers reported in the paper are a 36% reduction in response-level hallucination rate and a 60% reduction in mention-level hallucination rate on Object HalBench, with consistent gains on HallusionBench as well. Third, the paper checks whether this comes at the cost of general multimodal ability and reports that performance on ScienceQA, MM-Vet, and TextVQA is preserved.

Two additional results are worth noting. The calibration idea transfers to SimPO and RDPO, which supports the claim that the issue is objective-level rather than implementation-specific. The authors also test a text-only setting and find improved factual alignment there too, suggesting that context blindness is a broader weakness of DPO-style training, not only a multimodal problem.
