19:T625,CorVer computes a sentence-level reward from Wikipedia co-occurrence statistics. The pipeline is simple by design. For each generated sentence, a 0.5B triplet extractor, QuCo-extractor-0.5B, extracts a (head, relation, tail) triple. The method keeps the first valid triple, discards the relation, and uses only the subject and object entities. Each entity is reduced to its content words to absorb surface-form variation. These words are then submitted as an AND query to an Infini-gram-backed Wikipedia index, which returns a bounded-window co-occurrence count over token positions within a 1,000-token passage window.

That count is not used directly. It is mapped through a piecewise-constant reward function with thresholds and bounded reward levels, so the co-occurrence signal shapes sentence-level credit without overwhelming the response-level reward. The model output follows a think/answer template. Sentences from both blocks are aligned back to tokens through a token-to-sentence mapping, allowing sentence rewards to be converted into token-level returns. This gives process supervision with opposite gradients for different sentences in the same completion.

The final return combines three channels, denoted in the paper by λ_f, λ_j, and λ_c: response-level rewards plus the corpus-grounded sentence reward. The key distinction from FSPO, KnowRL, or FaithRL is not the existence of process supervision, but the source of supervision. CorVer replaces neural verification with corpus-grounded statistics, changing both the cost profile and the failure mode.1a:T4c6,The evaluation spans 6 models, 5 factual QA benchmarks, and 30 model-benchmark cells, with model sizes from 3B to 14B. The paper reports consistent gains across all 30 cells. Under feasible baseline configurations, CorVer outperforms four neural-verifier baselines in 18 of 20 comparison cells. Efficiency is another major result: reward computation is reported to be 4.8x to 8.4x faster than the baselines, which matters directly for RL rollout throughput.

A key supporting experiment is the annotation study showing that sentence-level factual correctness increases monotonically with co-occurrence count. Figure 5 is central: it validates the proxy assumption behind the reward. The paper also includes sensitivity analysis for the thresholded reward mapping, suggesting the method is not overly fragile to the exact discretization. The authors further argue that the approach is especially helpful for rare-entity facts because corpus lookup does not share the same parametric memory limitations as neural verifiers.

The provided text does not expose every absolute score, benchmark-specific number, or all training hyperparameters, but the experimental setup is clearly broader than a single-model proof of concept.1b:T47e,If reading selectively, focus on four parts. First, the introduction’s formulation of the reward-design dilemma for factual QA, especially the rare-entity argument. Second, the method section describing triplet extraction, content-word reduction, Infini-gram lookup, the piecewise reward map, and token-to-sentence alignment. Third, Figure 5 and the associated annotation analysis, because the whole method depends on co-occurrence count being a useful proxy for factual correctness. Fourth, the main comparison tables against FSPO, KnowRL, and FaithRL, especially the speed numbers.

For a closer read, examine three possible weak points. One is where co-occurrence may fail as a factuality proxy: negation, temporal updates, compositional claims, or alias-heavy entities. Another is extractor error, especially for complex or multi-hop sentences. The third is the decision to keep only the first valid triplet, which saves cost but may miss the sentence’s main factual content.
