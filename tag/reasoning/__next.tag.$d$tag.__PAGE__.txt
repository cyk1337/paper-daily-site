(1) Trajectory level: Value-Variance Adaptation. A value model estimates token-level V(s_t) along each generated trajectory. The variance of these values (or a closely related advantage-variance proxy) is used as an intrinsic randomness / exploration indicator: high variance implies more exploratory, low variance implies rigid generation. The paper sketches a bound showing the policy-gradient norm can be upper-bounded by a term involving advantage variance under a bounded ∥∇ log π∥ assumption, motivating variance-aware control of update magnitude. Based on this, CVPO modifies advantage computation by scaling/reweighting trajectories according to their value-variance, with different handling for different reward types (e.g., sparse correctness vs denser rewards), aiming to extract more informative gradients from otherwise homogeneous outcomes.
(2) Prompt level: Dynamic Curriculum Learning. It maintains a moving estimate of prompt difficulty and dynamically reweights/samples prompts to counter difficulty drift, allocating more training budget to prompts that remain challenging at the current stage.
Compared to GRPO, CVPO is explicitly value-based and goes beyond group reward averaging by injecting a variance signal into advantage shaping. Compared to VAPO, it adds an uncertainty-aware (variance-aware) advantage adjustment plus an explicit curriculum mechanism.7:T481,The framework treats per-problem failure probability of the policy as a random variable over sampled problem instances. By the Hausdorff moment theorem, a distribution supported on [0,1] is uniquely determined by its full sequence of moments, so single-moment objectives capture only partial information about policy behavior. Multi-Moment Policy Optimization (MMPO) jointly minimizes multiple moments of the failure distribution, an objective that maps operationally to minimizing the expected truncated number of rollouts needed to obtain a first correct response, naturally balancing average performance and attention to high-failure hard problems. A generalized moment transformation framework further unifies a broader family of policy optimization objectives—including MaxRL, which is interpreted as optimizing moments of a transformed rather than raw failure distribution—and proves that this family of objectives is strictly Schur-convex, meaning optimization explicitly pushes for more balanced success rates across problems. Additional analysis shows that successful steering leaves the overall rate of cue use roughly unchanged while reducing hidden cue use.9:T620,The method, LATCH, has two components. The first is CVC, Confidence-Verified Commit, which decides when the whole sequence may stop. Instead of monitoring a fixed region or using schedule-dependent heuristics, CVC re-extracts and relocates the current candidate answer span at every denoising step. It then requires both high confidence on that span and sustained argmax identity stability across multiple steps. Only when the candidate answer itself has remained stable does the decoder allow a global fill-and-stop. The key change is that the stopping rule verifies the object of interest, the answer candidate, rather than a proxy statistic.

The second component is BWEC, Block-Wise Early Commit, which decides where local commitments may happen during decoding. The authors argue that non-final blocks usually contain intermediate reasoning text, so they can be handled with a cheaper local confidence rule. BWEC therefore applies only to non-final blocks. The final block, and any global termination decision, remains under CVC. This separation is meant to preserve the speed benefits of adaptive sampling while preventing local confidence signals from prematurely freezing the answer region.

A central analytical premise is that candidate stabilization is highly task-dependent. The paper measures stabilization timing with metrics such as s0.9, the decoding progress by which 90% of trajectories have stabilized, and finds a very wide range across tasks. That observation motivates abandoning progress-only early exit in favor of candidate-aware verification.a:T5b0,The evaluation covers 11 tasks and two model families, with LLaDA-8B-Instruct used as the reference decoding backbone and transfer tested to Dream. With one frozen CVC threshold tuple and one fixed BWEC threshold, without per-task or per-model retuning, LATCH achieves 9.3–17.8× speedup on short-answer tasks and 2.0–3.3× on long-reasoning tasks, while staying within a 2.0-point accuracy tolerance relative to full decoding on every task and both model families.

The failure analysis is more informative than the headline speedups. Under a matched free-form zero-shot protocol, Prophet and SchED exceed the 2-point tolerance on all five long-reasoning tasks and both models, with errors as large as 69 points. That strongly suggests the issue is not implementation detail but the level of evidence used for termination. SlowFast, which only adjusts commitment pace, also violates the tolerance on every long-reasoning setting. KLASS remains acceptable only on its calibration backbone and drops by 5–52 points on Dream. The paper uses these results to argue that local commitment heuristics cannot substitute for explicit sequence-level convergence verification.

One notable empirical claim is transferability: the same thresholds work across tasks and model families for LATCH, whereas prior methods do not. That is a meaningful sign that candidate-aware stopping is better aligned with actual convergence behavior than schedule-aware stopping.b:T56c,The experimental design is intentionally simple. Invisible reasoning is defined as consequential computation in internal representations during the forward pass without an interpretable trace in output tokens. The authors insert a fixed filler-token sequence before the model’s answer. The key control is that the same filler sequence is used for every problem, so it cannot encode information about a particular question or answer at the token level. In evaluation, few-shot examples include the question, an assistant-side filler span, and the ground-truth answer; the test query is also followed by an assistant-side filler prefill before the model generates the answer. The paper proposes three diagnostic criteria: performance improves with filler tokens; the improvement depends on filler content; and filler preferences differ across models. These criteria make it less plausible that the effect comes from ordinary semantic prompting, and more plausible that model-specific representations and attention dynamics are being used. The authors apply Logit Lens to track the layer-by-layer evolution of answer-option logits in MCQA prompts. For each sample, they cache hidden states from every transformer block in a single forward pass. At each layer, they take the hidden state at the answer position, apply the model’s final layer norm, project it through the unembedding matrix, and extract only the logits for the option-label tokens such as A/B/C/D. This yields a ranking trajectory across layers. HDL is then defined as the point beyond which the relative ranking of options remains stable.

That definition is stronger than simply checking whether top-1 stops changing. It captures whether the model’s internal preference structure has settled. The paper then tests whether this phenomenon is architecture-level rather than an artifact. It does so through cross-model and cross-dataset evaluation, LoRA before/after comparisons, varying the number of answer options, and changing label formats. The label-format result is important: alphabetic labels and Arabic numerals produce sharper HDLs than Roman numerals, which hints that tokenizer statistics and token geometry affect how clearly commitment can be read out.

Relative to prior work, the contribution is diagnostic rather than algorithmic. No learned exit policy is trained, and no layers are removed. The paper tries to measure committed inference as a naturally emerging property of standard transformers. The main caveat is also clear: the analysis depends on Logit Lens, whose faithfulness on intermediate layers is still debated, and the setup is restricted to single-token answer labels rather than open-ended generation.e:T5a4,The study covers four model families—Qwen, Llama, Granite, and Mistral—on four MCQA benchmarks. The main empirical pattern is that a distinct HDL appears across models, and layerwise accuracy often jumps sharply around that layer before flattening. The headline number in the abstract is a gain of up to +0.61 accuracy for Qwen on CommonsenseQA at the HDL relative to earlier layers, after which performance remains largely stable. That supports the interpretation that later layers mostly reinforce rather than revise the decision.

The most informative ablations are threefold. First, label-format ablations show that A/B/C/D and 1/2/3/4 yield clearer decision boundaries than Roman numerals. This suggests that token identity, pretraining frequency, and tokenizer behavior matter for observability. Second, varying the number of options does not remove the HDL, which argues against the phenomenon being a quirk of four-way prompting. Third, LoRA fine-tuning leaves the HDL largely unchanged, supporting the claim that it is tied to model architecture more than to downstream adaptation.

For a pretraining-oriented reader, the most interesting patterns are not the exact benchmark numbers but two structural observations: ranking stabilization looks abrupt rather than gradual, and ranking freeze is not the same as confidence formation. This lets the authors separate anxiety-driven failures from capability-driven failures and successful attempts. That distinction is central to the paper. Second, it measures token-use calibration. After producing an answer, the model is asked to estimate how many tokens were required to complete the solution. This estimate is compared with the true token count through a winsorized token ratio.
