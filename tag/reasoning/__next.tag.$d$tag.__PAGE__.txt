Formally, the policy first samples a mode m in {TH, NT} for a prompt x, then generates a response z conditioned on x and m. The routing objective is defined with an efficiency margin b: thinking is preferred only if its estimated utility exceeds no-thinking by at least b; ties go to NT. This margin is a reward-space prior that biases toward the cheaper mode when quality is similar.

The first key mechanism is paired current-policy counterfactual rollouts. For selected prompts, the current policy is forced to answer once in TH and once in NT. This produces paired responses under both modes for the same prompt, allowing direct cross-mode utility comparison. Because both rollouts come from the current policy, the routing target tracks the model’s evolving capabilities instead of relying on fixed labels, heuristics, or a teacher.

The second mechanism is hierarchical counterfactual credit assignment. The paper uses a disjoint token-level rule. At the initial routing token, the usual within-mode advantage is replaced by a bounded cross-mode utility credit. For the response tokens, only within-mode GRPO advantage is used. This means the routing token learns which mode to choose, while the response tokens learn how to answer well within the chosen mode. Compared with sequence-level policy gradients over the whole output, this better matches the hierarchical structure of the decision process.

The third mechanism is a paired-to-self-routed curriculum. Early in training, the method keeps enough paired prompts so both modes continue to receive evidence and updates. Later, control is gradually transferred to autonomous self-routing. The purpose is to prevent early mode bias from collapsing exploration before the value of both modes is estimated reliably.

The Scaffolder proposes new tasks conditioned on historical outcomes, and the Learner attempts them in an external environment. The motivation is straightforward: if both roles share weights, improvements acquired by the Learner through RL can directly improve the Scaffolder’s ability to place tasks at the right difficulty.

Second, difficulty alignment through the so-called Stretch Zone. The paper does not optimize for “harder is better.” It tries to jointly satisfy task validity, learnability, and behavioral stability. In the formulation, a domain verifier provides a validity signal, a difficulty reward favors tasks near the Learner’s boundary, and a replay term reinforces likelihood on verified successful trajectories. This trade-off matters. If the generator only pushes difficulty upward, it can drift into unsolvable regions. If training only replays successful trajectories, it collapses back into the comfort zone.

Third, an alternating dual-loop update scheme. One loop updates the Scaffolder for difficulty alignment; the other updates the Learner for task solving. The implementation is not a single differentiable end-to-end loss. The Scaffolder generates adaptive constraints from historical feedback, such as bargaining bottom lines or resource constraints in optimization tasks. After verifier filtering, these tasks are passed to the Learner, which is optimized with a GRPO-style group-relative reward objective. At the end of each epoch, verified successful trajectories are replayed with an SFT-style objective to stabilize behavior and reduce drift.

Relative to prior work, the main differences are clear. It does not rely on a frozen environment generator. It is not a teacher-student pipeline. It is not standard curriculum learning with a manually scheduled difficulty ladder. And it is not classic two-agent self-play with fully separate models. The design is closer to role switching inside one model than to conventional co-evolution between two independently trained agents.15:T5df,The evaluation spans two very different constrained-reasoning settings: multi-turn bargaining and mathematical/operations-research style optimization. The bargaining setup uses Craigslist, while the optimization side uses Mano Complex and Complex OR. The point is not a domain-specific trick, but whether one self-evolving mechanism can work in both social interaction and rigid constraint-solving environments.

The main empirical findings are threefold. First, STRETCH breaks early training plateaus that static RL baselines hit quickly. Second, it outperforms domain-specific baselines on both domains in final task success or score, suggesting the gains are not tied to one verifier or one reward design. Third, the ablations support the claimed mechanism: removing dynamic difficulty alignment causes the system to regress toward easy repeated tasks; removing replay makes training less stable and increases forgetting; separating Scaffolder and Learner into different models hurts difficulty calibration and sample efficiency.

From a pretraining perspective, the experimental scope is still limited. These are controlled post-training environments, not large-scale open-domain language modeling runs. The paper emphasizes training dynamics, plateau breaking, and cross-domain transfer more than parameter count, token budget, or scaling behavior.
