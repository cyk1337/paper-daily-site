9:T7df,PIVOT can be summarized as online local correction followed by student continuation, plus KL supervision on the corrected span. During training, the student first generates a rollout prefix. At a chosen intervention point, the teacher produces a correction conditioned on that student-generated prefix. The student then continues generation from the corrected context rather than from its original mistaken continuation. This produces teacher-assisted trajectories that are more likely to end in verifier-approved solutions, while still preserving part of the student’s own rollout distribution. That is a key difference from simply injecting full teacher trajectories.

The optimization has two coupled parts. First, the resulting trajectories are still optimized with the standard RLVR objective, instantiated with GRPO and verifier-based rewards. This keeps the training target anchored to task correctness rather than pure imitation. Second, a teacher-student KL loss is applied only on the correction segments, providing dense token-level supervision exactly where the teacher intervened. Instead of distilling the entire sequence, the method concentrates supervision on the local regions that are most responsible for steering the trajectory back toward a valid reasoning path.

Mechanistically, the method addresses both bottlenecks at once. Online intervention changes the rollout distribution and increases access to reward-bearing trajectories. Local KL makes those interventions learnable by turning them into parameter updates the student can reproduce later. The paper further studies practical design choices: where intervention should happen, how much teacher guidance to inject, and how the KL term should be used. A recurring conclusion is that too-early intervention risks turning training into teacher-led generation, while too-late intervention cannot rescue already broken trajectories. Either intervention alone or token supervision alone is often insufficient; the combination is the point.a:T63e,The evaluation covers two hard reasoning settings: logic games from SynLogic and mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with both in-distribution and out-of-distribution testing on the math side. The headline result is on hard logic games, where RL-only training makes limited progress: PIVOT improves mean avg@8 from 19.5% for the best baseline to 35.6%, close to a 2x gain. That is a meaningful result because it directly targets the sparse-reward regime where standard RLVR tends to stall.

The most informative ablations are not just score comparisons but mechanism tests. The authors isolate trajectory-level intervention and token-level supervision, showing that each one alone can still fail to produce strong learning. Their combination gives the strongest gains, which directly supports the paper’s framing. They also study intervention placement and the KL design, showing that teacher correction is not simply “more is better.” The intervention has to occur at a point where the student can still continue productively, while being early enough to change the probability of eventual success. On mathematical reasoning tasks, PIVOT consistently beats the evaluated baselines on both ID and OOD benchmarks, suggesting that the gains are not limited to a single synthetic logic environment.

The strongest part of the empirical section is the controlled decomposition of why the method works. The paper does more than report a better final number; it shows that changing the trajectory distribution and densifying the local learning signal solve different pieces of the RLVR failure mode.b:T5b8,For a quick read, start with the introduction and the main method figure. - Judge: given (x, y), learns to discriminate whether y comes from the reference set or from the current generator’s on-policy samples, producing a “probability of being reference” score.
- Generator: optimized with RL (implemented on top of Prime-RL) to maximize the judge score.

There are four labeled boxes and four letters A–D. Starting from an initial arrangement, the model must apply k swaps, with k in {2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24}, writing the full state after each swap and then the final answer. Because every intermediate state is deterministic, step correctness can be scored exactly. The key intervention is the Edit condition: after the model has produced a partial chain up to step m, the state at step m is replaced with a different but valid arrangement, and the model is asked to continue. If the continuation and final answer evolve consistently from the corrupted state, the run is labeled follows-edit; if the model returns to the true trajectory, it is labeled repair; other categories capture additional failure modes.

This design has several strengths. The intervention is applied to the model’s own chain rather than to an externally written explanation, so it directly tests whether the model uses previously written text as computational input. The corrupted state is valid, which prevents trivial rejection based on malformed formatting. The paper also separates compliance, prefix accuracy, CoT accuracy, and direct accuracy, so reliance is not conflated with formatting failures, low capability, or answer bias.

The evaluation spans 25 open-weight models: Qwen3 size series with both base and post-trained variants, Gemma 3, Llama 3, and multiple OLMo 2 pretraining checkpoints. That itself is useful: more pretraining tokens do not automatically place a model in the high-reliance regime on this kind of serial reasoning task.10:T751,The authors build a fully verifiable full-plan benchmark on 4x4 sliding puzzles, including blocked-cell variants. The model sees an initial board and a goal board, then must output the entire action sequence at once. The evaluator replays the sequence step by step and stops at the first illegal move; there is no skipping, correction, or partial credit for reaching the goal midway through a longer invalid sequence.

To make efficiency verifiable, the benchmark uses a restricted family of boards with certified optimal lengths. For a state s, h(s) is defined as the sum of shortest free-cell graph distances from each tile to its goal cell. Since each move can reduce this quantity by at most one, h(s) is a lower bound on the optimal solution length. The dataset retains only boards for which a witness solution of length exactly h(s) exists, so the optimum d* is certified as d*=h(s). This avoids trusting the model and avoids expensive online exact search for every evaluation case.

The main metric is success-weighted plan efficiency, SPE = R × Q × E. R is execution reliability: the fraction of outputs that are fully legal. Q is planning coordination: among legal plans, the fraction that actually reaches the goal. E is action economy: among completed plans, the average ratio d*/L between optimal length and produced length. This decomposition is useful because it cleanly separates three failure modes: illegal execution, legal but incomplete planning, and completed but inefficient planning. It also yields SPE = C × E, where C is completion rate, so failed attempts still affect the overall score instead of disappearing from efficiency analysis.

The primary cohort controls both exact optimal horizon d* in {10, 20, 30, 40} and obstacle count k in {0, 1, 2, 3}, with 20 boards per cell. A separate stress cohort uses longer horizons with more obstacles.11:T554,The study evaluates 8,400 complete-plan outputs from seven model conditions. Each condition has 960 outputs on the primary cohort and 240 on the stress cohort, with three trials per board. In the primary cohort, Astra, Fable 5, and Opus 5.5 all achieve R=1 and Q=1, meaning every output is legal and reaches the goal. But their action economy differs materially: E is about 0.830, 0.807, and 0.745, corresponding to average excess lengths of 8.33, 9.65, and 13.01 moves over the certified optimum. This is the central empirical point: identical success can conceal meaningful differences in planning efficiency.

Other models fail in different ways. Some lose mostly on legality, some on legal-but-incomplete long-horizon coordination, and some complete reliably but with much longer plans. The paper reports bootstrap confidence intervals and resamples at the board level rather than the individual-output level, preserving the balanced design.

An important caveat is that the certified-optimum construction restricts the benchmark family. Because the retained boards satisfy d*=h(s), they emphasize long-sequence coordination without requiring heuristic retreat or temporary detours.
