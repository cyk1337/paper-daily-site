18:T8ce,The method has three core pieces. First, a unified parameter space. Instead of maintaining separate generator and solver models, the authors use one base model, switched by role-specific system prompts into a Scaffolder or a Learner. The Scaffolder proposes new tasks conditioned on historical outcomes, and the Learner attempts them in an external environment. The motivation is straightforward: if both roles share weights, improvements acquired by the Learner through RL can directly improve the Scaffolder’s ability to place tasks at the right difficulty.

Second, difficulty alignment through the so-called Stretch Zone. The paper does not optimize for “harder is better.” It tries to jointly satisfy task validity, learnability, and behavioral stability. In the formulation, a domain verifier provides a validity signal, a difficulty reward favors tasks near the Learner’s boundary, and a replay term reinforces likelihood on verified successful trajectories. This trade-off matters. If the generator only pushes difficulty upward, it can drift into unsolvable regions. If training only replays successful trajectories, it collapses back into the comfort zone.

Third, an alternating dual-loop update scheme. One loop updates the Scaffolder for difficulty alignment; the other updates the Learner for task solving. The implementation is not a single differentiable end-to-end loss. The Scaffolder generates adaptive constraints from historical feedback, such as bargaining bottom lines or resource constraints in optimization tasks. After verifier filtering, these tasks are passed to the Learner, which is optimized with a GRPO-style group-relative reward objective. At the end of each epoch, verified successful trajectories are replayed with an SFT-style objective to stabilize behavior and reduce drift.

Relative to prior work, the main differences are clear. It does not rely on a frozen environment generator. It is not a teacher-student pipeline. It is not standard curriculum learning with a manually scheduled difficulty ladder. And it is not classic two-agent self-play with fully separate models. The design is closer to role switching inside one model than to conventional co-evolution between two independently trained agents.19:T5df,The evaluation spans two very different constrained-reasoning settings: multi-turn bargaining and mathematical/operations-research style optimization. The bargaining setup uses Craigslist, while the optimization side uses Mano Complex and Complex OR. The point is not a domain-specific trick, but whether one self-evolving mechanism can work in both social interaction and rigid constraint-solving environments.

The main empirical findings are threefold. First, STRETCH breaks early training plateaus that static RL baselines hit quickly. Second, it outperforms domain-specific baselines on both domains in final task success or score, suggesting the gains are not tied to one verifier or one reward design. Third, the ablations support the claimed mechanism: removing dynamic difficulty alignment causes the system to regress toward easy repeated tasks; removing replay makes training less stable and increases forgetting; separating Scaffolder and Learner into different models hurts difficulty calibration and sample efficiency.

From a pretraining perspective, the experimental scope is still limited. These are controlled post-training environments, not large-scale open-domain language modeling runs. The paper emphasizes training dynamics, plateau breaking, and cross-domain transfer more than parameter count, token budget, or scaling behavior. To assess how far the idea transfers to foundation-model training, one would want larger models, longer runs, and more open-ended task distributions.1a:T460,For a quick pass, focus on three places. First, the introduction and the figure defining Comfort, Stretch, and Difficulty zones. That framing drives the whole method. Second, the main training diagram and the alternating update procedure. The key question is why the authors insist on one shared parameter space instead of two models. Third, the ablations, especially the variants without difficulty alignment, without replay, and with separated parameter spaces. Those are the sections that tell you whether this is more than repackaged curriculum RL.

A practical reading order is: problem setup, training loop, then ablations. In about 30 to 45 minutes, that is enough to capture the main idea. Two points deserve deeper inspection. One is how the difficulty reward estimates the capability boundary and how task-specific that estimate is. The other is whether unified role switching creates interference, where the model’s task-generation preferences and task-solving policy contaminate each other. Those issues would become central if one tried to move this idea into pretraining or self-generated data pipelines.1b:T5c5,The base setup is masked diffusion language modeling. During training, completion tokens are masked according to a sampled time t, and the model predicts the original tokens at masked positions. During inference, a chunk of length C starts fully masked; the model iteratively predicts masked positions in parallel and keeps selected high-confidence predictions until the chunk is filled. Registers are fixed-position tokens inserted into the prompt. After a chunk is denoised, an additional forward pass over the prompt and completed chunk saves the last-layer hidden states at the register positions, producing an R-by-d continuous state. For the next chunk, those saved states replace the input embeddings at the same register positions, while the rest of the prompt remains unchanged. The same slots are reused after every chunk, so the model must learn what to preserve, what to overwrite, and how to expose that state to the next denoising process. The supervised recipe splits a long reasoning trace into chunks, clears generated text between chunks, and trains the next chunk from the prompt plus carried register state. To prevent shortcut learning, the training sometimes masks attention from the new chunk to the prompt and forces prediction from a fully masked chunk. The work also compares task-directed registers trained by next-chunk loss with reconstruction-trained memory tokens, and integrates registers into chunked GRPO for reinforcement learning refinement.1c:T40d,Read closely for about 60 to 90 minutes. Start with Figure 1 to understand the bounded-state setting: generated text is cleared, and the carried state size does not grow with the number of chunks. Then read Figure 2 and the method section for the inference loop: denoise a chunk, run an extra forward pass, save register hidden states, and inject them into the next chunk. The most important technical material is the training recipe, especially text clearing, prompt-attention masking, and fully masked prediction. These choices create the need to use registers. In the experiments, focus on the comparisons against discrete-text carry, reconstruction memory tokens, and full-sequence SFT. The GRPO section is useful for seeing whether RL improves the register read-write policy rather than only improving local chunk generation. Good follow-up questions are register-capacity scaling with R and d, interpretability of the carried state, cross-task transfer, and whether the same objective can be moved into pretraining or mid-training.1d:Ta2b,方法核心是 Discrete Beckmann Transport Models，简称 DBTM。理解它要抓住三个层次。

The first layer is representation. A discrete token sequence is embedded into a continuous space where each token is a vertex of the V-dimensional simplex. A full sequence is therefore a point in (R^V)^L. The data distribution lives on one-hot vertices, while the prior is continuous Gaussian noise. The modeling goal is to learn a transport from the ambient continuous space to the discrete data manifold.

The second layer is dynamics. Standard flow matching learns a time-dependent velocity field b_t(x) and requires ODE integration at sampling time. Flow-map methods learn a two-time operator X_{s,t}, but the off-diagonal terms are usually learned either from a teacher or through unstable bootstrapping. The third layer is training. Rather than supervising the terminal point directly, the paper derives a conservation equation, b · ∇T = 0. Intuitively, as one moves along a flow line, the final transport destination should remain unchanged, so T is constant in the flow direction. This is the central technical contribution: the PDE residual is turned into a trainable objective that can be minimized from data, removing the need for a teacher flow. The practical training procedure uses a frozen copy of the current model as a bootstrap target plus an Euler correction, iteratively regressing toward a better approximation of the true transport map. A partially trained T_theta is not treated as a failed endpoint predictor; instead, it is interpreted as mapping to an intermediate point along the autonomous flow. That makes iterative application of T_theta meaningful at inference time.

Instead of interpolating the entire sequence uniformly, the method preserves part of the context and learns transport on the remaining positions. The main technical contribution is Tilted-Target DOPD. The paper argues that directly reusing DOPD’s sampled-token surrogate on cached trajectories does not preserve the intended stationary solution. There are three important design choices.

First, reward variants. With same-instance reward, z=x, so the coach is encouraged to diagnose what went wrong on that specific example and preserve useful intermediate results. With cross-instance reward, z is sampled from a disjoint set of related instances, forcing the coach to extract reusable structure such as task rules or general strategies. This distinction is central: it separates local error correction from transferable experience.

Second, optimization. For each source trajectory, the coach samples multiple candidate experience snippets, each is evaluated through actor rollouts, and the coach is trained with GRPO using relative outcomes within the group. This makes sense because there is no token-level supervision for “good experience,” and the reward is only observable through the actor’s downstream behavior.

Third, iterative experience updating. Starting from empty experience, the coach repeatedly rewrites the current experience after each new actor attempt. The updated experience replaces the previous one rather than simply appending more history. That matters because raw trajectories accumulate errors and irrelevant details; replacement makes the experience representation closer to a belief-state update than to naive memory growth.

For a generated chain, define candidate boundaries. At each boundary, read the model’s current belief about the final answer. First, the paper uses fork-before attribution. If belief changes sharply over segment (t-, t], the causal step is that segment, so the score is assigned to t- and sibling continuations are re-rolled from just before the belief-moving step. This aligns the counterfactual branch with the step whose contribution is being estimated.

Second, the paper instantiates belief readout at three access levels.

PROBE-JS is the black-box version. At each boundary, the model is interrupted with a short probe such as “so what is your final answer?”, and the resulting answer distribution is sampled. The score is the Jensen–Shannon divergence between consecutive boundary distributions. Large JS indicates that the segment caused a meaningful shift in answer belief. The advantage is that it only requires sampling access; the downside is extra generation at every boundary.

LENS-SHIFT is the white-box version. Instead of making the model answer explicitly, it uses a logit-lens style readout to measure how strongly each layer already commits to the final answer, producing a depth profile at each boundary. The score is the change in this profile across adjacent boundaries. The motivation is that belief shifts may appear as representational reorganization across depth, not only as a change in final logits. This avoids extra generation but requires hidden-state access and a target final answer for readout.

BSV, or Belief-Shift Vector, is an amortized readout. The authors fit an activation direction offline by contrasting high-confidence and doubtful states, then score a boundary by projecting its hidden state onto that direction. In the paper, BSV is mainly used for pre-RL validation: if one could measure belief shift more directly, would it align better with the Monte-Carlo value curve? So BSV functions more as an analysis instrument than the default online training method.
