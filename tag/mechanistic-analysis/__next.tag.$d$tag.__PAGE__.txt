An LLM-as-judge check labels 94.6% of clean-perturbed pairs as intent-preserving.d:T725,The paper compares three state structures under a shared setup. Frontier superposition stores only the active alternatives at the current step. Cumulative superposition stores the full reached history. General weighted superposition allows arbitrary weight allocation over that history. All three use the same graph, candidate bank, downstream query family, and decoder, so the comparison isolates only one design choice: what the continuous state preserves.

The theoretical core is a critical-dimension analysis. Objects receive independent Gaussian embeddings, and the latent state is a normalized superposition of those embeddings. A common decoder then tries to distinguish a useful direction from competing directions. The authors derive an exact finite-dimensional score law and use it to compare the minimum hidden dimension needed for reliable decoding. The main result is that for a broad class of state-independent, nonlocalized, bounded-influence queries, cumulative superposition benefits from coherent reinforcement among historically useful components, while unrelated components behave mostly as random interference. So even though cumulative states have larger support, the signal relevant to future computation can be stronger rather than weaker.

The paper then maps this abstraction back to Transformer mechanics. Attention expands the current computation, while residual writing determines whether previous objects are discarded or preserved. In weighted superpositions, the weakest retained coefficient becomes the routing bottleneck: if some historical items receive too little weight, later attention cannot reliably reuse them. This leads to a minimax design principle: when future queries are unknown, uniform cumulative weighting is robustly near-optimal, and in the formal setup it is exactly minimax.e:T4e1,The experiments are structured to validate the theory rather than to chase a benchmark headline. First, there are controlled simulations checking that the finite-dimensional score law predicts actual retrieval and discrimination behavior. Second, width-scaling experiments compare frontier, cumulative, and weighted superpositions as hidden dimension varies. The central empirical pattern is that cumulative superposition reaches usable performance at smaller widths than frontier superposition for the nonlocalized query classes studied in the theory. Third, the paper implements the attention-residual construction in lightweight models and GPT-2-style token-level settings to test the mechanism-level claim that the weakest retained coefficient is the practical routing bottleneck.

Two aspects are especially useful. One, the cumulative advantage is not restricted to trivial symmetric or purely additive queries; the query family is allowed to be continuous, correlated, asymmetric, and identity-dependent. Two, the failure mode of weighted superposition is interpretable: the problem is not simply insufficient total energy, but poor allocation, where potentially useful historical items are assigned coefficients too small to be reused later.f:T4b0,Start with the setup and the definitions of the three superposition structures. The deeper questions are whether the query class matches real next-step computation in LLM reasoning, how sensitive the conclusions are to the independent Gaussian embedding assumption, and how one would train a recurrent or latent-CoT model to preserve history in a balanced way without flooding the state with irrelevant information.10:T7bf,The method is deliberately simple but well targeted. The authors define two axes. Direct rate (DR) measures how often the model repeats its top answer over 30 samples of the original prompt at temperature 0.6. This captures concentration under one wording. Framed rate (FR) then asks whether that same direct top answer survives 30 matched scenario reframings of the underlying question. Importantly, FR is not the concentration of answers under reframing; it is the reappearance rate of the original direct top answer. That design choice is the core of the paper, because it measures persistence of a specific preference rather than generic consistency. The final bias depth score is π = DR × FR, which is high only when the model is both concentrated under direct prompting and robust to reframing.

The dataset construction is also central. They build 4,442 opinion prompt families, each grounded in a real Olmo-3-7B-SFT training example, and create matched everyday-scenario reframings for each family. Because the task is open-ended generation rather than multiple choice, answer normalization is necessary to merge semantically equivalent outputs and avoid contaminating FR with superficial phrasing variation. The contribution is therefore less about a new model and more about turning a causally meaningful question—does a preference survive paraphrastic and situational variation?—into a scalable evaluation protocol.

The paper then runs this protocol across the full Olmo-3-7B release pipeline: Pretrained → SFT → DPO → RLVR. That allows stage-wise tracking of bias depth. It also adds two attribution analyses: whether the post-SFT top answer matches the pretrained top answer, and whether a biased answer can be traced back to a specific SFT training example. Second, along the full Olmo-3-7B pipeline, most Deep bias is already present at the pretrained stage, whereas Shallow bias keeps increasing through SFT and every later post-training stage.
