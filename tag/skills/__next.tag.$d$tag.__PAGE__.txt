9:T7dd,HERD has two coupled components: an evolving harness and a constrained recursive distillation step.

On the harness side, the system does not simply cache raw examples. Instead, it maintains two forms of external state. First, it refines textual skills that summarize reusable patterns from the current and past tasks. Second, it stores a bounded set of high-residual examples that those skills fail to explain well. This split is deliberate. Storing only examples wastes context budget and scales poorly with stream length; storing only abstract skills loses tail cases and exceptions. HERD treats the harness as a selective, fast-learning layer that keeps both abstraction and residual evidence.

On the parametric side, the model is updated by distillation rather than direct supervised finetuning on the raw task stream. The student starts from the previous checkpoint and is trained to match the behavior of the prior system when that system is equipped with the current harness. In effect, the harness organizes and filters experience first, and only then is that experience written into the weights. This is meant to reduce noise and lower drift compared with ordinary SFT.

The central technical idea is drift-constrained distillation with dual anchors. One anchor is the previous checkpoint, which limits per-stage movement and protects short-term retention. The other is the original base model, which limits cumulative drift across many stages and helps preserve broad general capabilities. The paper presents this as the key to recursive stability: the challenge is not merely learning stage t, but still behaving sensibly after stage 20 or 25.

Relative to standard continual finetuning methods such as replay, regularization, or LoRA-based sequential updates, HERD inserts an explicit external selection layer before weight updates. Relative to pure retrieval or prompt-memory approaches, HERD aims to deploy only the consolidated weights, so inference does not inherit an ever-growing context cost.a:T7c9,The main evaluation uses TRACE+17, a 25-stage task stream composed of 8 TRACE tasks and 17 Super-NaturalInstructions tasks. The reported metrics include final average performance over seen tasks, acquisition, forgetting, and degradation on general capability probes.

The headline result is that HERD reaches a final mean of 0.706 over all seen tasks at the end of the stream, outperforming all baselines and beating the non-parametric methods by 27 points. That gap is important because it supports the paper’s core claim that a harness alone does not scale as a long-term learner under bounded context. At the same time, HERD’s acquisition score is 0.713, statistically indistinguishable from the plasticity floor set by Seq-LoRA, so the method does not buy stability by simply refusing to learn new tasks.

For forgetting, HERD reports 0.010, roughly half or less than every weight-updating baseline except SDFT. The paper notes that SDFT suffers generation collapse, which is an important caveat: low forgetting numbers can be misleading if the model’s outputs degenerate. On general tests, HERD drops by only 6.3 points, whereas other baselines lose around 20.0 to 25.5 points, suggesting that the cumulative-drift control from the base-model anchor is doing real work.

A useful qualitative result is the stage-5-to-stage-12 rule retention example shown in Figure 1: a rule learned earlier remains present in the weights alone, without the harness, across three random seeds. That is stronger evidence of actual consolidation than a small benchmark gain.

The most informative ablations are the ones that separate skills from residual memory, and dual-anchor distillation from weaker update rules.
