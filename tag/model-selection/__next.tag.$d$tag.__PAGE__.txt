9:T672,LRD consists of three complementary measurement families.

The first is the Frenet family, which captures global subspace motion. For each layer, the representation matrix is centered and reduced to its dominant singular subspace. The sequence of layers is then treated as a discrete path on the Grassmann manifold. Adjacent-layer Grassmann geodesic distance serves as a notion of speed, while Menger curvature over triples of consecutive layers measures whether the path bends. This family answers whether the dominant global directions are still changing.

The second is NRS, the Neighborhood Retention Score. It measures how much local nearest-neighbor structure is preserved from one layer to the next. This is important because global subspace stability does not imply local neighborhood stability, and vice versa.

The third is GFMI, Graph Filtration Mutual Information, which measures how well an intermediate layer aligns with the final layer in terms of structural organization. Instead of using a simple similarity score, it compares graph-based filtrations and computes mutual information, aiming to capture relational or topological agreement with the final representation.

A notable implementation choice is that for each model-task pair, the subspace dimension r is fixed as the smallest rank explaining 95% of the variance of the final layer, and the same r is used for all layers. This improves comparability across depth, though it also makes the final layer the reference point. The main idea is that the three families target different aspects of representation dynamics: global motion, local retention, and final-layer alignment.a:T648,The empirical study covers 31 off-the-shelf models and 30 MTEB tasks: 25 embedders, both encoder-based and decoder-based, plus 6 base LLMs. Tasks include classification, retrieval, semantic textual similarity, reranking, and duplicate detection. The breadth is sufficient to support architecture-level and task-level observations.

The first result is descriptive but important: LRD exposes differences that final-layer representations alone do not reveal. Different model families show distinct trajectories in speed, curvature, neighborhood retention, and alignment-to-final-layer, and these trajectories also vary by task type. Representation formation is therefore structured, not just a monotonic march toward the last layer.

The second result is about label-free model selection. The paper aggregates layer-wise measurements into model-level scores and finds positive correlation with downstream performance, with d_{0,L} being the strongest signal. The implication is that the overall representational displacement from input to final layer may serve as a useful unsupervised proxy for model quality.

The third result is on layer pruning. GFMI outperforms random pruning at the 15% and 20% budgets and achieves the best median change at every budget considered. In other words, preserving layers that are structurally important for final-layer alignment is more reliable than dropping layers at random.

For foundation-model work, the key takeaway is not a single number but the fact that layer-wise dynamics can be turned into actionable selection signals rather than remaining purely interpretive.b:T4a2,For a quick but useful read, start with the definitions of the three measurement families and make sure their roles are distinct in your mind: Frenet for dominant subspace motion, NRS for local neighborhood preservation, and GFMI for structural alignment with the final layer. If these are all collapsed into generic 'layer similarity,' the paper loses most of its value.

Then move to the result figures organized by model family and task type. The most informative part is not which curve is highest, but the shape of the trajectories: where representations move rapidly, where they stabilize, and where local structure is reorganized. Finally, read the two application sections on label-free model selection and pruning, since they determine whether the framework has practical utility.

With limited time, around 40 minutes should be enough for a solid skim. If reading more deeply, pay special attention to GFMI, since it is the least intuitive component and the one most directly tied to pruning utility. 为了解决开放式任务评估标准不统一的问题，作者提出 4C taxonomy，把回答质量拆成四类约束：Competence、Content、Control、Compliance。虽然全文片段里没有把每一类展开到最细，但从任务构造可以看出，它们分别覆盖能力完成度、内容正确与充分性、格式/风格/结构控制，以及对指令和规则的遵守。作者不是直接写一个总分 rubric，而是为每个任务手工定义细粒度 rubric，再用这些 rubric 组合出 preferred / dispreferred response pairs。

数据构造上，作者把四类开放任务改写成 response discrimination 形式，覆盖 Medical、Legal & Finance、Complex Instruction Following、Creative Writing。关键是 generator-verifier pipeline：先生成候选回答，再通过 rubric 控制，构造“满足全部 rubric 的 preferred response”和“故意违反部分 rubric 的 dispreferred response”。这样得到的不是粗糙的正负样本，而是带有可解释错误类型的对比样本。RuDE 评估时，base model 只需判断哪一个回答更好，或者对候选回答做相对偏好打分。

和已有代理指标相比，区别很明确。MMLU 测的是知识回忆和选择题判断，PPL 测的是 token-level 拟合；两者都不直接触及开放式对齐任务中的格式控制、意图理解、约束满足。RuDE 则把评估对象从“知识是否记住”转成“是否具备学会对齐行为的判别基础”。这也是它与传统 reward model eval、preference eval 的不同之处：这里评的是未经 post-train 的 base model，并且目标是预测其可塑性，而不是评估一个已对齐模型的当前偏好质量。d:T733,The core method is RuDE, short for Rubric-based Discriminative Evaluation. Instead of asking a base model to generate a strong open-ended answer, RuDE asks it to discriminate between a preferred and a dispreferred response. The motivation is that discrimination is easier than generation for a base model, and that the ability to recognize better responses may be a good proxy for the ability to learn to generate them after post-training.

To make open-ended evaluation systematic, the paper introduces a 4C taxonomy: Competence, Content, Control, and Compliance. These dimensions cover whether the response completes the task, whether the content is correct and adequate, whether formatting/style/structure constraints are followed, and whether explicit instructions or rules are obeyed. For each task, the authors manually define fine-grained rubrics under these dimensions.

The data pipeline is also important. Four open-ended domains are converted into response-discrimination datasets: medical, legal and finance, complex instruction following, and creative writing. A generator-verifier pipeline constructs contrastive pairs. Preferred responses satisfy all rubrics, while dispreferred responses intentionally violate selected rubrics. This gives the benchmark more structure than generic preference pairs, because the negative examples are tied to interpretable failure modes.

Relative to prior proxies, the distinction is clear. MMLU mainly measures knowledge recall and multiple-choice discrimination; perplexity measures token-level fit to pretraining data. Neither directly targets the ingredients that matter in post-training, such as instruction adherence, controllability, or alignment with human intent. RuDE instead evaluates whether the base model already contains a discriminative substrate that post-training can exploit.e:T522,The main empirical claim is strong correlation with downstream post-training outcomes: RuDE scores achieve Pearson correlation above 90% with the performance of fully instruction-tuned models. This is substantially stronger than the weak correlation reported for traditional base-model metrics such as MMLU on open-ended post-training tasks.

The evaluation spans four domains and multiple pretrained base models, followed by actual post-training through instruction tuning or RL. The most concrete case highlighted in the paper is that RuDE predicts Qwen3-4B-Base to have higher post-training potential than Qwen2.5-7B-Base, and this ranking is later validated after RL. That result matters because it shows RuDE is not merely a size proxy.

The likely ablation message is threefold. First, discriminative evaluation is more reliable than direct generative evaluation for base models because it avoids the instruction-following bottleneck. Second, fine-grained rubric-controlled contrastive pairs are more informative than coarse preference labels. Third, evaluating across diverse open-ended domains improves robustness of the ranking signal.
