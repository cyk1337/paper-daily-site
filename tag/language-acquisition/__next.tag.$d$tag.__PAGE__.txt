c:T6d9,The technical core is a time-dependent Rasch/IRT-style model of vocabulary acquisition. Each word j has a difficulty δ_j. Each child i has an ability θ_i(t) composed of a baseline efficiency ξ_i, an acceleration term κ_i log(t/a0), and a waking-hours term H that links developmental time to cumulative exposure. Word production probability is determined by the gap between child ability and word difficulty. The central modeling move is to interpret growth on ratio-scale variables with real zeros, so log transforms have a direct meaning in terms of returns to experience rather than being a generic curve-fitting trick.

Three design choices matter. First, the model is built on interpretable units such as tokens heard per hour and vocabulary size in word types. Second, word difficulty is estimated freely rather than reduced to frequency, which lets the analysis isolate whether learning efficiency itself changes over development. Third, the authors compare four nested models: no acceleration, no individual efficiency variation, no individual acceleration variation, and the full accelerating accumulator. This is important because it separates true acceleration from apparent curvature induced by heterogeneity.

A major strength, visible only in the full paper, is that the model is fit to item-level longitudinal CDI data rather than aggregate vocabulary counts. That means word difficulty, age of acquisition, and child trajectories are jointly constrained. The paper also checks robustness with hierarchical pooling, 2PL IRT variants, alternative item selections on CDI forms, and linear-time growth alternatives. The high κ estimates survive these changes, which makes the result much harder to dismiss as a parameterization artifact.d:T77e,The empirical analysis uses five longitudinal Wordbank datasets covering American English, Norwegian, and Japanese, with monolingual typically developing children aged 8–36 months. Total N is 1,841, and each child has at least three longitudinal administrations. The study focuses on reported production rather than comprehension because it is available over a wider age range and is more reliable.

The full accelerating model wins on all five datasets. Relative to the next-best ablation, the item-level leave-one-out expected log predictive density improves by more than 760 in every dataset, which is a large gap for this kind of model comparison. The estimated acceleration parameter κ is far above the pure-accumulator value of 1 in every case, roughly 10.6 [10.5, 10.8] to 13.3 [13.2, 13.3]. These estimates remain essentially unchanged in larger data settings, hierarchical pooling, 2PL IRT variants, and alternative CDI item selections. Linear growth models do not fit better.

There is also substantial between-child variation in acceleration, with σ_b ranging from 3.2 to 6.6, yet the fitted population distribution places 99.5% of children above κ=1. The authors are careful about what can and cannot be inferred at the individual level: typical CDI series are too sparse to estimate a child-specific κ precisely, but adding a population acceleration term improves trajectory prediction for 91.9% of children when at least five prior CDIs are available.

One of the most useful interpretive results is the conversion from acceleration to exposure requirements. The model implies that the number of exposures needed to learn a word drops sharply over development. Later-acquired words are not simply easier; the learner is more efficient. In the LM comparison, even models trained on child-directed speech do not show this accelerating pattern and instead look closer to constant proportional returns.e:T487,Start with the model definition and Figure 1. Those are the parts that establish the result as a property of the data rather than a flexible fit.

Next, focus on the age-of-acquisition and exposure interpretation. This is where the paper becomes most relevant to pretraining: acceleration is turned into a measurable statement about sample efficiency improving over time.
