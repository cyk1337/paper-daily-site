9:T623,The core technique is a **dual-spectrum diagnostic protocol**: (1) Activation covariance spectra — compute covariance matrices of hidden states at each layer on a fixed validation pool, perform SVD to obtain the eigenvalue spectrum, and fit a power-law exponent α via log-log regression over a specified rank window [low, high] (denoted α(I)); (2) Per-sample gradient spectra — for a selected weight matrix (defaulting to W_O in the final attention block), compute per-sample gradients on validation sequences, stack them, and analyze the SVD singular value spectrum.

The key innovation is the **α_tail** metric design: instead of using a single universal rank window, the window is dynamically adjusted with model scale ([100,200] for 12-layer, [200,400] for 36-layer, [400,800] for 48-layer models). Larger models learn more resolved feature directions, so the boundary between learned head and unresolved tail sits at higher rank. This window-shifting strategy makes the diagnostic comparable across scales.

Three core empirical findings: (1) Batch size acts as a latent determinant of representation geometry — runs with different batch sizes settle into systematically distinct activation spectra at equal loss; (2) The activation covariance tail (α_tail) measured early in training reliably forecasts downstream token efficiency; (3) Movement of the activation spectrum head correlates with learning dynamics. The normalization is important: it removes scale effects and makes operators more comparable across lengths and degree profiles. The authors then use Hermitian dilation to reduce spectral analysis of a rectangular bipartite operator to spectral analysis of a symmetric block matrix, making singular values the natural object for symmetric diagnostics.

The central decomposition writes M as an orthogonal sum of a symmetric component M_sym and an antisymmetric component M_asym under the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. The main theorem states that any spectral diagnostic depending only on singular values, or only on M_sym, is invariant under transpose and therefore structurally orientation-blind. In practical terms, such diagnostics can measure transport capacity but cannot recover transport direction. A quantitative converse then shows that the transpose sensitivity of any Lipschitz diagnostic is controlled by ||M_asym||_F. This makes the antisymmetric residual the minimal complementary quantity needed to recover directional information.

On the symmetric axis, the paper builds semantics through conductance and Cheeger-type reasoning. Low conductance corresponds to bottleneck routing; abnormally high conductance corresponds to diffuse mixing. A useful nuance is that high conductance is not treated as automatically healthy. The theory is explicitly two-sided: both too little and too much mixing can indicate failure, but for different reasons. The degree sufficiency theorem further characterizes when coupling structure contributes information beyond simple degree heterogeneity.

A particularly interesting part is the closed-form architectural benchmark. For uniform causal attention, the authors derive an n-independent lower bound of 1/5 on conductance, with the worst cut near t*/n ≈ 0.32. For window attention, conductance can fall below this floor at O(w/n). This links a notion of healthy routing to concrete architectural choices, and turns the empirical fraction of heads falling below 1/5 into a population-level architectural signature.

The paper also makes a careful distinction between the object and the estimator. The theoretical object is true conductance ϕ, while empirical work uses a spectral sweep estimator \hat{ϕ}. The latter inherits finite-length artifacts, so length dependence in experiments may come from estimation bias rather than from the underlying transport property.
