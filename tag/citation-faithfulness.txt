18:T665,Methodologically, this is a controlled reproduction plus comparison study. The authors adapt the planting probe from Wallat et al. to a setting built around Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and three RLVR agents derived from it.

The procedure has three stages. First, the model answers a question given five retrieved passages, with exactly one citation per sentence. Second, a short span from the original claim is copied into a passage that was retrieved but not cited for that claim, and the model is run again on the doctored context. Third, the evaluation checks whether the citation for the same claim shifts to the doctored passage.

Several design choices matter. Since Qwen2.5 does not expose exact grounding spans, the planted span is selected deterministically as the four consecutive words in the claim with the most characters. Because the regenerated answer may be paraphrased, the authors do not simply ask whether the doctored citation appears anywhere. Instead, they align the new answer to the original claim using Jaccard overlap and score a claim-aligned outcome. The most important addition is the no-planting control. Rather than reporting raw planted rates, the paper asks how much citation movement is actually caused by the plant relative to a baseline where nothing is planted. This avoids conflating genuine post-rationalization with ordinary answer variability or self-repetition.

For the RLVR comparison, the setup is intentionally clean: one shared base model, fixed retrieval corpus, fixed prompt, and fixed scorer. That makes differences easier to attribute to training rather than to model family or infrastructure.19:T4f9,The experiments span four QA datasets. Three findings stand out.

First, post-rationalization is common even without RL. On Wikipedia-style datasets, roughly one citation in seven is unfaithful, around the 14% range. So the baseline instruction-tuned model already treats citation partly as a surface-matching task rather than strict evidence attribution.

Second, RLVR does not fix the problem. Under fixed-context inference, Search-R1 and ReSearch stay within a couple of percentage points of the base model, and R-Search is slightly worse. The authors also provide a detection bound: with their sample sizes, they would likely have detected an improvement of about seven percentage points, so a large hidden gain is unlikely.

Third, the control experiment matters. Even with no planting at all, the selected four-word span reappears in 60%–74% of regenerated answers, simply because models often reuse their own wording. This means evaluation protocols that condition on span recovery or report only raw planted rates can overstate the effect.

A useful detail is the distinction between claim-aligned scoring and cited-anywhere scoring. The latter is kept only as a sensitivity check and is shown to be looser.
