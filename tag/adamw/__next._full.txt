18:T5d4,The contribution is not a new optimizer but a tightly controlled reassessment. The setup has three important pieces. First, the authors choose problem families that can be systematically swept: symmetric PSD factorization, matrix completion, and NMF. These cover unconstrained low-rank recovery, missing-observation settings, and constrained factorization with nonnegativity and redundancy. Second, they run equal-budget, carefully tuned comparisons across Muon, Adam, AdamW, and GD, instead of inheriting community-default hyperparameters. This matters because Muon is sensitive to learning rate, momentum, weight decay, and the approximation details of the orthogonalization step. Third, they interpret results through the lens of what orthogonalization is actually doing. Muon approximately orthogonalizes matrix-shaped gradients, flattening singular-value anisotropy and moving closer to steepest descent under the spectral norm. One might expect that to help on spectral problems, but the paper shows that in classical matrix factorization this does not automatically beat coordinate-wise adaptivity; AdamW’s per-coordinate scaling is often more useful under ill-conditioning. Where Muon does help is NMF: orthogonalized updates appear to discourage factor collapse and reduce redundant directions, leading to more diverse factors. So the gain may come less from faster fitting of dominant singular directions and more from an implicit regularization effect on representation geometry.19:T418,The experimental contribution is about relative behavior across regimes rather than a single headline number. Four findings stand out. First, on standard low-rank matrix factorization, Muon’s advantage mostly vanishes after fair tuning, with AdamW and GD reaching similar or better convergence and final error. Second, on matrix completion, Muon again does not show a stable lead, which weakens the simple intuition that spectral normalization should automatically help on spectrally structured tasks. Third, on NMF, Muon shows a more consistent advantage, especially in overparameterized settings or when factor redundancy is more severe, yielding better reconstruction and less factor overlap. Fourth, ill-conditioning alone does not hand the win to Muon; in many cases AdamW’s adaptive scaling remains stronger. The abstract truncates before detailed numbers, but the full paper’s value is the systematic sweep over conditioning, parameterization, and task variants, with conclusions grounded in tuned comparisons rather than one-off runs.1a:T641,The iterate average optimization problem is formalized as a continuous-time optimal control problem: noisy gradient descent near a quadratic minimum is approximated by the standard Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, with the control objective of minimizing squared error of the returned iterate average subject to a quadratic penalty on control magnitude, forming a solvable linear-quadratic stochastic control problem. The optimal controller applies two corrective forces to the live iterate: pulling it toward the estimated optimum, and pulling it toward consistency with the accumulated trajectory average, to prevent the live weights from drifting too far from the average. Since the exact optimal controller requires unavailable quantities during training (true optimum location, loss Hessian), practical heuristics are applied: the EMA of iterates is used as a proxy for the optimum, reducing the control term to a simple pull of live weights toward their EMA. The resulting PACE algorithm requires only one extra copy of model weights, identical to standard EMA overhead, with no additional complex state. Formal analysis establishes two key results: (1) for general convex losses with unbiased gradients, PACE achieves the standard SGD convergence rate up to a constant factor dependent on EMA strength, meaning it is never substantially worse than SGD; (2) in the quadratic setting used for derivation, PACE strictly reduces the limiting mean squared error of the averaged estimator relative to uncontrolled SGD with EMA, with arbitrarily large improvements possible on specific quadratic instances.1b:T5ee,Start with the problem framing to understand the core misalignment between standard optimization targets and the deployed averaged model, the most important conceptual contribution of the work. Compare PACE to existing averaging approaches when reviewing the algorithm definition: standard EMA does not affect the training trajectory at all, Schedule-Free computes gradients at the averaged point, while PACE computes gradients at the live iterate and only adds a lightweight pull toward the EMA. First, starting from the decoupled AdamW update, the paper derives a leading-order evolution equation for the squared weight norm and decomposes it into three forces: an alignment force, an injection force, and a decay force. The alignment force measures correlation between the current weights and the adaptive update direction u_hat; if updates point partly along the weight vector, the norm grows. The injection force comes from the second-order contribution of the adaptive step magnitude itself. The decay force is the linear pullback induced by decoupled weight decay. Second, the paper builds a bridge from norm dynamics to the Weibull scale λ. This relies on the previously observed k-lock phenomenon: for a class of transformer components, the Weibull shape parameter k stays close to its initialization anchor during training. Under approximate k-lock, RMS and λ differ by an almost fixed multiplicative factor sqrt(Gamma(1+2/k)), so norm dynamics can be mapped to λ dynamics up to a measurable reconstruction offset. Third, for the realistic case where Adam moments are missing, the authors introduce a spline displacement method based on the AdamW displacement identity. Instead of using a crude two-checkpoint difference, they fit sparse checkpoint trajectories with splines to better recover local displacement directions and then estimate the alignment force. Relative to prior work, the novelty is not in another terminal norm law or another descriptive fit of weight distributions, but in explicitly tying optimizer-level force decomposition to a higher-level Weibull statistic.1d:T470,The main experiments use self-trained Pythia-70M models with ground-truth optimizer moments retained, which allows direct measurement of the three forces. The paper also reports an exploratory observation that the absolute peak of λ varies with training-data coherence, but the authors are careful not to overclaim because they do not run controlled data-mixture experiments.1e:T846,Gefen has two main components.

The first is shared second-moment estimation. The paper starts from a theoretical analysis on a two-layer MLP and studies when two parameters with a large mixed Hessian entry should also have similar squared-gradient magnitudes. The key result is that, under feasible Hessian bounds, increasing the magnitude of the mixed Hessian contracts the admissible ratio of squared gradients toward 1. This gives a clean design principle: parameters that are strongly coupled in curvature are natural candidates for sharing Adam’s second-moment estimate. Since explicit Hessian computation is intractable in large models, Gefen uses the structure of the first-step squared gradients as a proxy. For each parameter tensor, it flattens the tensor, enumerates proper divisors p of the tensor length n, reshapes g0^2 into consecutive blocks of size p, and computes an intra-block heterogeneity score E(p). Small E(p) means the squared gradients within each block are similar, so replacing element-wise v with one shared scalar per block should incur limited distortion. Larger p gives more aggressive sharing and lower memory, so the method selects p by balancing compression against heterogeneity. An important empirical observation is that E(p) is not necessarily monotonic in p; some larger divisors align better with real structural patterns such as attention heads or channel groups. That is why automatic search can outperform hand-written grouping rules.

The second component is first-moment compression. Instead of using a generic low-bit quantizer, Gefen reuses the same block partition and quantizes the first moment m with a learned codebook. The assignment is solved exactly with dynamic programming. Two design choices matter here. First, the codebook is learned rather than manually specified. Second, reusing the same partition avoids introducing another independent block-size hyperparameter. In effect, Gefen is a joint optimizer-state compression scheme: structured sharing for v and codebook-based quantization for m, both built around an automatically inferred block structure.1f:T6d7,The main empirical claims are threefold. First, memory: Gefen reduces optimizer-state memory by about 8× relative to AdamW, corresponding to roughly 6.5 GiB saved per 1B parameters. That is the headline systems result. Second, quality retention: across both LLM and non-LLM models, the method maintains performance comparable to AdamW, with some settings showing slight gains. The introduction explicitly states that Gefen achieves the lowest memory footprint among compared methods while keeping quality and throughput comparable or better. Third, the automatic grouping mechanism appears to capture real structure rather than arbitrary compression. Beyond the toy theory, the appendix shows similar behavior in real tensors, including attention and convolutional layers, where the discovered partitions produce more homogeneous squared gradients within blocks.

The most relevant baselines are AdamW, Adam-mini, and quantized optimizer variants.
