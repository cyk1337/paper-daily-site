Shading raises size-ranking accuracy from 4.0% to 11.0%, with smaller gains on angle ranking. Increasing model size up to 235B does not remove the gap.a:T745,The method has three main components. First, example selection. EVAN estimates scaled image information gain (scaled-IIG) for each training instance, measuring how much the image contributes to answer correctness. Only visually dependent examples are selected for intervention, which avoids injecting noise into cases that can already be solved from language alone. To prevent the selected pool from collapsing into a narrow subset of tasks, the paper uses a mixed-ranking strategy that balances visual dependence with data diversity.

Second, evidence ablation. For each selected example, the method identifies query-critical image patches that support the verified response. These patches are then masked to create an evidence-ablated image. The key design choice is that the perturbation is not coarse or task-agnostic; it removes exactly the visual evidence that keeps the answer grounded. Sampling from the current policy on this ablated image tends to produce responses that remain fluent and plausible, but are now visually ungrounded. These become EVAN’s hard negatives.

Third, RL optimization. The negatives are not optimized under the ablated image. Instead, they are brought back under the clean image condition, so the model is explicitly trained to suppress language-prior failures when the relevant evidence is actually available. The training recipe includes several stabilization mechanisms: filtering out low-probability or overly long ablated responses, bounding negative advantages so negatives do not dominate the update, and combining the method with DAPO-style clipping and dynamic sampling for stable long-form reasoning optimization. Compared with prior multimodal RL methods that use image perturbations, the main difference is that EVAN treats perturbation as a tool for constructing informative negatives, not as the end objective.b:T5fe,The experimental story is not just about end-task accuracy; it is also about grounding behavior. On a set of challenging multimodal reasoning benchmarks, EVAN improves over standard RLVR/GRPO baselines, with larger gains on subsets where visual evidence is more necessary. That pattern matches the method design: it is meant to correct language shortcuts, not to uniformly help every example.

The ablations are the most informative part. Removing scaled-IIG-based selection and applying evidence ablation to all samples hurts performance, showing that visually dependent example selection is necessary. Replacing query-critical masking with random masking or coarse perturbations produces weaker negatives and worse downstream gains, which supports the claim that negative quality matters more than perturbation quantity. The stability ablations are also important: without plausibility filtering or bounded negative advantages, training becomes less stable, indicating that the method is not simply “add negative rewards and hope for the best.”

The first is the Asymmetric Planner-Executor architecture. A diffusion LM acts as the planner and generates K reasoning trajectories in parallel for the same query. A larger autoregressive model acts as the executor, evaluates the candidates, selects the best plan, and synthesizes the final answer. The asymmetry is not just about model size. It is about role specialization: the DLM handles parallel exploration, while the ARM handles conditional understanding, candidate discrimination, and fluent final synthesis. This division is motivated by the fact that bidirectional denoising is naturally suited to globally refining a whole reasoning trace, whereas AR models remain stronger at coherent conditional generation once candidate plans are available.

The second component is RDC, Reward-Guided Denoising for CoT. The paper interprets each reverse diffusion step as a thought step, and the full denoising trajectory as a stochastic chain of thought. Standard diffusion training optimizes reconstruction or likelihood-related objectives; RDC augments this with a terminal reward that reflects whether the trajectory leads to a correct answer. In the paper’s framing, this is an integrated denoising-guidance objective: instead of reranking trajectories only after generation, the reverse transition operator p_theta(x_{t-1} | x_t, q) is trained to prefer intermediate states that are more likely to end in correct solutions. That is the key distinction from best-of-N or post hoc reranking. The reward is meant to reshape the planner’s generation distribution, not just filter its outputs.

From a modeling perspective, the important move is to treat diffusion trajectories explicitly as reasoning trajectories and to inject outcome supervision into the denoising dynamics. This differs from AR process reward models, which usually score already generated steps. Here the reward influences the denoising transitions themselves. The paper also includes a theoretical motivation: under logarithmic resource constraints, parallel refinement is argued to be strictly more expressive than strictly sequential generation. Two-stage training:
