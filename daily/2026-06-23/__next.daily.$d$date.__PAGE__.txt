4:T46f,WebGraphMix operates on the Common Crawl host-level hyperlink graph. Each node is a host, and each directed edge is a hyperlink between hosts. The graph used in the paper has 13.9M nodes and 439.6M edges. The method computes centrality scores on this graph, maps each document to the score of its host, and then constructs pretraining mixtures that emphasize central hosts, peripheral hosts, or a controlled combination of both. The main centrality measures are betweenness centrality and Katz centrality. Betweenness captures bridge-like nodes that lie on many shortest paths, while Katz centrality captures influence propagated through links to other influential nodes. The authors also test PageRank, but it does not improve results, matching a similar observation in DCLM. The experiments are integrated into the DataComp-LM pipeline, with tokenization, shuffling, and optimization held fixed to isolate the effect of data selection. The paper also combines graph centrality with the DCLM-fasttext quality classifier using multiplicative and divisive scoring, testing whether topology adds information beyond content-based quality.5:T63b,This is a unified evaluation and controlled-analysis paper. The authors evaluate eight modern DLMs, covering both pure diffusion and block-diffusion families, and compare them against strong autoregressive baselines under a shared protocol. The key contribution is not a catalog of model internals, but a decomposition of the main degrees of freedom that govern DLM behavior. First, they run a unified downstream evaluation across eight benchmarks spanning general knowledge, reasoning, coding, and machine translation, instead of letting each model be judged on a different task mix. Second, they treat inference budget as a first-class variable: denoising steps, sequence length, block size, and unmasking ratio are explicitly analyzed rather than hidden as implementation details. Third, they run controlled small-scale scaling experiments under matched training conditions to compare perplexity and scaling behavior with fewer confounds from data and compute differences. Fourth, they add a systems view by reporting peak memory and FLOPs not only for a single forward pass but for full generation. The most informative comparison is between pure diffusion and block diffusion. Pure diffusion offers stronger global parallel refinement in principle, but often requires many denoising rounds. Block diffusion tries to recover some of that flexibility while reducing total generation cost through local blockwise generation and conditioning. The paper’s contribution is to place these design choices into one experimental framework and study how they affect quality, efficiency, and scaling.6:T566,The experiments appear in three main groups. The first is the unified benchmark evaluation: eight DLMs are compared with autoregressive baselines on eight tasks covering knowledge, reasoning, coding, and translation. The authors emphasize comparable inference budgets rather than paper-specific best-case settings. The second group is hyperparameter and decoding ablations: they study how performance changes with more denoising steps, where diminishing returns begin, how sequence length and block size affect both quality and cost, and how parallel unmasking trades off throughput against output quality.
