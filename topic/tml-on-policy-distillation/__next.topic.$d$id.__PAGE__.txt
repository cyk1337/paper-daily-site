The two are not substitutes; they split search and compression. RL changes the data distribution through self-sampling, reward filtering, and repeated exploration on hard instances. OPD turns a terminal signal from one rollout into token-level teacher distributions or preference-like dense feedback. The risk comes from the same mechanism: if student rollouts leave the teacher support, token-level KL can harden errors; if the teacher sees too much privileged reference information, OPD may learn leakage paths; if reward extrapolation is used to exceed the teacher, structured outputs can hit a cliff. The more executable recipe is staged or interleaved: use RL to discover verifiable solution families, then use OPD, self-distillation, prefix distillation, or offline log-prob caching to compress length, cost, and variance. RLVR uses verifiable rewards to move a model onto new solving trajectories, especially in math, code, and long-CoT settings where sampling distribution changes. OPD reads teacher logits, preferences, or textual feedback on the student's own rollouts and turns one terminal reward into O(T) token-level training signals. This split explains both sides: OPD often helps small models, shorter responses, post-RL compaction, and multimodal grounding; it also fails outside teacher support, on near-deterministic structured outputs, under privileged teacher exposure, and when sparse exploration never finds positives. The classic PPO objective optimizes a clipped policy ratio, with the practical goal of moving the policy under reward pressure without taking an uncontrolled step [PPO2017]. DeepSeek-R1 [DeepSeekR12025], Kimi k1.5 [KimiK152025], OpenAI competitive programming work [OpenAICompetitive2025], and Skywork-OR1 [SkyworkOR12025] all use verifiable rewards in the search stage: sample long CoTs, filter verified successes, update the policy, and repeat. [CoT2022] provides early evidence that long reasoning trajectories can be expressed by language models. OPD enters from a different point. MiniLLM [MiniLLM2023] and Agarwal et al. [Agarwal2023SelfGenerated] ask the teacher to label prefixes generated by the student, including mistakes, which makes the training distribution closer to deployment. Hinton et al. [HintonKD2015] distillation extracts dark knowledge from teacher distributions; OPD places that teacher signal on student rollouts. Gudibande et al. [FalsePromise2023] rejects the claim that imitating strong-model outputs is enough to copy capability. The reason is exactly this distribution gap: offline answers cover trajectories visited by the teacher, not necessarily recovery actions from student error states.
