The narrow conclusion is this: context scaling is not about increasing the addressable window; it is about making distant evidence repeatedly necessary for loss reduction during training and actually used for composition, synthesis, and generation at evaluation time. Nominal 128K is a systems capability; effective 128K is a learned behavior, and the gap between the two is the data distribution. 9:T7bd,Camp A’s core claim is that long context is primarily a positional extrapolation problem: if RoPE or position bias is handled correctly, train-short test-long should work. Press et al. [Press2021ALiBi], Chen et al. [Chen2023PI], Zhu et al. [Zhu2023PoSE], Chen et al. [Chen2023LongLoRA], Ding et al. [Ding2024LongRoPE], Liu et al. [Liu2024E2LLM], Xiao et al. [Xiao2024InfLLM], and Gemini Team [Gemini2024] provide engineering evidence for this route: much longer position ranges are attainable, and the cost need not be prohibitive. The problem is that these results usually first show “can run stably at longer lengths,” then partly show “still works on some tasks,” but rarely give a clean same-base, same-token-budget comparison between PE-only and data recipe changes. Fu et al. [Fu2024DataEngineering] fill that gap: in 4K→128K continual pretraining, changing positions without changing data gives limited recovery on long tasks; adding long-document upsampling while preserving a domain mix close to base pretraining gives more direct gains. Xiong et al. [Xiong2023EffectiveLongCtx] likewise connect longer-sequence continual pretraining and long-document upsampling with long-task improvements on the same base model. He et al. [He2023NeverLost] further show that even Lost in the Middle behaves more like a training-objective and data-construction issue than a pure PE issue. Kazemnejad et al. [Kazemnejad2023PEImpact] define the boundary: PE mainly affects extrapolation stability and attention-pattern distortion; not all degradation should be attributed to positional frequency mismatch. The practical reading is that PE is a prerequisite, not the main battleground. The careful reading is that the main variable is not token count itself, but the construction distribution. Cross-span dependency density, topic coherence, information complementarity among same-source rewrites, retrieval-like dependencies, and the gap between synthetic and natural long-document distributions determine whether megadocs create training signal or just repeat one semantic neighborhood. Its relationship to `agent-context-scaling-hyperdoc` and `context-scaling-4d` should stay precise: synthetic megadocs are a pretrain data-axis intervention, not inference-time memory and not a harness taxonomy; they can serve as evidence for whether synthetic long-context data transfers to downstream long-horizon generalization. If evaluation stops at perplexity or short-range QA, the value will be overestimated; a stronger loop should include position-dependent, long-horizon, needle+synthesis, Summary of a Haystack, and NoCha-style stressors [Hsieh2024RULER][Karpinska2024NoCha][Laban2024SummaryHaystack].c:T8b0,Many training pipelines treat packing as a throughput optimization: reduce padding and fill sequences. That view is too narrow. More precisely, packing determines which learnable relations the model sees inside one forward pass. The key point in Shi et al. [Shi2023InContextPretraining] is not “pack more densely,” but retrieve related documents, cluster them, and concatenate them adjacently so that cross-document references, repeated entities, and local alignments become frequent within the same training sequence [Shi2023InContextPretraining]. Under the same compute budget, this improves ICL, long-context QA, and RAG-related tasks, which is hard to explain through throughput alone.

Mechanistically, there are three layers. First, distribution: Chan et al. [Chan2022DataDist] show that ICL depends on burstiness and skewed Zipfian structure rather than emerging automatically from parameter count; packing increases repetition and alignment density, pushing the training distribution toward ICL-favorable regimes. Second, circuits: Olsson et al. [Olsson2022InductionHeads] show that induction heads use repeated patterns in prior context to predict later tokens; if related-document packing makes patterns like “A:B appears earlier, then A:?” more common, it should more often train useful copy/alignment circuits. Third, representation: the RYS / Sapir-Whorf hidden-state results suggest that the model middle behaves more like a shared semantic space; packing can be understood as increasing how often distant evidence is projected into that space and jointly determines the next prediction with the current token [Ng2026SapirWhorf].

Staniszewski et al. [Staniszewski2023StructuredPacking] and Ding et al. [Ding2024FewerTrunc] point in the same direction from structured packing and low-truncation angles: with the same data pool, different sequence construction changes what the model actually learns. The caveat matters: direct causal evidence from packing to induction heads to effective context remains incomplete, and public work mostly shows mechanistic alignment rather than a closed proof [Bertsch2024ICLLongContext].
